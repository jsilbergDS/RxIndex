L7: I concur with the memoranda and reviews of the Division Director and Cross Disciplinary Team Leader for this application, and will approve this new drug application today. It is clear that angiotensin-II increases blood pressure to a significant extent in patients with septic or other distributive shock. I do, however, wish to add and/or emphasize a few points that were not highlighted in the reviews.
L8: The principal study (ATHOS-3, described below) demonstrates unequivocally that angiotensin-II increases mean arterial pressure (MAP) in patients with shock. Enrolled patients were described as having “catecholamine-resistant hypotension,” defined in the protocol as requiring a summed catecholamine dose of &gt; 0.2 µg/kg/min (norepinephrine equivalent dose) for 6 to 48 hours, to maintain a MAP between 55-70 mmHg. It is not clear, however, whether higher doses of catecholamines would have increased MAP to the 75 mmHg target; i.e., patients were not necessarily on maximum doses of the other vasopressors at the time of randomization. Thus, the effect of angiotensin-II when added to maximum doses of other vasopressors is unknown, and a variation of this text has been added to the package insert (Section 14).
Patients requiring &gt; 500 mg of hydrocortisone (or equivalent) daily, patients with a history of asthma or bronchospasm, and patients with Raynaud’s phenomenon were excluded from participation in the study. The review team was unable to find a rationale for these exclusions, based on anticipated actions of the drug. (Ideally, these exclusions would have been removed from the protocol prior to initiation of the study, unless supported by a reasonable rationale.) Having had these exclusions in place in the study protocol, however, we will communicate them in Section 14 of labeling.
The protocol required that patients have central venous access for at least the initial 48 hours of study, but was silent with respect to access after 48 hours. The drug was given via peripheral line in another study. There are no adverse events mentioning extravasation of drug (extravasation of catecholamines can cause local tissue damage). The Division has discussed that matter with the applicant, and the package insert will note that the drug was given predominantly via central intravenous line in ATHOS-3, (b) (4).

L10: Benefit-Risk Summary and Assessment
L11: As noted in Dr. Rose's summary, the demonstration of benefit is based on a single, multinational, randomized, placebo-controlled trial in 344 adults with distributive shock and persistent hypotension, defined as patients who had been adequately volume resuscitated and who required a total catecholamine dose &gt; 0.2 \mu\text{g/kg/min} for 6 to 48 hours to maintain a MAP of 55-70 mmHg. Patients were randomized 1:1 to receive angiotensin-II or placebo by the central intravenous route. The test drugs were to be titrated to achieve and maintain a MAP of \geq 75 \text{ mmHg}. Doses of vasopressors were to be held constant at their baseline infusion rates. The 1o endpoint assessed the proportions of "responders" at 3 hours. Responders were defined as patients who achieved a MAP of \geq 75 \text{ mmHg} or an increase of \geq 10 \text{ mmHg} from baseline. Response rates were 70% in the angiotensin-II group (114 of 163 subjects) and 23% in the placebo group (37 of 158 subjects), p &lt; 0.0001.
L12: As noted by Dr. Rose, the study has many of the characteristics of a single adequate and well-controlled study that could, by itself, provide evidence of effectiveness, as outlined in FDA's 1998 Guidance for Industry: "Providing Clinical Evidence of Effectiveness for Human Drugs and Biological Products." The effect size (47% absolute increase) is large and clinically meaningful on its face. The results are statistically persuasive, and robust to any type of exploration. Positive results were observed across sites in the U.S., Canada, and Australia, and across other important baseline demographic and disease subsets. The population studied seems generalizable to the US population at large.
L13: Although all recognize that MAP is not a measure of how patients feel, there is the belief that MAP is related to organ function, and ultimately to overall function and survival.
L14: The study demonstrated that 47% of patients (in absolute terms) obtained a (mean) 7 mmHg increase in MAP. For this \sim 7 \text{ mmHg} increase in MAP, the number needed to treat (NNT) is \sim 2. The single greatest safety concern is an increased risk of thrombotic events. If we assume that the absolute risk of thrombotic events is 4-8%, the number needed to harm is between 25 and 12. From the standpoint of thrombosis, therefore, the worst case is that for 12 patients treated, 1 will have a thrombotic event and 6 will increase their MAP by 7 mmHg.

Analysis of ConditionDistributive shock is due to sepsis in about 90% of cases, with the remainder from anaphylaxis or neurogenic causes. Typical manifestations are hypotension and hyperlactatemia. Cardiac output is typically increased, but myocardial depression is present in some patients. Recent publications report in-hospital or 28-day mortality rates of 42% to 50%.Distributive shock occurs in roughly 200,000 US adults each year. It is frequently fatal despite intensive monitoring and treatment. Thus, there is an urgent unmet need for effective therapies.

Current Treatment OptionsFor patients with shock with hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation, current treatment guidelines recommend use of vasopressors to raise MAP to \geq 65 mmHg, in order to increase perfusion of vital organs. Available therapies to raise MAP are those that leverage the sympathetic nervous system (catecholamines: norepinephrine, epinephrine, phenylephrine, ephedrine, dopamine, and metaraminol) and the arginine-vasopressin system (vasopressin). All but dopamine are indicated for raising blood pressure in patients with hypotension in various settings. (Dopamine is approved for correcting “hemodynamic imbalances” in patients with shock syndrome.) Of note, none of these drugs has been shown to improve clinical outcomes. (It would be difficult to support a study to show a mortality benefit, given that these agents are standard of care and they are perceived, though not proven, to decrease mortality.)Although several vasoconstrictors are approved for treating hypotension in patients with shock, none have been shown to improve clinical outcomes, and the mortality rate is high despite aggressive treatment. Additional treatments would be of value. None of the available treatments utilize the renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS).

BenefitSubstantial evidence of efficacy was provided by a single randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multinational study of angiotensin-II in patients with distributive shock and hypotension. Drs. Liu, Senatore, and McDowell have provided primary reviews of the study, with concurrence by their supervisors. The study enrolled patients with “catecholamine-resistant hypotension,” defined as patients who had been adequately volume resuscitated and required a total catecholamine dose &gt; 0.2 \mu\text{g/kg/min} for 6 to 48 hours to maintain a MAP of 55-70 mmHg. Patients were randomized 1:1 to receive angiotensin-II or placebo by the intravenous route. The test drugs were to be titrated to achieve and maintain a MAP of \geq 75The percentage of responders was 70% in the angiotensin-II group and 23% in the placebo group. Thus, the treatment effect was approximately 47%, or 1 in 2 patients. The number needed to treat (NNT) = 1/0.47 \approx 2. The actual benefit was a (mean) 7 mm Hg increase in MAP.

Risk
Drs. McDowell, Senatore, and Rose have considered the safety of angiotensin-II, and have identified a number of potential harms. The most notable risk is thrombotic/thromboembolic events, which will merit a warning in labeling. Depending on how they are defined, the absolute risk difference is 4.3% (for deep venous thrombosis) to 8% (for thrombotic/thromboembolic events). This risk is considered to be drug-related by the review team, because there is a mechanistic basis for pro-thrombosis.
Several adverse events occurred more frequently in the angiotensin-II group. These included:

• thrombocytopenia (10% vs. 7% as an adverse event; platelets decreased by \geq 50\% in 26% vs. 19%)
• fungal infection (6% vs. 1%)
• delirium (6% vs. 1%)
• acidosis (6% vs. 1%; lactate doubling, 10% vs. 6%)
• hyperglycemia (4% vs. 3%)
• peripheral ischemia (4% vs. 3%)


Given the number of adverse events of interest, it is difficult to determine which of these (if any) are likely to be drug effects vs. chance findings. The typical factors that support drug causality include a known (or suspected) mechanism of action, known risks of related drugs, dose-response, consistency between independent studies, and consistency between related adverse events. For these adverse events in this study, these factors are not operational or cannot be assessed.
Several adverse findings seem contrary to the expected effects of the drug. For example, given that the goal is to shunt perfusion towards vital organs, one would expect improved renal and central nervous system function; however, the

The Division takes reassurance from the positive trend in mortality, and I agree with their thinking. The best case scenario is that the drug is actually lifesaving, in which case, a larger trial might find a mortality benefit. The worst case scenario is that, despite the harms that the drug produces (i.e., thrombosis, thromboembolism, renal dysfunction, delirium, and acidosis), there tends to be a positive effect on mortality.



Risk Management


Labeling will include recommendations for monitoring of MAP and prophylaxis against thrombosis. No one believes there is a need for a REMS.
