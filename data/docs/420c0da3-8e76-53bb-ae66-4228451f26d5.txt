L45: ## 1 Executive Summary
L46: ## 1.1. Product Introduction
L47: Gallium Ga 68 PSMA-11 Injection (referred to as ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 in this review) is a peptide-based, positron-emitting radiopharmaceutical that binds to prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), a protein that is overexpressed in most prostate cancers. ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 is a new molecular entity. The proposed indications are for positron emission tomography (PET) of PSMA-positive lesions in men with prostate cancer in two defined clinical settings. The first setting is when metastasis is suspected in patients who are candidates for initial definitive therapy. The second setting is when cancer recurrence is suspected based on elevated serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level. ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 is intended to be administered intravenously at a dose of 3 to 7 mCi.
L48: New drug application (NDA) 212642 and NDA 212643 for ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 were submitted simultaneously under the 505(b)(2) pathway. As further described in Section 3.2, the two NDAs share all data except for separate manufacturing information. As such, the review of non-clinical, clinical pharmacology, clinical, and statistics information is identical for each NDA. A unique product quality review has been separately written for each NDA.
L49: ## 1.2. Conclusions on the Substantial Evidence of Effectiveness
L50: Substantial evidence was submitted that demonstrates the ability of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 to image prostate cancer in two populations: 1) men with suspected metastasis who are candidates for initial definitive therapy, and 2) men with suspected recurrence based on elevated serum PSA. Main support for efficacy was derived from two adequate and well-controlled trials which were conducted prospectively by the Applicant and were supported by reviews of published literature.
L51: The first adequate and well-controlled trial evaluated the imaging of prostate cancer-bearing pelvic lymph nodes in intermediate- to high-risk patients preparing for radical prostatectomy. Using histopathology as the reference standard in patient-level analysis, ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET had a sensitivity of 47% (95% confidence interval 29-65%), specificity of 90% (84-96%), and positive predictive value (PPV) of 61% (41-81%).
L52: The second adequate and well-controlled trial evaluated the imaging of metastatic disease in patients with biochemical recurrence of prostate cancer. ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 had a patient-level PPV of 83% (75-91%) using a histopathology reference standard.
L53: The performance of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET in these two trials demonstrates the clinical usefulness of this imaging test in the studied patient populations. Reviews of the published literature submitted by the Applicant for each of the populations of intended use support the
L54: generalizability of the results beyond the two sites of the trials conducted by the Applicant. In summary, the Applicant has provided substantial evidence of effectiveness of 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET.
L55: ## 1.3. Benefit-Risk Assessment
L56: ## Benefit-Risk Summary and Assessment
L57: In American men, prostate cancer is the most common malignancy and the second-most common cause of cancer death. While many imaging techniques have been applied to prostate cancer, none have optimal performance. The need for improved prostate cancer imaging is reflected in the recurrence rate of up to 30% after definitive therapy of what was thought to be localized disease. Similarly, many patients with biochemical recurrence fail to have their disease localized by imaging. Diagnostic challenges persist despite recent imaging advances, such as multiparametric MRI and PET drugs approved for prostate cancer, such as ^{18}F-fluciclovine.
L58: ^{68}Ga-PSMA-11 injection is a positron emitting radiopharmaceutical proposed by the Applicant. The data submitted in this application from two adequate and well-controlled trials prospectively conducted by the Applicant support approval for imaging evaluation of suspected prostate cancer metastasis in two patient populations, men who are candidates for initial definitive therapy and men with biochemical evidence (serum PSA level) of prostate cancer recurrence (hereafter referred to as biochemical recurrence).
L59: In patients with intermediate- to high-risk prostate cancer who are candidates for prostatectomy, the data submitted show low sensitivity but high specificity of ^{68}Ga-PSMA-11 PET for detection of pelvic lymph node metastases. Of note, there is reason to suspect that the diagnostic performance was underestimated due to trial design and conduct. The data also suggest that ^{68}Ga-PSMA-11 PET performs better in patients with higher grade prostate cancer in this setting. Regardless, it is anticipated that many patients with pelvic lymph node metastases will not be detected by ^{68}Ga-PSMA-11 PET. However, given similar limitations in available imaging techniques, such false negative results are expected to have no impact on the treatment plan of definitive therapy in affected patients. The potential value of ^{68}Ga-PSMA-11 PET lies in the demonstration that PPV exceeds the expected prevalence of lymph node metastasis in the population of intended use. While a positive PET scan may still need to be confirmed by other means, the results would allow a patient with metastasis to avoid the morbidity of surgery for more appropriate treatment. A positive PET scan might also help target therapy as part of the emerging paradigm of treatment of patients with limited metastases, also referred to as oligometastatic disease.
L60: In patients with biochemical recurrence, the submitted data clearly show favorable performance of ^{68}Ga-PSMA-11 PET. Although traditional

The pelvic lymph nodes are typical sites of initial metastasis of prostate cancer.
Proper management of prostate cancer involves assessment of the risk of aggressive disease as well as evaluation of the location and extent of disease.

Current Treatment Options	Many imaging techniques have been applied to prostate cancer, including ultrasound, CT, MRI, bone imaging using 99mTc-medronate or 18F-fluoride, 111In-capromab pendetide SPECT, and PET using 11C-choline or 18F-fluciclovine. The technique used varies depending on what information is needed.
For patients with newly diagnosed prostate cancer who are at intermediate or higher risk, treatment choice is influenced by whether cancer has spread to regional lymph nodes and more distant sites. Current standard-of-care relies on imaging with 99mTc-medronate bone scan along with CT or MRI of the abdomen and pelvis to detect such metastases. The diagnostic performance of these techniques is moderate. Recurrent prostate cancer after prostatectomy has been estimated to occur in up to 30% of men who had no evidence of metastatic disease on initial conventional imaging. While therapeutic methods may be a factor, this recurrence rate suggests that disease outside the prostate gland is missed by conventional imaging techniques in a number of cases.

Despite the availability of many different techniques for imaging prostate cancer, there is an unmet need for better diagnostic performance.

With the marketing discontinuation of 111In-capromab pendetide, no imaging drugs are currently approved specifically for imaging of prostate cancer prior to definitive therapy.

Benefit	Two adequate and well-controlled prospective trials were conducted by the Applicant and submitted in this NDA.
Trial PSMA-PreRP (pre-radical prostatectomy) enrolled 325 men with intermediate- to high-risk prostate cancer before therapy with radical prostatectomy to determine how effective ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 is at identifying prostate cancer in the pelvic lymph nodes. In the 123 patients who could be evaluated, the sensitivity was low, but the specificity was high. Positive predictive value (PPV) exceeded the estimated prevalence of lymph node metastasis in this patient population. Design and conduct of the trial suggest that diagnostic performance was underestimated. Subgroup analysis suggests that the test may perform better in patients with higher grade cancer.

L76: ## 2 Therapeutic Context
L77: ## 2.1. Analysis of Condition
L78: The American Cancer Society estimated that in 2019, prostate cancer would be the most commonly diagnosed cancer in American men, with 174,650 new cases predicted (Siegel et al. 2019). While prostate cancer-specific mortality has decreased over recent history, by 51% from 1993 to 2016, prostate cancer was predicted to be the second most common cause of cancer induced fatality in men for 2019, with 31,620 deaths predicted. Also, in recent years there has been a trend toward diagnosis of prostate cancer at higher stages, which is presumably due to changes in prostate cancer screening practices. These statistics demonstrate the need for continued advances in prostate cancer diagnosis and therapy.
L79: The large majority of prostate cancers are carcinomas of two broad types, glandular and neuroendocrine (Humphrey 2017). The most common prostate cancer overall is a glandular type, acinar adenocarcinoma, although another glandular type, intraductal carcinoma, may often coexist with it and suggests a more aggressive course (Humphrey 2017). While less common, the neuroendocrine carcinomas of the prostate are important to recognize as they are treated differently. One of these, small cell carcinoma, is notable for its propensity to arise after treatment of acinar adenocarcinoma, accounting for about of one of three patients with small cell carcinoma of the prostate.
L80: The natural history of prostate cancer is variable, ranging from indolent tumors that remain confined to the prostate for decades to highly aggressive tumors that rapidly metastasize and lead to death. Accordingly, it is necessary to predict the aggressiveness of the cancer at diagnosis to determine management and prevent undertreatment or overtreatment. Typically, a panel of risk factors is evaluated, but because there are many contributory factors, several methods have been devised. One of the earliest was the D'Amico risk classification, which includes the tumor stage by the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM criteria (T: primary tumor features, N: involvement of regional lymph nodes, and M: the presence or absence of distant metastases), tumor grade by Gleason score, and serum level of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and divides patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories (D'Amico et al. 1998). The widely used National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) classification (Mohler et al. 2019) includes additional information from the prostate biopsy, such as the number of positive cores and PSA density, to generate six risk categories. Most risk stratification schemes do not explicitly incorporate imaging, with the goal of minimizing unnecessary testing procedures and associated expense. Instead, imaging of patients with newly diagnosed prostate cancer is performed once a risk threshold has been passed.
L81: As with most other malignancies, prostate cancer can metastasize throughout the body and very often follows typical pathways of progression. Prostate cancer is most likely to spread initially to the pelvic lymph nodes, including the obturator, internal iliac, and external iliac
L82: chains. These sites are designated regional metastases. Once beyond the pelvic lymph nodes, the abdominal retroperitoneal lymph nodes and bones are the most common locations of metastasis. More advanced patterns of tumor progression can involve extrapelvic, non-nodal soft tissue sites like liver and lung.
L83: For prostate cancer localized to the prostate gland, if the risk of progression is considered high enough, curative intent therapy using radical prostatectomy or radiation therapy has good success rates (Mohler et al. 2019). After definitive therapy, the patient's PSA level should fall and become undetectable in the case of prostatectomy or reach a nadir in the case of radiotherapy. Monitoring for recurrence largely focuses on a combination of clinical signs and symptoms as well as serum PSA level. A rising PSA level after definitive therapy indicates a very high likelihood that prostate cancer is present and is termed biochemical recurrence (Roach et al. 2006; Cookson et al. 2007). Current treatment in this situation is typically non-curative in intent, but optimal management still depends on knowledge of disease location and extent. In addition, localized therapies such as radiotherapy or percutaneous ablative techniques are being tested on oligometastatic prostate cancer in initial and recurrent settings, and precise localization of disease could further facilitate such therapies.
L84: 68Ga-PSMA-11 allows PET imaging of prostate cancer through binding to the prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA). This molecule, also known as glutamate carboxypeptidase II or folate hydrolase I, is a transmembrane protein with an extracellular enzymatic domain. PSMA is expressed in normal prostate epithelial cells where its function is unknown (Silver et al. 1997; Bostwick et al. 1998). PSMA is also expressed in other normal tissues, particularly the glia of the central nervous system where it is involved in glutaminergic neurotransmission, the renal proximal tubules, in breast epithelium, and in the gut where it may be involved with folate uptake.
L85: In the majority of prostate adenocarcinomas, PSMA is overexpressed compared to benign prostate epithelium. For example PSMA was overexpressed in 33 of 35 tumor specimens in one case series (Silver et al. 1997). PSMA also appears to be more highly expressed in higher grade tumors (Bravaccini et al. 2018). However, neuroendocrine prostate cancer, particularly when it arises after treatment of prostate adenocarcinoma, may have low or absent PSMA expression. PSMA expression is also less common in bone metastases than in the primary prostate tumor (Silver et al. 1997).
L86: The term prostate-specific membrane antigen is somewhat misleading as there are many literature reports of PSMA overexpression in certain non-prostate malignancies and non-malignant conditions. For many malignancies, this effect may be mediated by expression of PSMA in the neovasculature rather than in malignant cells (Chang et al. 1999). This phenomenon has been reported in several cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer, melanoma, colon cancer, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, and thyroid carcinoma. In certain case series, PSMA expression results in visualization of non-prostate cancers on 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET (Salas Fragomeni et al. 2018). Similarly, benign tumors such as thyroid adenoma and
L87: hemangioma (soft tissue and bone) may be visualized by ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET. Normal osteoblasts express PSMA, and this observation may explain the increased uptake of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 in a variety of benign bone diseases such as Paget disease, fibrous dysplasia, osteoarthritis, and fracture (Hofman et al. 2018).

L230: ## Cell binding to PSMA and internalization of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11
L231: Competitive cell binding assays and internalization experiments were reported by Eder et al. (2014) in LNCaP cells (human prostate cancer cell line). To determine specific cell uptake and internalization, 10^5 cells per well were seeded in poly-L-lysine coated 24-well cell culture plates 24 hours before incubation. After washing, the cells were incubated with 25 nM of the radiolabeled compounds (either radiolabeled at room temperature (RT) or 95 °C) for 45 minutes at 37 °C and at 4°C, respectively. Specific cellular uptake was determined by competitive blocking with the PSMA inhibitor, 2-(phosphonomethyl)pentanedioic acid (500 µM final concentration, 2-PMPA). Cellular uptake was terminated by washing four times with 1 mL of ice-cold PBS. Cells were subsequently incubated twice with 0.5 mL glycine-HCl in PBS (50 mM, pH = 2.8) for 5 minutes to remove the surface-bound fraction. The cells were washed with 1 mL of ice-cold PBS and lysed using 0.5 mL 0.3 N NaOH. The radioactivity of the probes was measured in a gamma counter. The cell uptake was calculated as percent of the initially added radioactivity bound to 10^6 cells [%IA/10^6 cells].
L232: The results of internalization showed that for both preparations (RT and 95 °C), there was radioactivity bound to cell surface membranes and internalized within the cells (Panel A of Figure 4). As expected, there was more internalization at 37 °C incubation (approximately 20–35%) as compared to 4 °C incubation. More importantly, there was complete blockage of radioactivity when the cells were incubated in the presence of 500 µM of 2-PMPA (a competitive blocking agent for PSMA).
L233: Panel B of Figure 4 shows determination of binding affinity of nonradioactive Ga-PSMA-11 (natGa-PSMA-11) to LNCaP cells as a function of the labelling temperature. The cells were incubated with the radioligand (^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11) in the presence of different concentrations of nonradioactive Ga-PSMA-11 (0–5000 nM, 100 µL/well). The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) was found to be 27\pm 1.3 nM.

L268: ## 6.3.2. Clinical Pharmacology Questions
L269: ## Are there clinically relevant food-drug or drug-drug interactions, and what is the appropriate management strategy?
L270: Patients with prostate cancer can be divided into hormone-sensitive and castrate-resistant groups. Certain patients with prostate cancer receive ADT or other treatments that target the androgen pathway, such as androgen receptor antagonists and androgen synthesis inhibitors. It is suspected that the concomitant use of these therapies could impact the efficacy of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11, depending on hormone-sensitive or castrate-resistant prostate cancer status.
L271: A publication evaluated the influence of long-term use of ADT on PSMA PET/CT findings (Afshar-Oromieh et al. 2018). The authors performed a retrospective analysis of all 1,704 patients who underwent a ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET/CT scan at their institution from 2011 to 2017 to detect prostate cancer. Of 306 patients scanned at least twice, 10 had started and continued ADT with a clinical response between the two ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 scans. In these 10 patients the authors compared the tracer uptake intensity and volume of prostate cancer lesions on ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET/CT before and during ongoing ADT.
L272: Overall, 31 prostate cancer lesions were visible in all 10 patients before initiation of ADT. However, during ongoing ADT (duration 42–369 days, median 230 days), only 14 lesions were visible in 8 of the 10 patients. The average tracer uptake values decreased in 71% and increased in 13% of the prostate cancer lesions. Of all lesions, 33% were still visible in six patients with a complete PSA response (\le 0.1 \text{ ng/ml}). The authors concluded that continuous long-term ADT reduces the visibility of hormone-sensitive prostate cancer on ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET/CT and recommended PSMA PET/CT scanning before starting ADT.
L273: Another study evaluated the effect of various treatments targeting the androgen pathway on ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 imaging in men with hormone-naive (starting therapy with luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone [LHRH] \pm bicalutamide) and castrate-resistant (starting therapy with enzalutamide or abiraterone) metastatic prostate cancer (Emmett et al. 2019).
L274: Serial ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET was performed at baseline and on days 9, 18, and 28 in eight men with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer commencing LHRH \pm bicalutamide (cohort 1) and seven men with metastatic castrate-resistant prostate cancer commencing either enzalutamide or abiraterone (cohort 2). Gleason score, age, time since diagnosis, and prior treatments were documented. Testosterone and PSA were measured at baseline and all imaging time points. ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET/CT was quantitatively analyzed for SUVmax, SUVmean, and total tumor volume.
L275: In cohort 1, a reduction in SUVmax was recorded by day 9 after treatment. A reduction from baseline SUVmax occurred in 86% (6/7) of patients by day 9, with an associated PSA response in all patients. Total tumor volume decreased in all patients (interquartile range= 27 to 97%). After
L276: day 9, PSMA response heterogeneity was noted, with persistently high or increasing SUVmax in 38% (3/8) and marked reduction in 62% (5/8).
L277: In cohort 2, an increase in intensity of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 SUV was recorded by day 9 after treatment. All patients demonstrated an increase in SUVmax and SUVmean on PSMA PET compared with baseline. PSA responses were more delayed in cohort 2, with two of seven men demonstrating PSA progression.
L278: The authors concluded that ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 imaging is affected by the tested treatments targeting the androgen pathway in a manner that is influenced by the presence of hormone-sensitive or castrate-resistant prostate cancer status. These results may have important implications for interpretation of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET.
L279: ## Applicant's perspective on impact of initiation and duration of ADT on ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET
L280: There is controversy regarding the impact of androgen receptor inhibition, such as ADT, on PSMA expression soon after initiation. ADT can result in an increase in expression of PSMA on tumor cells in animal models and in humans, potentially increasing the sensitivity of PSMA PET. However, heterogeneous effects of ADT have been observed clinically with increases in PET signal from baseline in some patients and decreases in others. This variability likely relates to the competing effects on cell death, which decreases uptake, and PSMA upregulation, which increases uptake.
L281: In the Afshar-Oromieh et al. (2018) study on the impact of long-term ADT, the second PET study was performed during the time of therapeutic effect of ADT. In that study, the average PSA at time of the pre-ADT imaging study was 37 ng/dL versus 1.8 ng/dL at the time of the imaging study on chronic ADT, and two patients had a negative PSA at the time of post-ADT imaging. The decrease in SUV and number of lesions on the second PSMA PET on ADT was related to decreased tumor volume due to efficacy of treatment rather than decreased sensitivity of the imaging study.
L282: Based on these findings, the Applicant believes that ADT should not be modified prior to PSMA PET imaging.
L283: ## FDA conclusion on potential drug-drug interactions
L284: The potential effect of treatments targeting the androgen pathway is cited in the prescribing information (Drug Interactions, Section 7) to inform the prescriber about the possible impact (either reduction or increase in SUV) of these therapies on ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET. The efficacy of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET in patients receiving these therapies requires further study.

L292: ## 7.2. Review Strategy
L293: Primary evidence of effectiveness and safety for 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET for imaging of prostate cancer was provided in two prospective trials, PSMA-PreRP and PSMA-BCR. These trials were conducted in two distinct populations, patients with new diagnoses of prostate cancer who were candidates for initial definitive therapy and patients with biochemical recurrence of disease. The Applicants for NDA 212642 and NDA 212643 collaborated to perform these studies and the Module 5 data presented in each application were identical.
L294: Unless otherwise specified, all analyses of these trials reported in Section 8 of the review were performed by the clinical and statistical team using data submitted by the Applicant. Any substantial differences in results from the Applicant's analyses are discussed in the relevant section. Analyses performed by the clinical reviewer were derived from the updated fulldata.xpt (PSMA-PreRP) or fac.xpt (PSMA-BCR) datasets received as an amendment to the
L295: applications on January 24, 2020, along with the fab.xpt and fap.xpt (PSMA-BCR) datasets submitted with the initial applications, using SAS Enterprise Guide version 8.1. The 95% confidence intervals for proportions used the method of Wald without continuity correction, unless the sample size was small (defined as np &lt; 5 or n(1-p) &lt; 5), in which case Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals were reported. Evaluations for difference used t-test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for proportions. All tests were two-sided.
L296: Because ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 has been widely used experimentally for several years, the Applicant included two meta-analyses from the scientific literature in similar populations as those of the prospective trials, PSMA-PreRP-Meta and PSMA-BCR-Meta, as evidence of effectiveness. The statistical and clinical teams have reviewed these sources, but due to the retrospective nature of most of the literature, the potential for bias was assessed as high. As such, the meta-analyses were considered as secondary supportive evidence and are briefly reviewed in Section 8.
L297: After the NDA was submitted, the results of a prospective study of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 in patients with prostate carcinoma who were candidates for initial definitive therapy (van Kalmthout et al. 2019) were published. The study design and patient population were very similar to the PSMA-PreRP trial, and the results directly addressed the effectiveness of the radiotracer in that population. However, because of the lack of access to the source data, this study was also considered supportive evidence and is briefly reviewed in Section 8.

L301: ## Trial Design
L302: PSMA-PreRP was a prospective, single-arm, open-label study of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET for imaging of PSMA expressing prostate carcinoma performed at two sites, UCLA and UCSF. It enrolled adult males with biopsy-proven intermediate or high risk prostate carcinoma. Patients had not yet received definitive therapy (including prostatectomy, radiation therapy, or focal gland therapy), and were planned to undergo prostatectomy with pelvic lymph node dissection. Patients were required to have a Karnofsky performance status of at least 50. Patients with lower performance status could certainly tolerate the PET scan, but they would be less likely to tolerate the prostatectomy.
L303: After enrollment, each patient received 3 to 5 mCi (UCLA) or 3 to 7 mCi (UCSF) of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 intravenously followed 45 to 60 min (UCLA) or 50 to 100 min (UCSF) later by PET/CT or PET/MR from mid-thigh to skull base. Unless advanced scatter correction algorithms were available on
L304: ## NDA/BLA Multi-Disciplinary Review and Evaluation NDA 212642
## Gallium Ga 68 PSMA-11 Injection
L305: the scanner, patients received 20 mg of furosemide intravenously with the radiotracer to reduce scatter and scatter correction artifact related to urinary excretion of radiotracer. As discussed in Section 6 of this review, the selection of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 dose was supported by reports of successful imaging at similar doses that were published prior to study initiation.
L306: Three to five readers from a centralized pool of six nuclear medicine physicians and radiologists independently interpreted each ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET scan. These central readers also had access to the anatomic images (CT or MR) obtained with the PET scan for attenuation correction. The readers were blinded to all other information except the history of pre-prostatectomy status. Training was required for the readers prior to participation in the study. Each reader rated multiple predefined regions as positive or negative based on the presence or absence of focal radiotracer uptake greater than physiologic background activity. A single result for each scan was generated by assigning the majority read result in each region, as further described in the Study Endpoints section below. No contract research organization was utilized.
L307: A local read of each PET scan was performed by a single unblinded site reader promptly after the scan, and the result was made available to the physicians caring for the patient, including the urologist performing the prostatectomy. If the local read demonstrated PET positive pelvic lymph nodes, the prostatectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection was to proceed as planned. If the local read demonstrated PET positive bone or extrapelvic soft tissue lesions, correlative imaging or biopsy was to be performed and the treating urologist would determine whether prostatectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection should be performed.
L308: Urologists performed the pelvic lymph node dissections using standard of care template technique guided by surgical anatomy rather than imaging. The reference standard for the PET result was the pathology report from this template pelvic lymph node dissection. The identity and number of separate lymph node groups sent to pathology was to be determined by the usual practice of the urologist performing the surgery. If nodes were read as positive on PET but none were found by histopathology, a pelvic CT or MRI would be performed to determine whether the PET positive nodes were removed.

L314: ## Study Efficacy Endpoints
L315: The primary endpoints were “sensitivity and specificity of 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET for the detection of regional nodal metastases compared to pathology at radical prostatectomy on a per patient basis.” These endpoints were discussed with FDA in a meeting and follow up communications spanning August 10, 2016, to January 9, 2017, and agreement was reached. FDA advised that the same two of three readers should meet the predefined sensitivity and specificity goals; this advice was incorporated into the protocol, but ultimately was not implemented in a manner that allowed optimal per-reader analyses.
L316: Current standard of care therapy for prostate cancer localized to the prostate gland most often includes observation, radiotherapy, or prostatectomy, depending in part on the underlying extent of disease. If disease has spread beyond the prostate gland, prostatectomy has traditionally been considered futile, and the substantial morbidity associated with prostatectomy is generally avoided in this situation. However, the potential option of directed therapy of oligometastatic disease is also currently being explored clinically. In either case, management decisions often rely on accurate detection and localization of extraprostatic lesions. Unfortunately, current imaging techniques have limited sensitivity and specificity for detection of pelvic nodal metastases and distant metastatic disease. Thus, there is an unmet need for agents that can better detect extraprostatic disease.
L317: ## NDA/BLA Multi-Disciplinary Review and Evaluation NDA 212642
## Gallium Ga 68 PSMA-11 Injection
L318: Because prostate cancer most often spreads to the pelvic lymph nodes before becoming widely metastatic, and because pelvic nodal dissection is frequently performed along with prostatectomy for staging and prognostic purposes, assessing performance of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET in detection of pelvic lymph node metastases as an endpoint is both clinically relevant and practical. However, it does not directly assess the ability of the PET to detect all extraprostatic disease, and it is not certain that the PET will have equal performance for pelvic lymph nodes and distant metastatic disease.
L319: During PET reads, pelvic lymph nodes were assigned to one of nine regions: right common iliac, right external iliac, right internal iliac, right obturator, left common iliac, left external iliac, left internal iliac, and other. Any PET positive node in a region led to a PET positive classification for that respective region. The number of PET positive nodes was not recorded. Note that it is not possible to precisely colocalize PET positive lymph nodes with histopathologically positive lymph nodes due to current surgical methods that use regional anatomy to guide template dissection. Instead, the pelvic regions were condensed into 3 groups (right, left, and other) for both PET read results and the reference standard surgical histopathology results. Any PET positive region contained within a group led to a PET positive classification for that group. Similarly, any histopathology positive lymph nodes within a group led to reference standard positive classification for that group. The node groups were then determined to be true positive, true negative, false positive, or false negative by comparing the PET and histopathology classifications. Patients were then categorized using the rule that the first matching node group from the following ordered list defined the patient status: TP, FP, FN, TN. For example, if a patient had groups that were FP, FP, and TP, the patient would be TP, while if they had groups that were TN, TN, and FN, they would be FN.
L320: An important secondary endpoint was “positive and negative predictive value of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET for the detection of regional nodal metastases compared to pathology at radical prostatectomy on a per patient basis.” While the study population must closely match the intended population of clinical use for the result to be applicable, if this is fulfilled, then the PPV will accurately reflect the likelihood that a PET positive patient has pelvic lymph node metastasis. Identification of patients with pelvic lymph node metastases without an unduly large number of false positive results is anticipated to be one important use of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET in this patient population. The data for the PPV and negative predictive value (NPV) endpoints were derived in the same manner as for the primary endpoints.

L367: ## Protocol Deviations
L368: Several protocol deviations were reported by the Applicant. Except for the delay in the post-scan follow up call as described in 1 below, the impact of these deviations on the study results was likely minimal.
L369: - Post-scan follow up call: All patients were to receive a follow up telephone call from 1 to 3 days after the PET scan to screen for adverse events. The call was made more than 3 days after the scan for 113 patients (35%), all enrolled at UCLA. As described in later sections, similar late follow up also occurred for patients at UCLA participating in PSMA-BCR. The late follow up likely explains the lower adverse event rate at UCLA compared to UCSF (0.8% versus 4.2%, respectively).
- Radiotracer dose: A total of 16 patients (5%), all from the USCF site, received 68Ga-PSMA-11 doses outside the prescribed range of 3-7 mCi. Fourteen patients received doses &gt;7 mCi, up to 7.5 mCi. Two patients received doses &lt;3 mCi, as low as 2.84 mCi.
- PSA: Per protocol, PSA was to be measured within 30 days prior to the PET scan. PSA was measured outside of this time window in 244 patients (75%). The Applicant notes that the PSA value at prostate cancer diagnosis, rather than any subsequent PSA value, is used for risk stratification, therefore subsequent measurements are of limited utility.
- Performance status: Performance status at enrollment was to have been documented by a physician for all patients. This documentation was instead performed by a clinical research coordinator for 43 patients in the Full Analysis Set (34%), all enrolled at UCSF. It was not reported whether similar deviations occurred for patients who did not receive prostatectomy.
- Vital signs: Patients were to have heart rate and blood pressure recorded before and after the PET scan. Three patients (1%) did not have complete recording. Two patients lacked post-scan vital signs, while one lacked vital signs both before and after PET.
- Informed consent: Two patients from UCLA lacked signed informed consent documents. The Applicant described this violation as an administrative error in the informed consent process that was otherwise routinely performed.

L388: As shown in Table 11, there was a trend toward higher PSA levels in the no prostatectomy group that was excluded from the primary endpoint analysis, however the clinical significance of this trend is questionable due to the small magnitude of the difference. L390: ## Treatment Compliance, Concomitant Medications, and Rescue Medication Use
L391: Study drug was administered at the clinical sites; therefore, drug compliance is not applicable. Information about prior and concomitant medications was not collected other than ADT exposure.
L392: All enrolled patients successfully completed a 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET scan in this single arm study. However only 127 of 325 patients (39%) proceeded to prostatectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection, which was needed for reference standard determination. It was anticipated in the protocol that the surgery rate would be about 75%; this value is less than 100% due to the expectation that there would be identification of new findings on the PET scan as well as miscellaneous other factors. The protocol specified that if PET positive pelvic lymph nodes were found, the treating urologist would be notified and the patient would still proceed to surgery. If PET positivity for distant metastatic disease was found, the lesions would be further evaluated using other imaging techniques or by biopsy before deciding whether prostatectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection were appropriate.

L498: ## 8.1.3. PSMA-BCR
L499: ## Trial Design
L500: PSMA-BCR was a prospective, single-arm, open-label study of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET for localization of biochemically recurrent prostate cancer. The study was performed at two sites in the United States, UCLA and UCSF. It enrolled men who developed biochemically recurrent prostate cancer after definitive therapy with prostatectomy or radiotherapy. Patients who had received focal gland therapies such as cryoablation were not included. The definition of biochemical recurrence was serum PSA of &gt;0.2 \text{ ng/mL} more than 6 weeks after prostatectomy or serum PSA &gt; 2 \text{ ng/mL} over nadir after radiotherapy alone. The recurrence criterion for patients following prostatectomy is a modification of the American Urological Association definition to eliminate the requirement for a repeat PSA measurement (Cookson et al. 2007). The recurrence criterion

L502: for patients following radiotherapy is from the widely used American Society for Radiation Oncology-Phoenix definition (Roach et al. 2006). Patients were required to have a Karnofsky performance status of at least 50. Patients with lower performance status could certainly tolerate the PET scan, but it could be argued that these patients are not in the intended population of use as they may not be able to tolerate therapy.

L526: ## Study Efficacy Endpoints
L527: The protocol-defined primary endpoints varied by protocol version and by study site. At UCLA, the primary endpoints were sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV in the initial version of the protocol. In versions 2 through 4, under which most patients were enrolled, the primary endpoint was PPV on a per-patient and per-region basis using a histopathology reference standard. At UCSF, version 1 of the protocol defined the primary endpoint as sensitivity and PPV on a per-patient and per-region basis using a histopathology reference standard. Version 2 changed this to sensitivity on a per-patient and per-region basis using the composite reference standard defined in the previous section. Note however, the study execution did not allow accurate estimation of the false negative rate, and in the PSMA-BCR CSR the primary endpoint was defined as patient-level and region-level PPV using a histopathology reference standard. Patient-level sensitivity against a composite reference standard was changed to a secondary endpoint by the Applicant and was not considered a relevant endpoint for this review.
L528: Important secondary endpoints described in various protocol versions are patient-level and region-level PPV using the composite reference standard, PET positivity rate stratified by PSA level, and inter-reader reproducibility. The PET positivity rate, also commonly referred to as the detection rate, was not validated by a reference standard. Therefore, the correct detection rate, defined as the number of true positive patients divided by the number of patients with sufficient data to classify their result, was also calculated by the review team and will be discussed with the other results of the trial.
L529: While specificity would have been a useful endpoint, the study design does not allow accurate estimation of this parameter. Alternate study designs that would allow specificity to be
L530: measured for this population are often not practical, and the Applicant and FDA agreed that specificity would not be assessed in a meeting held on August 10, 2016.
L531: Patients with rising PSA after definitive therapy of prostate cancer, referred to commonly as biochemical recurrence, are typically assumed to have residual or recurrent disease. The choice of therapy for these patients depends on the location and sometimes the extent of disease. However, currently available imaging techniques including other PET agents approved for prostate cancer imaging, such as ^{18}F-fluciclovine and ^{11}C-choline, have relatively poor detection rates and high false positive rates for prostate cancer, particularly at initial recognition of biochemical recurrence when PSA levels are low. Thus, PPV and detection rate are relevant endpoints.
L532: To compare the PET result to the reference standard, the Applicant first identified PET positive regions with available reference standard information. If the reference standard was collected from at least one PET positive subregion, the region was classified as true positive when the reference standard determination was “cancer confirmed” or as false positive when the reference standard determination was “cancer ruled out.” If none of the subregions with reference standard information was PET positive, the Applicant considered the region to be a subregion mismatch and indeterminate rather than attempting to assign true negative or false negative classification. Because of the use of PPV as the primary endpoint and the inability to consistently sample PET negative lesions, this approach is reasonable. For the patient-level analyses, if a patient had more than one region classified, the patient was categorized as true positive if there was at least one true positive region and categorized as false positive if there were only false positive regions. Of note, most patients had only one region classified, so region-level results closely matched patient-level results.

L573: ## Protocol Deviations
L574: The eight reported protocol deviations are summarized below. Deviations 1 to 4 might have influenced study results as described for each item. The remaining deviations were considered unlikely to substantially influence the study results.
L575: - Lack of definitive therapy: Seven patients, six at UCSF and one at UCLA, were enrolled in the study without prior prostatectomy or definitive radiotherapy. Five of these patients had non-histopathology composite reference data, all classified as TP. Two patients had histopathology data, one TP and one FN. It is unclear whether the UCSF patients were included in the Applicant's analysis; the UCLA patient, who was TP by the composite reference standard, was included. These patients were excluded from the FDA primary and secondary analyses below.
- PSA level not meeting definition of biochemical recurrence: A total of 11 patients (9 at UCSF, 2 at UCLA) with a history of radiation therapy were enrolled without evidence of biochemical recurrence, either because they did not have PSA more than 2 ng/mL higher than their nadir or because they did not have documentation of their nadir PSA. Six of these patients had composite reference data (three TP, two FP, one FN) and four had histopathology data (one TP, two FP, one FN). The Applicant included these patients in the analyses, stating that they represent a more challenging population for the test. The clinical reviewer concurred and also included them in analyses.
- Missing PSA level within 30 days: Per protocol, all patients were to have a PSA level obtained within 30 days of the PET scan. For 313 patients (49% of enrolled patients; 187 at UCSF and 126 at UCLA), the PSA level was obtained outside of this window. For patients with this protocol deviation, the median time between PSA level and PET scan was 57 days, the range was 31 to 367 days, and the interquartile range was 43 to 82 days. The Applicant stated this deviation would not affect the PPV or sensitivity endpoints and included the patients in all analyses. However, detection rate stratified by PSA level is an important measure of test performance in the biochemical recurrence setting because PSA increases with disease volume and tumor tends to be easier to detect in patients with higher volume disease. Since the PSA level was obtained before the PET scan, a longer delay could improve apparent test performance by this metric. However, the effect that such delay had in these cases was likely not extreme since a PSA doubling time of 60 days is associated with only very aggressive disease and more typical doubling times are on the order of 300 days. As such, all patients were included in analyses that stratified by PSA level, regardless of the timing of collection relative to the PET scan.
- Post-scan follow up call: All patients were to receive a follow up telephone call between 1 to 3 days after the PET scan to screen for adverse events. The call was made more than 3 days after the scan for 249 patients (39%), all enrolled at UCLA. As described elsewhere, this delay also occurred for patients at UCLA participating in PSMA-PreRP.
L576: The late follow up likely explains the lower adverse event rate at UCLA compared to UCSF (0.8% versus 4.2%).
L577: - Radiotracer dose: A total of 22 patients (3%; 21 at UCSF and one at UCLA) received radiotracer doses outside the 3 to 7 mCi range. Among these patients, 18 received more than 7 mCi, with the highest administered dose of 7.6 mCi. Four patients received less than 3 mCi, with the lowest administered dose of 2.5 mCi. Note that UCLA used a more restrictive 3 to 5 mCi dose range and thus there might be additional unreported protocol deviations related to dose. However, UCLA doses from 5 to 7 mCi would fall within allowed dosing at UCSF.
- Vital signs: Patients were to have heart rate and blood pressure recorded before and after the PET scan. Six patients (1%; all from UCLA) did not have complete recording.
- Performance status: Performance status at enrollment was to have been documented by a physician for all patients. A total of 160 patients (25%) had performance status documented by a non-physician. An additional 161 patients (25%) had performance status documented by a physician but outside the predefined screening window. All of these patients were enrolled at UCSF.
- Sulfa allergy: Sulfa allergy was an exclusion criterion because of the theoretical risk of cross reactivity with the furosemide administered to most patients with the ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 dose. A total of 14 patients with sulfa allergy were enrolled at UCSF and 13 of them received furosemide. No allergic reactions were reported.

L586: ## Other Baseline Characteristics
L587: As shown in Table 31 above, patients with reference standard data were more likely to have had radiotherapy and less likely to have had prostatectomy than patients without reference standard data. It is possible that ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET performs differently in patients with radiotherapy versus prostatectomy. For example, there will usually be more residual, viable normal prostate tissue after radiotherapy than after prostatectomy, which could affect interpretation of the PET scan. However, the rates of each therapy in PSMA-BCR patients appeared reasonably representative of the expected population of use.
L588: The PSA level trended higher for patients who had reference standard data than those without, and the size of this difference appeared clinically meaningful in the setting of biochemical recurrent prostate cancer, in which numerous imaging modalities have demonstrated a strong dependence of detection rate on PSA level. For example, in one study ^{18}\text{F}-fluciclovine PET had a positivity rate of 36% when PSA was 1 ng/mL or less, but 66% for levels between 1 and 2 ng/mL (Andriole et al. 2019). As the definition of biochemical recurrence required a higher PSA level for patients with definitive radiotherapy than those with prostatectomy (nadir+2 ng/mL versus 0.2 ng/mL), it is possible that the difference in prostatectomy rate drove the difference in PSA level. Additionally, negative PET scans, which are expected to occur more often with lower PSA,

L591: Patients in the reference standard group were more likely to have conventional imaging available at enrollment, and this imaging was more likely to be positive. This trend persisted when analysis was limited to patients with a positive ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET scan (n=238 with reference standard data versus n=231 without, conventional imaging available in 52% versus 42%, conventional imaging positive in 21% versus 11%). Because conventional imaging is considered relatively insensitive for localizing biochemically recurrent prostate cancer, these trends, like the trend in PSA level, raise the possibility that patients with easier to diagnose disease were selected for analysis, as compared to the general biochemical recurrence population. FDA subgroup analyses below will address this concern.

L595: treating physician was also aware of the ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET scan result via the local read, though the central read was not made available. L746: ## 8.1.7. Integrated Assessment of Effectiveness
L747: The Applicant's data support the effectiveness of 68Ga-PSMA-11 for imaging of prostate cancer. However, there are important gaps in the resulting evidence that must be considered when evaluating whether standards for effectiveness have been met. These gaps can be divided broadly into issues of test performance and issues of experimental design and execution, discussed in turn below.
L748: ## Test performance:
L749: The PSMA-PreRP study failed to meet its primary endpoint goal for the lower bound of the 95% CI of sensitivity to be greater than 65%, with an observed point estimate of 47% and a 95% confidence interval of 29-65%. However, it is important to note that the sensitivity goal was chosen based on the performance of the agent in preliminary retrospective analyses, rather than on a clinically meaningful threshold. Therefore, failure to meet the sensitivity goal does not necessarily indicate lack of potential clinical utility. Because definitive therapies for prostate
L750: cancer such as prostatectomy and radiotherapy are associated with significant morbidity, and pelvic nodal metastases may spare patients from receiving them, identification of pelvic nodal disease on PET could provide benefit by avoiding futile therapy. While low sensitivity will decrease the number of patients who benefit, each individual patient with true positive results will potentially benefit. Conversely, patients with pelvic nodal metastasis that is missed by the PET scan will likely proceed to definitive therapy just as they would have with no PET scan at all.
L751: Given this clinical context, the most relevant statistic is PPV, which ideally would be high enough to avoid additional confirmatory testing of positive results. In PSMA-PreRP, PPV was initially estimated at 61% with a 95% confidence interval of 41-81%. A conservative modification of the Applicant's post hoc analysis estimated PPV at 74% with a 95% confidence interval of 54-93% (Table 22). As discussed further below, trial execution issues likely underestimated performance in PSMA-PreRP. Also, since PPV is dependent upon prevalence of metastasis in the intended patient population, performance would be expected to improve in patients with suspected metastasis (i.e., those of higher risk stratification). This point will be reflected in the labeled indications statement. The fraction of patients with positive pelvic lymph nodes in PSMA-PreRP, 24%, was within the typical range reported in the published literature, though estimates vary substantially based on numerous risk factors as well as the extent of pelvic lymph node dissection (Liss et al. 2013; Heck et al. 2014; Batra et al. 2015).
L752: It is also noted that current treatment algorithms for prostate cancer prior to definitive therapy incorporate conventional imaging techniques with low sensitivity. For example, multiparametric MRI has sensitivity of up to 55 to 73% (Caglic et al. 2019) for metastasis in this setting. As such, clinicians are familiar with the limitations of low sensitivity imaging techniques. Along these lines, Section 5: Warnings and Precautions of the labeling will include information on the risk of misdiagnosis. Specifically, this section will state that a negative image does not rule out the presence of prostate cancer and a positive image does not confirm the presence of prostate cancer.
L753: Subgroup analyses in the PSMA-PreRP study showed a trend for higher sensitivity and PPV in patients with summed Gleason score of 8 or higher. This observation may be explained in part by greater expression of PSMA in higher grade prostate cancers (Bravaccini et al. 2018), resulting in greater binding of radiotracer. While the sample sizes were not sufficient to demonstrate statistical significance, the trend in point estimate between the group with summed Gleason score of at least 8 versus the group with summed Gleason score of less than 8 was large enough to warrant mention in labeling (sensitivity: 55% versus 25%, PPV: 71% versus 33%, sample size: 65 versus 58).
L754: Consultation with the FDA Division of Oncology yielded a similar assessment of the potential clinical utility for 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET in the pre-definitive therapy setting. The consultant judged that the utility appears to be most apparent for men with higher grade tumors (Gleason score 8-10) which would place men into the NCCN high or very high risk category. On the other hand, the consultant judged the utility in men with intermediate risk tumors to be less evident.
L755: While the PSMA-BCR study met its primary endpoint goal for PPV for detection of prostate cancer in biochemically recurrent patients and showed reasonable PET positivity at clinically relevant low PSA levels, subgroup analyses suggest that performance may be modestly decreased in the prostate and prostate bed compared to the remainder of the body. The cause cannot be determined from the study results, but it is most likely a combination of architectural distortion from prior therapies, uptake in residual prostate tissue, excreted radiotracer in the posterior urethra, and scatter or scatter correction artifact from the urinary bladder. This issue will also be mentioned in labeling. Of note, the prostate or prostate bed may be treated with radiotherapy in biochemically recurrent patients regardless of whether tumor is identified there because it is a common site of disease recurrence.
L756: ## **Experimental design and execution:**
L757: In the PSMA-PreRP trial, it was anticipated that 75% of patients would proceed to prostatectomy with pelvic lymph node dissection, and thus have reference standard data. However, only 39% of the enrolled patients received surgery. This observation raises the possibility of biased selection for entry into the primary analysis cohort. While the reasons for patients choosing other therapies could not be captured on an individual basis, the majority of the excluded patients (59%) proceeded to radiation therapy. It seems the most likely explanation for the low surgery rate is that the inclusion criterion for planned prostatectomy was not stringently enforced. The Applicant suggested that if there was bias related to this issue, it would typically involve patients with easier to diagnose disease, such as bulky pelvic lymph nodes, who opt to forgo surgery. The Applicant presents the reasonable argument that such bias would actually underestimate test performance by potentially restricting analysis to patients with relatively less advanced disease that is more difficult to detect.
L758: The type of initial definitive therapy in the PSMA-PreRP study was limited to prostatectomy because this therapy is often accompanied by template pelvic lymph node dissection as standard of care, which allowed collection of histopathology data for the study. In clinical practice, other therapies, particularly external beam radiotherapy or brachytherapy, are often used. However, it is expected that the sensitivity and specificity of 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET will be similar between patients who are candidates for radiotherapy and those who are candidates for surgery.
L759: The PSMA-BCR trial was affected by missing data at multiple levels. The case report form did not allow capture of lesion-level PET data, though this was offset somewhat by the use of 20 subregions. Similarly, the case report form restricted reference standard determination to the region-level. Many patients also had reference standard data for only some of their PET positive regions. Finally, the majority of PET positive patients had no reference standard data for the primary PPV endpoint, and a large minority had no data for the secondary PPV endpoint. Because no study specific follow up was required, the decision of whether or not to obtain reference standard information was made by the treating clinicians who had access to the 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET result in this open-label study. Accordingly, the likelihood of unintended selection

L761: bias and the potential impact on study results are both assessed as high. This concern is supported by subgroup analyses based on PSA level and availability of baseline conventional imaging that showed differences between patients with and without reference standard data. Multiple exploratory analyses were performed to attempt to control for these factors, all of which showed only mild decreases in PPV. Thus, while it is considered probable that the PPV of the test was overestimated, the impact appears to have been minor.
L762: An additional source of bias in the PSMA-BCR trial may have been the lack of blinding of the PET readers to PSA level. Because lesions could not be considered indeterminate in the trial, PSA data may have influenced categorization of lesions when readers were unsure. It also may have prompted a more thorough search for abnormalities in patients with higher PSA levels. Note there is some justification for making PSA level available to PET readers in routine clinical use, but it cannot be assumed to be always available, particularly outside academic settings, and therefore ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET would ideally be able to perform adequately without it. The lack of PSA blinding may have potentially improved PSA stratified PET positivity results, but this was a secondary endpoint. While the study was not powered to detect an effect on PSA stratified PPV, no effect was seen in such analysis.
L763: In both the PSMA-PreRP and PSMA-BCR trials, PET readers were selected from a pool (numbering six and nine, respectively), and each reader examined a different subset of PET scans. This practice was related to resource constraints, and it prevented formal evaluation of per reader results for the major endpoints. The necessary use of majority results for the PET scans is a weakness for both studies. However, analyses of multiple diagnostic performance metrics, including concordance rates, kappa values, and individual reader results, suggest acceptable reproducibility in reader performance such that the majority read data can be considered reasonably valid.

L770: reasonably extrapolated from the pelvic lymph node results given the performance of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 in detecting bone and extrapelvic soft tissue metastases in the PSMA-BCR study.
L771: It is anticipated that ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 will provide substantial clinical utility for patients with prostate cancer. ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 is the first PET radiotracer to target PSMA, and the first approved imaging agent to target the extracellular domain of PSMA. Routine image interpretation of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET is anticipated to be straightforward due to good availability of the radiotracer to the binding site and favorable time of clearance of nonbound radiotracer from the blood pool. An exploratory comparison of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 to CT and MRI from PSMA-PreRP data, also suggests that ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 will outperform conventional imaging (Table 25).
L772: ^{111}\text{In}-capromab (Prostascint), which binds the intracellular domain of PSMA, was approved for single-photon imaging of prostate cancer, but its marketing has been discontinued. In addition to ^{111}\text{In}-capromab, two other radiotracers have been approved for biochemically recurrent prostate cancer, ^{11}\text{C}-choline and ^{18}\text{F}-fluciclovine, and their use has been reported in the literature (Castellucci et al. 2009; Andriole et al. 2019). These radiotracers both identify the increased metabolism associated with prostate cancer, and thus are relatively nonspecific. No direct comparison of test performance is available in the application.
L773: When stratified by PSA, ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 PET appears to show favorable detection rates at low PSA levels in the biochemical recurrence setting. Conventional imaging methods such as CT and ^{99\text{m}}\text{Tc}-medronate bone scan are typically considered to have low detection rates, particularly at lower PSA levels (Kane et al. 2003; Okotie et al. 2004). It should be noted that reported detection rate data are not validated by a reference standard and when stratified by PSA may be confounded by varying proportions of patients with prostatectomy.

L792: ## Categorization of Adverse Events
L793: Patients were monitored for adverse events for 2 hours after drug injection and by phone call at 1 to 3 days after injection. L797: Blood pressure and heart rate were measured before and after injection of 68Ga-PSMA-11.
L798: Clinical laboratory testing was not performed. L854: ## Expectations on Safety in the Postmarket Setting
L855: In terms of adverse events, no significant new issues are anticipated in the postmarket setting. Additionally, despite PSMA being termed “prostate-specific,” it is expressed in other tumor types. These issues have been addressed in the drug labeling to better inform clinicians of the limitations of the test and to reduce associated risk of misdiagnosis.

L872: ## 8.3.2. PSMA-BCR Study
L873: As detailed in Section 8.1.4, patient- and region-level PPVs measured against either histopathology or composite reference standard exceeded the pre-specified performance threshold of 0.70 (70%) when evaluated at the 5% significance level using the Wilson score method. L975: In summary, based on data submitted in this NDA, it is recommended that ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 be approved for PET of patients with biochemical recurrence. The primary efficacy endpoints of patient- and region-level PPVs confirmed by histopathology have been carefully evaluated. Even though the proportion of patients whose reference standard results were unavailable was high at 54% on a patient-level and 65% on a region-level, extensive analyses by imputation or with available subgroup data that explored potential factors that may have biased collection of reference standard information, such as PSA level, region location, and lesion size, consistently suggest a similar positive conclusion regarding patient- and region-level PPV in most of the imputed analyses. These supportive analyses appear to provide a reasonable level of confidence that the satisfactory PPV performance measured in the trial would be maintained in ITD patients with imputation.
L976: Statistically, PPV alone without any counter measure such as NPV does not fully establish diagnostic performance. Therefore, the win criterion of a clinical study that focuses solely on PPV has to be carefully chosen. PPV and NPV are affected by the prevalence of disease, which is difficult to define in the biochemical recurrence setting since each patient can be considered inherently positive for disease. The prevalence of disease by region is even more challenging to define for assessment of PPV and NPV at region-level, as well as at lesion-level.
L977: In the biochemical recurrence setting, the more important consideration is the prevalence of disease that can be successfully imaged, which will in turn be dependent on the imaging techniques that are used. In the PSMA-BCR study, of the 271 patients with baseline conventional imaging results available, 30% (24-35%) had their baseline conventional imaging interpreted as positive, and 44% (38-49%) had their baseline conventional imaging interpreted as either positive or equivocal. Of note, not all of these positive or equivocal interpretations were confirmed to be truly positive, so these rates overestimate actual disease that could be imaged by conventional techniques. These estimated rates of imaged disease are also similar to
L978: those generally reported in the literature for conventional imaging in the setting of biochemical recurrence. While dependent on PSA level among other risk factors, the detection rate in the general BCR population has been estimated as &lt;15% for both CT and bone scan (Kane et al. 2003; Okotie et al. 2004). Thus, the PPV goal of 0.70 for 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET that was met in the PSMA-BCR trial appears to be clinically relevant as it is considerably higher than estimates of imaged disease using conventional techniques.
L979: ## 8.4. Conclusions and Recommendations
L980: The review team finds the benefit of 68Ga-PSMA-11 outweighs its risks. The Applicant has presented sufficient evidence to warrant the approval of 68Ga-PSMA-11 for PET imaging of prostate cancer in patients with suspected metastasis who are candidates for initial definitive therapy or in whom recurrence is suspected based on elevated PSA levels.

L1075: Figure 3 displays the biodistribution of ^{68}\text{Ga}-PSMA-11 in nude mice bearing LNCaP, CWR22Rv1, and PC-3 xenografts, comparing 2-PMPA high dose and 2-PMPA low dose conditions.
(a) Organ Uptake (% ID/g): This bar chart shows the percentage of injected dose (% ID/g) in various organs (blood, liver, intestine, kidney, tumor, lung) for PC-3, CWR22Rv1, and LNCaP xenografts under 2-PMPA high dose and 2-PMPA low dose conditions. For all cell lines, the kidney shows the highest uptake, significantly higher than the tumor (indicated by **). The tumor uptake is significantly higher than the blood uptake (indicated by *). The uptake in the liver and intestine is generally low.
(b) Uptake Ratio: This bar chart shows the uptake ratio (Tumor/Kidney and Tumor/Liver) for PC-3, CWR22Rv1, and LNCaP xenografts under 2-PMPA high dose and 2-PMPA low dose conditions. The Tumor/Kidney ratio is highest for LNCaP (around 0.25) and CWR22Rv1 (around 0.20) compared to PC-3 (around 0.18). The Tumor/Liver ratio is highest for LNCaP (around 0.20) and CWR22Rv1 (around 0.18) compared to PC-3 (around 0.15).
L1076: Figure 4 consists of two panels, (A) and (B), illustrating cell binding, internalization, and binding affinity of ^{68}\text{GaPSMA-11}.
Panel (A): Cell Binding, Internalization, and Blocking Experiments. This bar chart shows the percentage of initially applied radioactivity (%IA) per 10^6 cells, comparing cell surface bound (red bars) and lysate (blue bars) radioactivity, under various conditions (37°C, 4°C, blocked, blocked + 4°C). The conditions are grouped into two temperature regimes: RT (Room Temperature, left side) and 95°C (right side). The Y-axis ranges from 0 to 5 %IA / 10^6 cells.
At 37°C (RT): Cell surface bound radioactivity is approximately 3.0 %IA / 10^6 cells. Lysate radioactivity is approximately 1.5 %IA / 10^6 cells.
At 4°C (RT): Cell surface bound radioactivity is approximately 1.5 %IA / 10^6 cells. Lysate radioactivity is approximately 1.2 %IA / 10^6 cells.
At 37°C (95°C): Cell surface bound radioactivity is approximately 3.2 %IA / 10^6 cells. Lysate radioactivity is approximately 1.0 %IA / 10^6 cells.
At 4°C (95°C): Cell surface bound radioactivity is approximately 1.8 %IA / 10^6 cells. Lysate radioactivity is approximately 0.5 %IA / 10^6 cells.
For blocked conditions (RT and 95°C), radioactivity is minimal in both cell surface bound and lysate fractions.
Panel (B): Binding Affinity. This graph plots cpm (counts per minute) versus concentration c [nM] on a logarithmic scale. Two sets of data are shown, representing the binding affinity of ^{68}\text{GaPSMA-11} under different labeling conditions.
• Glu-urea-Lys(Ahx)-HBED-CC labelled with ^{nat}\text{Ga} at RT (Red triangles)
▲ Glu-urea-Lys(Ahx)-HBED-CC labelled with ^{nat}\text{Ga} at 95°C (Blue triangles)
Both curves show a dose-dependent decrease in cpm, indicating binding. The curve for labeling at 95°C (blue triangles) generally shows slightly higher cpm values (better binding) than the curve for labeling at RT (red triangles) across the measured concentration range (from 10^{-1} nM to 10^4 nM).
