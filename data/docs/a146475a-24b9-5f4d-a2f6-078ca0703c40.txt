L58: ## I. SUMMARY
L59: ## 1. Background
L60: This memorandum responds to a consult request dated May 7, 2019 from the Division of Neurology I (DN1) regarding opicapone, trade name Ongentys (NDA 212489, with associated IND 104380). Opicapone is a selective peripheral, and reversible catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) inhibitor under development by Neurocrine Biosciences, Inc. (the Applicant). It is a new molecular entity (NME) to be used as adjunctive treatment to levodopa/carbidopa treatment in patients with Parkinson's disease (PD) experiencing "OFF" episodes, given at a recommended maintenance dose of 25 or 50 mg orally, taken once daily at bedtime. Opicapone has been approved in the European Union since June 2016 under the name Ongentys.
L61: Opicapone's primary mechanism of action is inhibition of Catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT). Catechol-O-methyltransferase is one of several enzymes that degrade catecholamines such as dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, and catecholestrogens.
L62: Animal models suggest that opicapone does not cross the blood-brain barrier and is not available in the brain at relevant concentration levels to inhibit central COMT activity. However, the blood-brain-barrier (BBB) is impaired in neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson's disease (Kortekaas et al., 2005; Desai et al., 2007; Bartels et al., 2008; Grey et al., 2015) and BBB P-glycoprotein (P-gp) function
L63: decreases with aging (Bartels et al., 2009), therefore, opicapone will be expected to penetrate the CNS of Parkinson's patients.
L64: Opicapone will be administered with levodopa/carbidopa. When taken with levodopa/carbidopa, opicapone blocks COMT, which is the major peripheral metabolizing enzyme for levodopa. As a result, when COMT is inhibited by opicapone, more levodopa is available to cross the blood brain barrier and to be converted to dopamine. In fact, clinical studies have shown that in PD patients opicapone administered at bedtime increased total levodopa exposure 62-94%, and peak levodopa concentrations by 44% (Mod 2.7.2).
L65: Opicapone is not a scheduled substance under the Controlled Substances Act (CSA). Based on in vitro ligand binding testing, opicapone does not demonstrate significant binding activity at receptors associated with abuse-related effects, however, its use is associated with central nervous system (CNS)-mediated adverse events. A number of abuse-related adverse events were reported during clinical studies: in healthy volunteers; somnolence, disturbance of attention and, in Parkinson's patients, hallucinations, somnolence, insomnia, confusional state, memory impairment, disturbance of attention, affective disorder, delirium, agitation, restlessness, aggression, impulse control disorder, dopamine dysregulation syndrome, psychotic disorder, feeling abnormal, depression, anxiety, and paranoia. The most common abuse-related AE was hallucination reaching 9.6% in Study # BIA-OPC-401. The majority of abuse-related AEs such as hallucinations, insomnia, depression, confusion, and restlessness, were dose-related, and increased with a dose of 50 mg (study # BIA-OPC-401) which is likely due to the higher exposure to levodopa.
L66: Based on opicapone's potentially abuse-related AEs, CSS recommended that the Applicant evaluate opicapone for abuse potential (Meeting minutes, Jan 18, 2018). In response, the Applicant submitted a receptor binding study and abuse-related and dependence and withdrawal adverse event assessments.
L67: ## 2. Conclusions
L68: - Opicapone is a new molecular entity (NME) not chemically or pharmacologically similar to any known drug of abuse. The data submitted do not provide a signal of abuse potential and the preliminary data considered during development did not indicate a need for further abuse-related nonclinical or clinical studies. We do not propose to schedule the drug under the Controlled Substances Act.
- Binding assays demonstrate that opicapone and its metabolite BIA 9-1103 did not bind to targets associated with abuse-related effects.
- Abuse-related adverse events were more frequent in Parkinson's patients after opicapone administration than placebo including AEs of hallucinations, memory impairment, confusional state, cognitive impairment, agitation, delirium, affective disorder, and disturbance of attention. Some of these AEs were even more frequent than in entecaponetreated group, such as hallucinations [total: OPC 17 (4.8%), vs entecapone 1 (0.8%)]. The total number of hallucinations in study # BIA-OPC-401 in PD patients reached 9.6% which is highly concerning.
L69: - In the absence of concomitant levodopa administration, COMT inhibitor administration does not increase central dopaminergic activity, thus, abuse of opicapone in healthy individuals is unlikely.
- This drug may pose abuse and misuse risks mainly to the selected group of Parkinson's patients abusing dopaminergic drugs in a context of dopamine dysregulation syndrome (DDS). This risk can be mitigated with appropriate label warnings. Parkinson's patients in this subpopulation who are prone to, or experiencing DDS might have further worsening of their syndrome, considering that opicapone may serve as a particularly strong levodopa-enhancing drug.
- The Applicant did not conduct a systematic evaluation of dependence, withdrawal, or rebound in their Phase 2/3 clinical program. However, it is recommended that product labeling advises to taper the drug at the end of treatment, as it is possible that sudden worsening of PD symptoms after drug discontinuation can lead to decreased postural stability and harm to patients from falls.
L70: ## 3. Recommendations
L71: - Based on the abuse and dependence-related data submitted, opicapone will not require a section 9 Drug Abuse and Dependence in product labeling if NDA 212489 is approved. Opicapone is not being recommended for control under the CSA.
L72: ## 2. Label Recommendations:
L73: - Drug product labeling should have warnings concerning the potential for PD patients to develop DDS, similar to other dopamine enhancing drugs approved to treat PD. We agree with the Warning section language:
L74: “Use with caution in Parkinson's patients with suspected or diagnosed dopamine dysregulation syndrome.”
L75: - We recommend advising to taper the drug at the end of therapy, which can be included in section 2 Dosage and Administration.

L116: ## 3. Clinical Pharmacology
L117: ## 3.1 Pharmacokinetics: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Elimination (ADME)
L118: ## Absorption
L119: For opicapone (OPC), time to maximum plasma concentration (t_{max}) in healthy subjects ranged between 1.5 and 4.0 hours following single doses of up to 1200 mg nonmicronized OPC and multiple doses up to 50 mg qd nonmicronized OPC. For subjects with PD, opicapone 50 mg administered in the evening resulted in a median t_{max} of 4.0 hours (range: 1.5 to 10 hours). Opicapone exposure increased less than dose-proportionally over a 5 mg to 1200 mg range of nonmicronized OPC.
L120: The t_{1/2} of opicapone is short, ranging between 0.8 and 3.2 hours following single doses of 10 to 1200 mg nonmicronized OPC in healthy subjects. In subjects with PD administered OPC 50 mg in the evening, t_{1/2} values ranged from 1.1 to 2.8 hours (mean: 1.85 hours).
L121: ## Distribution
L122: Plasma protein binding of BIA 9-1103 was concentration-independent across the concentration range of 0.3 to 30 µg/mL. BIA 9-1103 did not affect the plasma binding of 14C-OPC.
L123: ## Metabolism
L124: BIA 9-1103 (OPC sulphate) is the only main circulating metabolite of OPC, it is not active and does not have clinically relevant off-target activity.
L125: Other metabolic pathways include reduction, methylation, glucuronidation, and glutathione conjugation. BIA 9-1079, BIA 9-1104 and BIA 9-5049 are active metabolites, but only circulate at low exposures. The rate and extent of exposure to OPC were significantly lower in the fed state compared to the fasting state and t_{max} was delayed with food.
L126: ## Elimination
L127: Following administration of a single dose of ^{14}\text{C}-labeled OPC, a mean of 96% of the administered dose of radioactivity was recovered. The main excretion route was feces, accounting for approximately 70% of the administered radioactivity. The remainder of the radioactivity was excreted in urine (5%) and via expired air (20%). The majority of radioactivity was eliminated within 144 hours postdose.

L169: ## Reviewer's comments
L170: This Applicant generally asserts that hallucinations in PD patients on opicapone are adverse events of levodopa therapy and Parkinson's disease, which is true. However, side-by-side comparison with another COMT-inhibitor entacapone in the same patient population on a comparable treatment regimen for Parkinson's disease shows that opicapone has an increased frequency of hallucinations. This might be explained by the relatively high potency of opicapone to increase levodopa levels. In PD patients receiving levodopa every 3 hours (q3h) or every 4 hours (q4h) who were administered 50 mg qd micronized OPC at bedtime had increased total levodopa exposure 62-94% and increased peak levodopa concentrations of 43% to 44% (Mod 2.7.2, p 18). Levels of levodopa after administration of opicapone were much higher ~20-55% than after administration of entacapone (Study # BIA-91067-124).
L171: Another frequent AE in the study was insomnia, however it was higher for entacapone, 7 (5.7%), relative to both opicapone, 16 (4.5%), and placebo, 1 (0.8%), however opicapone rates were higher than placebo
L172: Other adverse events that were more frequent for opicapone than entacapone were depression/depressed mood, somnolence, sleep disorder, disturbance in attention and memory impairments.

L192: ## *Dopamine dysregulation syndrome (DDS) and Impulse control disorder (ICD)*
L193: Dopamine dysregulation disorder is sometimes considered part of ICD spectrum which includes gambling, binge eating, hypersexuality, and compulsive shopping.
L194: Impulse control disorder will be addressed by the division's medical officer, however, from the data provided by the Applicant it seems there was some increase in frequency of ICD, especially with highest with dose 50 mg of opicapone (see **Table 8**). Dopamine dysregulation disorder was noted once. Also, the phase 4 study BIA-OPC-401 already showed clearly stimulatory character of the drug and emergence of impulse control disorder (0.8%), and some of its aspects hypersexuality (0.2%) and gambling (0.2%). These facts together suggest that the drug likely will be abused by Parkinson's patients with dopamine dysregulation syndrome, therefore warning in the label should be included to use caution in patients with Dopamine dysregulation disorder known or suspected.
L195: The Applicant did not use the Questionnaire for Impulsive-Compulsive Disorders in Parkinson's Disease (QUIP scale) specifically developed to capture different aspects of impulse control disorder which could have provided more granular analysis of DDS and ICD and the tendencies to develop these disorders during the opicapone treatment. The Applicant used only Minnesota Impulsive Disorders Interview - Kleptomania (MIDI), a scale which captures only one of many aspects of ICD.

L219: ## 4.5 Dependence and Withdrawal in Clinical Studies
L220: The Applicant did not systematically evaluate dependence, withdrawal and rebound in phase 2/3 studies. Questionnaires to assess withdrawal symptoms such as depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and suicidality were not administered to the patients during these studies. An assessment for withdrawal symptoms only occurred at the post-study visit, scheduled for 14 (\pm 3) days after drug discontinuation in the Phase 2/3 studies. As the half-life of opicapone is 1.1 to 2.8 hours (mean: 1.85 hours), withdrawal symptoms may begin the day after drug discontinuation. Therefore, this withdrawal assessment likely missed some if not majority of the withdrawal symptoms and did not adequately evaluate dependence and withdrawal.
L221: In the 74-day letter CSS asked the Applicant whether an adequate evaluation of dependence and withdrawal could be conducted in an ongoing or planned placebo-controlled study in PD patients treated with opicapone for at least four weeks. However, the Applicant responded that no ongoing or currently planned placebo-controlled studies of opicapone in patients with Parkinson's disease during which additional withdrawal assessments could be incorporated.
