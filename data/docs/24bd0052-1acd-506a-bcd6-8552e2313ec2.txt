L37: ## 1 Executive Summary
L38: ## 1.1. Product Introduction
L39: ExEm Foam (air polymer-type A) intrauterine foam is an ultrasound contrast agent presented as a single-dose kit comprised of one 11-mL syringe containing 5 mL of clear gel [polymer-type A (hydroxyethyl cellulose), glycerin, and purified water], one 11-mL syringe containing 5 mL of sterile purified water, and a plastic coupling device that connects the two syringes. Air is also present within the syringes, such that when the components are mixed together, a foam is created (ExEm Foam). ExEm Foam is instilled into the uterus via a catheter under ultrasound imaging guidance to evaluate fallopian-tube patency. On ultrasound, the foam appears echogenic (white) when inside the fallopian tube. When the tube is patent, the foam will be seen traveling from the uterine cavity, through the tube, and spilling into the peritoneal cavity. The sterile gel is a 510(k)-cleared ultrasound coupling gel (b) (4)
L40: ## 1.2. Conclusions on Substantial Evidence of Effectiveness
L41: Four adequate and well-controlled studies were submitted as primary sources of efficacy in this 505(b)(2) application. Although underlying patient-level or tube-level source data were not submitted for verification of the study results, the diagnostic performance of sonohysterosalpingography (sono-HSG) with ExEm Foam (hysterosalpingo-foam sonography [HyFoSy]) in all studies exceeded 50% using the lower bound of the 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with point estimates for sensitivity and specificity \ge 80\%. The studies varied in the types of instillation catheters and ultrasound imaging techniques used and in operator experience, reflective of clinical practice. While the two studies that evaluated the diagnostic performance of HyFoSy and sono-HSG with a saline-air mixture (hysterosalpingo-contrast sonography [HyCoSy]) were not designed to demonstrate superiority of HyFoSy over HyCoSy or noninferiority of HyFoSy to HyCoSy, in one study where patients received both HyCoSy and HyFoSy and were evaluated with the reference standard, the point estimates of both tests for sensitivity and specificity were generally similar.
L42: ## 1.3. Benefit-Risk Assessment
L43: ## Benefit-Risk Summary and Assessment
L44: Pathology leading to fallopian tube occlusion is a major cause of infertility in the United States. As a noninvasive, nonionizing imaging modality available at the point of care, ultrasound plays an important role in the diagnostic armamentarium for guiding infertile couples toward appropriate therapy. Yet fallopian tube patency is poorly evaluated on ultrasound without the use of contrast agents, and none currently marketed are approved for fallopian tube assessment. In this multidisciplinary review and evaluation authorized under Section 505(b)(2) of Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, the review team relied on two reports published in the scientific literature to find that substantial evidence supports the effectiveness of ExEm Foam for assessment of fallopian tube patency in women with known or suspected infertility. Both reports demonstrate that in these patients, a high proportion of tubes were correctly identified as patent or occluded, using sonohysterosalpingography with ExEm Foam or equivalent and measured against laparoscopy with chromopertubation. For assessing safety, the review team also relied on data from FDA databases, reports on animal and other preclinical studies published in the scientific literature or owned by the Applicant, and the Applicant's reported experience marketing ExEm Foam outside the United States. Subsequent to labeling revisions, the review team finds that the benefit-risk of the product is favorable and recommends approval.
L45: Dimension	Evidence and Uncertainties	Conclusions and Reasons
Analysis of Condition	Infertility is the failure to achieve a clinical pregnancy after ≥12 months of regular unprotected sexual intercourse.
15% of couples will not conceive during this time period.
In 20 to 30% of couples with infertility, the cause is a tubal abnormality.
Tubal factors account for a proportion of the cases of infertility; an imaging test that can accurately diagnose tubal patency is needed for infertility work-up and subsequent management of infertility.

Current Treatment Options	Laparoscopy with dye is the reference standard for determination of fallopian tube patency.
Imaging tests include sonohysterosalpingography (sono-HSG) with a saline and air mixture acting as a contrast agent (HyCoSy) and hysterosalpingography (HSG) with radiographic iodinated contrast.
The reference standard is invasive and requires exposure to sedation or general anesthesia; the imaging tests are less invasive and do not require sedation/anesthesia.
HSG involves the use of an iodinated contrast agent that could result in a hypersensitivity reaction; the procedure also exposes the patient to radiation.

L47: Dimension	Evidence and Uncertainties	Conclusions and Reasons
Benefit	Compared to HSG, sono-HSG with ExEm Foam (HyFoSy) does not expose the patient to radiation or iodinated radiographic contrast and it can be performed in the office setting.
ExEm Foam used in HyFoSy is postulated to be more stable and have a longer imaging half-life than the saline and air mixture used in HyCoSy; however, differences in stability and imaging half-life between the two ultrasound contrast agents have not been confirmed in clinical trials.
All efficacy studies submitted were from the literature; no patient-level or tube-level data sets were submitted. The studies evaluated the diagnostic performance of HyFoSy compared to the reference standard.
The definition of tubal patency indicated by peritoneal spillage of contrast agent was used in all studies submitted for efficacy except for one.
None of the efficacy studies were prospective studies designed to compare the diagnostic performance of HyFoSy to HSG.
The two efficacy studies that evaluated the diagnostic performance of both HyCoSy and HyFoSy were not designed to demonstrate superiority of HyFoSy over HyCoSy or noninferiority of HyFoSy to HyCoSy; in the one study where patients were evaluated with both HyCoSy and HyFoSy, the diagnostic performance of both tests was similar.
Studies varied in terms of ultrasound imaging techniques and instillation catheters used.
Despite variations in operator experience, ultrasound imaging techniques, and instillation catheters used (reflecting clinical sites' preferences), the sensitivity and specificity of HyFoSy was consistent across the studies and exceeded 60%.

Risk and Risk Management	Adverse events reported in the literature and in the foreign postmarketing database are similar to those reported for HSG and HyCoSy and are likely related to the procedure itself and not to ExEm Foam.
Potential safety concern of scarring of the peritoneum or reproductive tract impacting fertility was not identified in the literature search. The pH of the foam is physiologic, and the osmolality of the foam is less than that of approved HSG imaging agents, where no such concern has been identified.
Although the studies utilized various catheters for instillation without incident, the Applicant did not provide performance testing data to confirm the physical compatibility of ExEm Foam with cleared HSG and sono-HSG catheters.
Standard warnings and contraindications for HSG are applicable to HyFoSy and are included in the labeling.
The labeling will recommend usage of the foam with French gauge (Fr) 5 or larger catheters; one study reported resistance to instillation with use of a 5-Fr catheter. In the event of resistance to instillation, the label cautions against injecting forcefully and recommends using a larger catheter for instillation.
Catheter failure and breakage will be a focus of monitoring in the postmarket setting.

L51: ## 2 Therapeutic Context
L52: ## 2.1. Analysis of Condition
L53: The World Health Organization (WHO) defines infertility as “a disease of the reproductive system defined by the failure to achieve a clinical pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse” (WHO 2019). It is estimated that 15% of couples will not conceive during this time period, and these couples may benefit from an infertility work-up (Lindsay and Vitrikas 2015). Primary infertility refers to infertility when the couple is never able to conceive; secondary infertility occurs when the couple is not able to conceive following a successful birth. Infertility may arise from female factors, male factors, or a combination of both, and the fertility work-up includes evaluation of both partners. In 20% to 30% of couples, infertility is caused by a tubal abnormality such as scarring from previous infection (Brigham and Women's Hospital 2019).
L54: ## 2.2. Analysis of Current Treatment Options
L55: There are two tests that are widely used in the United States for the assessment of tubal patency: hysterosalpingography (HSG) and laparoscopy with chromopertubation. Chromopertubation is considered the reference standard exam. A dye such as methylene blue (an off-label use) is injected into the uterine cavity. Tubal patency demonstrated through contrast spillage from the fallopian tube fimbriae into the peritoneum is directly visualized with the laparoscope. HSG is an alternative and widely accepted procedure to laparoscopy with dye (Saunders et al. 2011). Tubal patency is established by visualization of radiographic contrast spillage into the peritoneum under fluoroscopy. Disadvantages of the procedure include radiation exposure and allergic reaction to the radiographic contrast agent. Omnipaque 240 and 300 (NDA 018956) and Lipiodol (NDA 009190) are approved contrast agents for HSG.
L56: Sono-HSG evaluates fallopian tube patency with ultrasound. When contrast is used, the technique is referred to as hysterosalpingo-contrast sonography (HyCoSy). The contrast agent in HyCoSy can be variable, as in HSG.
L57: No currently marketed
L58: ultrasound contrast agents have this indication. Alternatively, saline alone or a mixture of saline and air can be used as the contrast agent. Saline, acting as a negative contrast agent, appears black or anechoic within the uterine cavity and fallopian tubes. The air in the mixture increases the echogenicity of the saline in the fallopian tubes and peritoneum.
L59: The saline and air mixture acts as a positive contrast agent with resultant improved visualization under ultrasound. Physicians may alternate instillation of air and saline to create this mixture or use cleared devices such as FemVue (K110288) and SonoFlow (K142545) to provide this mixture. However, some users may prefer a positive contrast imaging agent over a negative contrast imaging agent. It is postulated that due to potential greater stability of ExEm Foam as compared to the saline and air mixture, the foam might provide a longer imaging half-
L60: life. However, testing has not been performed to demonstrate that ExEm Foam has a longer imaging half-life.

L121: ## 5.1. Executive Summary
L122: This NDA should be approved from a nonclinical perspective.
L123: ExEm Foam is prepared from a clear gel and sterile purified water. The gel is composed of glycerol 85% (b) (4), HEC (b) (4), and purified water USP/EP (b) (4). Glycerol, a clear, viscous, water soluble polyhydric alcohol is present in ultrasound gel, vaginal moisturizers and lubricants, food additives, cosmetics, and personal health care products. HEC is a water soluble, modified cellulose polymer containing hydroxyethyl side chains commonly used in personal vaginal moisturizers and lubricants, lotions, oils, skin and hair care products. The safety of glycerol and HEC is well established by their widespread and long-term clinical use, which is well documented in the literature and made available by the Applicant to support this NDA.
L124: The Applicant conducted biocompatibility safety studies on glycerol and HEC components of this product and no safety concern was reported. Based on available nonclinical pharmacology, pharmacokinetics (PK), toxicity, genotoxicity, reproductive and developmental toxicity, local tolerance, skin sensitization, and compatibility data on glycerol and HEC, there is no nonclinical safety concern with the use of ExEm Foam for the proposed indication and the new route of administration. However, because of lack of nonclinical safety studies involving intrauterine exposure of glycerol, HEC, or ExEm Foam, it was difficult to determine the risk of inflammatory reactions and the potential for scarring and adhesions in the uterus, tubes, and around the ovaries. The safety assessment of ExEm Foam following intrauterine exposure was therefore conducted using the following tools:
L125: - Search on FDA database for products containing glycerol or HEC for safety comparison to ExEm Foam
- Applicant's literature search on possible ExEm Foam effects such as embolization, inflammation, scarring, and adhesion following intrauterine or intravaginal use
- Search on available CDRH data on the clear gel and other products containing glycerol and HEC
- Relevant CMC data including osmolality and pH of ExEm Foam
L126: The information obtained from the FDA database on products containing glycerol and HEC yielded no safety signals and supported the safety information in the NDA. The analysis demonstrates that there are no safety concerns with the intended route of administration for ExEm Foam.

L129: ## 5.3. Pharmacology
L130: No stand-alone safety pharmacology data were provided in this submission because there is no known direct or indirect pharmacological activity associated with glycerol and HEC. Glycerol and HEC are extensively used in cosmetics, personal care, and health care products. Glycerol is present endogenously as a result of dietary intake from plant and animal sources and a precursor for glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and synthesis of triacylglycerols. HEC is a modified cellulose. It is characterized by poor permeability across gastrointestinal (GI) epithelial mucosa. Glycerol is systemically absorbed following intrauterine exposure of ExEm Foam, though it does not significantly affect the basal fasting plasma glycerol. However, HEC demonstrates very low permeability across epithelial mucosa of the uterus and is expected to exhibit negligible effect following intrauterine administration.
L131: The Applicant provided literature PK data on glycerol and HEC. The proposed route of administration for clear gel is intrauterine administration. However, these routes were not employed for glycerol or HEC in the nonclinical studies conducted in the available literature. The Applicant stated that nonclinical studies with oral administration are acceptable surrogates to characterize systemic toxicity potential with intrauterine administration. This statement is based on the similarity of the putative mechanisms of absorption across the GI and uterine mucosa for both compounds. The Applicant submitted PK data following oral administration of glycerol and HEC to assess the relevant PK parameters of ExEm Foam. The review team found that oral administration studies are not optimal surrogates for intrauterine and intravaginal administration studies, given that there is greater potential for absorption, irritation, inflammation, and epithelial cell damage in the uterus and vagina than in the GI tract.

L136: Type of Study	Major Findings
Metabolism	
Glycerol
Metabolic pathways of glycerol have been well elucidated in rats (Lin 1977; Frank et al. 1981). Systemically absorbed glycerol is initially phosphorylated to glycerol-3-phosphate by glycerol kinase. Up to 70% to 90% of glycerol-3-phosphate is subsequently oxidized to dihydroxyacetone phosphate by glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, and then isomerized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. It can then proceed to either the gluconeogenesis or glycolysis pathways (Doerschuk 1951). The remaining 10% to 30% of glycerol-3-phosphate is esterified to free fatty acids as triglycerides. The triglycerides in the body can be catabolized to free glycerol and fatty acids (which subsequently undergo beta-oxidation). Administration of ExEm Foam is not expected to alter the endogenous metabolic equilibrium since the systemic glycerol absorption will not impact normal endogenous fasting levels after ExEm administration.
HEC
No reports on the metabolism of HEC, a modified cellulose, were found in the literature. However, the metabolism of unmodified cellulose is well characterized (Cummings 1984). Anaerobic microbes in the colon can produce cellulase enzymes that cleave the 1,4 \beta linkages to form shorter chain polysaccharides, which are further fermented into short-chain volatile fatty acids (e.g., acetate, propionate, butyrate), CO2, CH4, and H2 (Chassard et al. 2005). Volatile fatty acids are further metabolized by known pathways for propionate to succinyl-CoA (tricarboxylic acid cycle); acetate is metabolized to acetyl-CoA; and butyrate is mostly metabolized to ketone bodies and CO2 in the colonic epithelial cells. Available rat data on [14C]-HPC (CTFA 1968; Kitagawa et al. 1976) demonstrate complete recovery of radioactivity in feces (>96%), which suggests minimal HPC metabolism and no volatile fatty acids formation

Excretion	
Glycerol
Data obtained from rats administered single doses of 26.3 mg [14C]glycerol showed that only 0.95% to 1.5% of the dose was recovered in feces after 24 hours (Doerschuk 1951). Consistent with metabolism of glycerol to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, and subsequent oxidation to CO2 by way of pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, the main route of excretion was respiratory 14CO2, accounting for 80% to 81% of the dose. The minor excretion of glycerol is responsible for urinary recovery of 14C, which was only 6.1% to 6.2% of the dose, in keeping with reabsorption of glycerol reported in the kidney (Lin 1977). The percentage 14C recovery was found as lipids in the carcass and liver, in agreement with glycerol-3-phosphate being an important intermediate in lipid synthesis and accounting for the remaining portion of excretion. Total 14C recovery in these studies ranged from 96.0% to 96.8%.
HEC
There are no available data on the excretion of HEC in the literature. However, data on two studies involving oral administration of [14C]HPC in rats showed that >96% radioactivity recovered was associated with feces; urine recovery ranged from 1.5% to 3.2% (CTFA 1968; Kitagawa et al. 1976). There was no radioactivity recovered in blood or as expired CO2.

L188: ## Genotoxicity Data on HEC
L189: There is lack of genotoxicity studies for HEC in the literature. However, there is information on several studies on the genotoxicity of structurally similar cellulose analogs (e.g., methyl cellulose [MC]), carboxymethyl cellulose, ethyl cellulose [EC]). These analogs tested negative in *in vitro* bacterial reverse mutation assays, several *in vitro* mammalian systems including host-mediated assay, chromosomal aberration study, mouse lymphoma L5178Y TK+/- assay, and
L190: several in vivo mammalian systems (e.g., micronucleus test, host-mediated assay, dominant lethal assays, and chromosomal aberration tests). Thus, this class of compound is not genotoxic.
L191: ## *In vitro nonmammalian assays*
L192: Ames test conducted on MC at up to 50 µg/plate with *Salmonella typhimurium* strains TA98, TA100, TA1535, TA1537, and TA1538, evaluated in the presence and absence of metabolic activation was negative (Blevins and Taylor 1982). Similarly, carboxymethyl cellulose was negative in *S. typhimurium* strains TA98 and TA100 in the presence and absence of metabolic activation, and also negative in *E. Coli* WP-2 in the absence of metabolic activation (Sugimura 1976). Coli* strain WP2uvrA/PkM101 (CM891) with and without metabolic activation (DeMerlis et al. 2005).
L193: ## *In vitro mammalian assays*
L194: MC at a concentration of 100 mg/mL was evaluated for mutagenicity in an in vitro host-mediated and chromosomal aberration study (Litton Bionetics 1974; Bergfeld et al. 2009) and showed no increase in mutant or recombinant frequency using *S. typhimurium* TA1530 and G-46 strains. In an in vitro chromosomal aberration study involving human tissue culture cells exposed to concentrations up to 800 µg/mL, no significant structural aberrations were reported in anaphase chromosomes (Litton Bionetics 1974). EC was tested for mutagenic potential in the mouse lymphoma assay (L5178Y TK+/- cells) in the presence and absence of S9 metabolic activation (DeMerlis et al. 2005) and no significant increase in mutation frequency was reported.
L195: ## *In vivo mammalian assays*
L196: In a host-mediated study involving mice administered 5 g/kg, MC demonstrated no significant increase in mutant or recombinant frequencies using *Saccharomyces* D3 (Litton Bionetics 1974; Bergfeld et al. 2009). Data obtained from an in vivo chromosomal aberration study involving rats orally administered single doses of MC orally up to 5 g/kg showed no significant structural aberrations in bone marrow metaphase chromosomes (Litton Bionetics 1974; Bergfeld et al. 2009). MC also demonstrates nonmutagenic effect in a dominant lethal assay in male rats dosed up to 5 g/kg and mated to untreated females (Litton Bionetics 1974; Bergfeld et al. 2009). In an in vivo micronucleus test, EC (tested as the excipient Surelease, which contained 25% EC) failed to cause any chromosome damage or bone marrow cell toxicity when orally administered by gavage to mice at doses of 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 g/kg (DeMerlis et al. 2005).

L218: ## Local Tolerance
L219: There is a lack of literature data on local tolerance studies for glycerol and HEC. However, in a published paper, it was found that 2.8% HEC in water is noncytotoxic to cervical HeLa cells in a 24-hr incubation at 37°C in 5% CO2 (Cunha et al. 2014). The Applicant provided a summary of FDA 510(k) clearance for marketed vaginal moisturizer/personal lubricant products in which glycerol and HEC are ingredients. In all the products, vaginal irritation studies were conducted in rabbits. The rabbit studies involved intravaginal application of the product once daily for approximately 5 days and following sacrificing, the entire vagina excised, examined, and scored for discharge, edema, and erythema. Histological examinations of the tissue were performed and found that all the products containing glycerol and/or HEC were reported to be nonirritating (data for Intimol™ were not specified). However, the Applicant did not provide the concentrations or amounts of glycerol and HEC components in these products. Therefore, it was not possible to compare to the glycerol and HEC exposure expected from ExEm Foam administration.
L220: ## Dermal Irritation
L221: ## Glycerol
L222: Dermal irritation of glycerol has been evaluated in a number of studies conducted in rabbits and guinea pigs.
L223: In a rabbit dermal irritation study conducted with glycerol (Weil and Scala 1971), glycerol was found to be nonirritating. Glycerol was applied (amounts not specified) to a 1"x1" shaved area on the backs of the animals, which was then occluded with a patch. Another similar study involving natural or synthetic glycerol applied to the shaved backs of female albino rabbits (Hine et al. 1953) also found no evidence of irritation. A modified Draize test was employed in another study in rabbits where 0.5 mL of a glycerol/water mixture was applied to the backs of female New Zealand rabbits, with skin evaluated 24 and 72 hours after application (Clark et al. 1979). The product was also found to be nonirritating. In guinea pig study, intradermal injection of 0.1 mL glycerol was administered to the abdominal region of guinea pig. Irritation was scored after 4 hours, 24 hours, and 1 week after injection (Latven and Molitor 1939). Glycerol was found to be minimally irritating (graded 1 on a scale of 0 to 4).
L224: ## HEC
L225: In a repeat-insult patch test (Elder 1986) HEC at 5% and 100% concentrations was found to be nonirritating. Each solution (0.5 mL) was applied on two patch areas: the right (scarified) and left (intact) flanks of male albino rabbits (6 per group). Patches exposed to HEC, HPC, and MC were occluded for 23 hours, removed, and readings (scale of 0 to 8) taken 1 hour and 48 hours later.

L229: ## *Glycerol*
L230: The sensitization potential of glycerol was evaluated following intradermal injection of 0.1 mL of 0.1% glycerol solution (natural or synthetic) in male guinea pigs every other day for a total of 10 injections. L231: ## HEC
L232: There were no reports on evaluation of the sensitization potential of HEC found in the literature. The Magnusson-Kligman guinea pig maximization test was conducted to evaluate sensitization of HPMC (CTFA 1980). The test involved administration of three intradermal injections into the shaven shoulder of 0.1 mL of 50% complete Freund's adjuvant in saline, 0.1 mL of 1% HPMC in saline, and 0.1 mL of 1% HPMC in 50% complete Freund's adjuvant in saline. Furthermore, HPMC was evaluated for sensitization in Hartley albino guinea pigs (n=10 males per group) using a modified Maguire method (CTFA 1978). Similarly, no responses were reported in this testing with HPMC.

L278: ## 6.1. Executive Summary
L279: No clinical pharmacology studies have been conducted by the Applicant.
L280: ExEm Foam is composed of 5 mL clear gel, containing glycerol and HEC. When the gel is mixed with an equal amount of sterile purified water, the viscosity of the gel is decreased, and air bubbles are introduced into the gel, creating an echogenic contrast agent. The product has no pharmacological activity; it is a foam that can flow through patent fallopian tubes, which is visible on ultrasound.
L281: Assuming that a full 10 mL of ExEm Foam is administered and all the glycerol is absorbed, the normal fasting endogenous glycerol plasma levels would not be affected. According to the Applicant, no literature data are found on absorption of HEC. However, due to the apparent poor permeability of HEC across GI epithelial mucosa, HEC would be expected to exhibit very low permeability across the epithelial mucosa of the uterus, and, consequently, produce negligible HEC systemic exposure (see also Sections 0 and 6.3 for details of ADME). Therefore, there is likely a very low exposure to HEC from the intrauterine application of ExEm Foam.

L286: ## 6.2.2. General Dosing and Therapeutic Individualization
L287: ExEm Foam is prepared using two syringes, one containing 5 mL of clear gel, and the other containing 5 mL of sterile purified water, in two 11-mL piston syringes. The syringes are connected with a coupling device. To prepare ExEm Foam, the syringes are depressed at least 10 times to dilute the gel and create small bubbles. As the gel and water are pushed through small openings in the syringes, turbulence causes local pressure drops, resulting in air dissolving in the gel and yielding the air-containing ExEm Foam that is stable for several minutes. The resulting foam is then infused into the uterine cavity via a standard catheter.

L327: ## Efficacy
L328: Four studies included in the first section of Table 18 were used for primary evidence of efficacy (van Schoubroeck et al. 2013; Ludwin et al. 2017a; Piccioni et al. 2017; Riganelli et al. 2018). In these studies, HyFoSy was performed in women with infertility or subfertility and compared to the reference standard, laparoscopy and dye. Diagnostic performance results (i.e., sensitivity and specificity) were reported or derived from 2x2 tables generated by the Applicant based on the publications. The Riganelli study compared the diagnostic performance of HyFoSy using 2D and 3D imaging techniques. Two studies (Ludwin et al. 2017a; Piccioni et al. 2017) compared the diagnostic performance of HyFoSy to HyCoSy, an alternative ultrasound test for tubal patency.
L329: The reference standard (laparoscopy and dye) uses the presence or absence of peritoneal spillage of the contrast agent to define a patent or an occluded tube, respectively. There was some variability in the ultrasound definitions used for patent and occluded; therefore, these definitions are reported for each study below. For both the reference standard and the ultrasound imaging tests, positive results refer to occluded tubes and negative results refer to patent tubes.
L330: Two studies (Dreyer et al. 2015; Rosic et al. 2018) were considered supportive. These two studies provided diagnostic performance results that were complementary to the four primary studies above. The women who were evaluated in these two studies underwent tubal occlusion with the Essure device for either sterilization (Rosic et al. 2018) or treatment of hydrosalpinx (Dreyer et al. 2015). Therefore, these women were highly likely to have tubal occlusion on HyFoSy as compared to the women above being evaluated for infertility. For example, the Ludwin paper estimated a 6% tubal occlusion rate for its sample size estimation. The papers by Rosic and Dreyer have limited generalizability due to the differences between the studied populations and the proposed target population. Therefore, these papers were treated as supportive sources of efficacy.
L331: ## Safety
L332: The primary sources of clinical evidence for safety came from published literature reporting ExEm Foam kit use and the pharmacovigilance database from the foreign postmarketing experience. These sources were augmented by comparisons of the product to the approved contrast agents for HSG (chemical profile) and cleared devices for HyCoSy using saline and air (safety profile). The safety profile of [REDACTED](b)(4) (clear gel) in the context of use as a coupling agent was also evaluated. For both the safety and efficacy analyses, although p-values were reported in the published literature as part of the studies' results, the review team considered these p-values nominal and they were not considered during the review.

L380: “Accuracy of Hysterosalpingo-Foam Sonography in Comparison to Hysterosalpingo-Contrast Sonography With Air/Saline and to Laparoscopy With Dye” (Ludwin et al. 2017a) was a prospective, observational study that included sexually active women between the ages of 21 to 40 years who had a medical history of infertility without prior evaluation for tubal patency. L383: ## Ultrasound Imaging Readers/Performers
L384: Three investigators evaluated tubal patency with ultrasound. All were specialists in gynecology and obstetrics, “with 12 to 15 years [of] experience in 2D ultrasound imaging and HyCoSy and 7 to 10 years of experience in 3D imaging.” The publication did not state the investigators’ experience with HyFoSy. Each investigator performed one of the tubal patency procedures for a given patient: 2D air/saline-HyCoSy, 2D/3D-HyFoSy, or 2D/3D-HDF-HyFoSy. The procedure performed was decided by a randomization program. Each investigator was blinded to the results of the other investigators’ findings.
L385: ## Reference Standard Readers/Performers
L386: Two different investigators (i.e., not the ultrasound readers) evaluated tubal patency with laparoscopy and dye. L388: ExEm Foam was prepared as per manufacturer’s instructions and was instilled via a 5-Fr silicone catheter with balloon.
L389: ## Ultrasound Imaging Technique
L390: During administration of the foam, tubal patency was evaluated real-time with 2D ultrasound. L391: ## 8.1.2.2. Study Endpoints
L392: Diagnostic accuracy of 2D/3D-HyFoSy and 2D/3D-HDF-HyFoSy in evaluating tubal patency as compared to laparoscopy and dye as the reference standard.
L393: ## Definition of Patent Tube on HyFoSy and HyCoSy
L394: - Contrast flow observed to pass from the tube into the peritoneal cavity
OR
- Contrast flow observed in either the intramural or isthmic part of the tube for (a) 10 seconds and/or (b) during 3D volume acquisition without any sign of hydrosalpinx and in the presence of peritoneal spillage
L395: ## Definition of Occluded Tube on HyFoSy and HyCoSy
L396: - Contrast not observed to pass from the tube into the abdominal cavity
OR
- Tubal lumen distended with flow visualized in the intramural or isthmic part of the tube
L397: The above definitions were used to define negative and positive imaging results on a tube-level, which when compared to the results of the reference standard were used for the calculation of diagnostic accuracy.
L398: *Comment: HSG and laparoscopy with dye use presence or absence of peritoneal spillage to diagnose the presence of a patent or occluded tube, respectively. The investigators used additional definitions for tubal patency and occlusion beyond these definitive criteria. For example, they considered that flow only within a portion of the visualized tube would be sufficient for diagnosis of fallopian tube patency. Unlike laparoscopy with dye, contrast flow within the entire length of the tubes can be directly visualized under fluoroscopy during HSG when patent, however, definitive diagnosis of tubal patency with HSG remains dependent on visualization of peritoneal spillage.*
L399: *The authors did not record which criterion was used to diagnose tubal patency or occlusion for each individual tube. Allowance of the second definition of tubal patency (flow within the tube for 10 seconds without seeing peritoneal spillage) would result in a greater likelihood of calling a tube patent, thereby decreasing sensitivity (by increasing false negatives) and increasing specificity (by increasing true negatives).*
L400: ## 8.1.2.3. Statistical Analysis
L401: Sample-size calculation was based on the sensitivity of 2D/3D-HDF-HyFoSy, with expected sensitivity of 99% and a disease prevalence of tubal occlusion of 6%. Required sample size was calculated to be 254 tubes or 127 patients assuming two tubes for each patient.
L402: The study compared the diagnostic accuracy of and the concordance between the three ultrasound imaging procedures: 2D-air/saline-HyCoSy, 2D/3D-HyFoSy, and 2D/3D-HDF-HyFoSy. McNemar's test was used to compare all the diagnostic methods. Data were presented in a contingency table, with p-values for comparing the methods' diagnostic accuracy (ACC). Agreement between each test was evaluated using the kappa (\kappa) statistic, proportion of agreement, positive percent agreement and negative percent agreement. The \kappa-value was interpreted in terms of the strength of agreement as follows: poor, 0.20; fair, 0.21 to 0.40; moderate, 0.41 to 0.60; good, 0.61 to 0.80; and very good, 0.81 to 1.00.

L697: ## Procedure-Related Embolization or Contrast Intravasation
L698: The prevalence of contrast agent intravasation during HSG has been reported to be 0.4% to 6.9% (Bhoil et al. 2016). This complication is the result of “backflow of injected contrast into the adjoining vessels.” The contrast passes from the uterine cavity to the myometrial veins and eventually into the pelvic veins and inferior vena cava. Conditions that increase endometrial vascularity and permeability are predisposing risk factors for contrast intravasation. Contrast intravasation may result in complications such as pulmonary embolization. Patients may present with coughing or dyspnea. The risk of pulmonary embolization related to intravasation of Lipiodol is included in its PI.
L699: There were no reports in the literature or complaints in the Applicant database of HyFoSy procedure-related intravasation or embolization or symptoms consistent with such complications. However, the ultrasound imager may not easily visualize the occurrence of foam intravasation, as the imaging plane of the uterus and fallopian tubes will likely be in a different plane than the pelvic and myometrial veins. Therefore, to specifically evaluate for this complication when the patient is asymptomatic, the imager would have to (1) move the probe towards these veins and (2) recognize the presence of echogenic material within the veins (which are normally anechoic). This is in contrast to HSG, where the entire pelvis is imaged in a single field-of-view during the procedure. As a result, opacification of the pelvic and myometrial veins by contrast is immediately visible when contrast intravasation occurs on HSG.
L700: The risk related to foam intravasation and embolization was further assessed by evaluating the consequences of intravasation and embolization of IV air, HEC, and glycerol at the volumes used in creating the foam.
L701: The labeling for CDRH-cleared devices that mix saline and air for sono-HSG/HyCoSy, FemVue (K110288) and SonoFlow (K142545), address the risk of air embolism. The FemVue label includes the following warning: “CAUTION: In order to minimize the risk of air embolism, do not exceed delivery of six (6) filled syringe volumes to the patient. Air embolism has not been reported with saline-air contrast volumes below 70 mL” (FemVue 2019). In contrast, the maximum volume of the foam is 10 mL, of which air is a very small component given that the foam is composed of 5 mL of clear gel and 5 mL of sterile purified water. Further, a review of the AEs reported to the FDA related to use of the FemVue and SonoFlow devices revealed no complaints related to intravasation or embolization. A review article on vascular air embolism classifies volumes of acute air embolism &lt;0.5 mL/kg as small and often well tolerated (Mirski et al. 2007).
L702: There is preclinical evidence demonstrating the safety of IV glycerol and HEC. Glycerol is also used as an inactive ingredient in FDA-approved products for IV administration as per the FDA Inactive Ingredient Database.

ExEm Foam is contraindicated in women with known or suspected reproductive tract malignancy due to risk of retrograde/peritoneal spread of tumor.

Several studies confirmed that the patients enrolled in the study were not pregnant prior to HyFoSy and/or scheduled the procedure during the proliferative phase of the menstrual cycle to minimize chance of pregnancy during HyFoSy. The studies did not assess risk of fetal harm related to the procedure. The Division of Pediatric and Maternal Health also performed a literature search of the PubMed, Embase, Micromedex, TERIS, Reprotox, and Briggs databases to find additional relevant articles not cited by the Applicant.6 No additional relevant articles were retrieved. See Section 5.5 for additional information.

L712: ## Overdose, Drug Abuse Potential, Withdrawal, and Rebound
L713: The product will be instilled into the uterus by the health care provider, most likely a gynecologist, radiologist, or medical staff under the provider's supervision. L726: ## 8.2.8. Integrated Assessment of Safety
L727: Based on the foreign postmarketing experience and the experience in the literature, it is expected that patients who undergo HyFoSy will experience symptoms related to the procedure itself such as pain, vasovagal reaction, and spotting. The incidence of infections is expected to be rare and likely within the range seen with HSG and HyCoSy. The product will be contraindicated in women with known or suspected genital tract infection or inflammation and in women who have had a gynecologic procedure within the last 30 days to minimize the risk of postprocedure gynecologic infection. An adverse reaction related to ExEm Foam itself or its components is unlikely. Peritoneal or reproductive tract scarring is not expected. The literature search does not show evidence of a risk of scarring. The pH of the female reproductive tract is comparable to the pH of ExEm Foam. The product has a lower osmolality compared to other approved agents for HSG, and it is intended for one-time use. Regardless, the potential for scarring will be monitored in the postmarket setting.
L728: ## 8.3. Statistical Issues
L729: This application is based on published medical literature. Except for (Ludwin et al. 2017a), which studied 132 patients (259 tubes), the study sample size was generally small (18 to 25 patients with 36 to 50 tubes). Diagnostic performance on sensitivity and specificity were prespecified in some studies (Ludwin et al. 2017a; Piccioni et al. 2017), whereas two studies (van Schoubroeck et al. 2013; Riganelli et al. 2018) lacked such prespecification. It is unclear whether the analysis results of the Riganelli et al. study (2018) were performed on a tube-level or on a patient-level; sensitivity and specificity were not defined. Based on the statistical team's review, results presented in the paper are suspected to be on a tube-level.

L731: The benefit risk of ExEm Foam is favorable. The diagnostic performance of HyFoSy is in comparison to the reference standard is acceptable. Based on the literature and the foreign postmarketing experience, the product does not appear to pose additional concerns of safety beyond the risks already expected with sono-HSG or HSG.
