L89: Chemical structure of Ziftomenib. The structure features a thieno[2,3-d]pyrimidin-4-yl ring system connected via an amino group to a piperidin-1-yl ring. This piperidin ring is further connected via a methyl group to an indole-2-carbonitrile ring system. The indole ring is substituted with a propyl chain that connects to a piperazin-1-yl ring, which is in turn connected to a methylsulfonyl group. The thieno[2,3-d]pyrimidin ring also has a trifluoroethyl group and a methylamino group.
L90: Mechanism of Action: Ziftomenib is a menin inhibitor that blocks that interaction of menin and lysine [K]-specific methyltransferase 2A (KMT2A). By disrupting the interactions between menin and KMT2A (wild-type or rearranged), menin inhibitors disrupt the leukemogenic transcriptional signaling induced by chromatin-binding complexes that are facilitated by and contain menin and rearranged KMT2A. NPM1 is a multifunctional protein that typically resides in the nucleolus but becomes mainly cytosolic and a leukemogenic driver when it is mutated (NPM1c). The leukemogenic activity of NPM1c is mediated by the activity of the menin/KMT2A (wild-type or rearranged) chromatin-modulating complex and is inhibited by the activity of menin inhibitors.
L91: Ziftomenib is a new molecular entity (NME). NDA 220305 was submitted under the 505(b)(1) pathway for the proposed indication of adult patients with relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with a nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) mutation using a dose of 600 mg daily.

L97: 1.2. Conclusions on the Substantial Evidence of Effectiveness
L98: Study KO-MEN-001 (KOMET-001, NCT NCT04067336) was an open-label, Phase 1-2, multiple cohort, multicenter study of ziftomenib monotherapy in adult patients \geq 18 years of age with relapsed or refractory (R/R) acute myeloid leukemia (AML) harboring a mutation in the NPM1 gene (NPM1m). Patients in the pivotal cohort of the study were required to have NPM1 mutations classified as A, B, or D subtypes; other mutations in NPM1 were permitted if they were reasonably likely to lead to cytoplasmic localization of the NPM1 protein. Treatment consisted of ziftomenib at a dose of 600 mg daily until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity.
L99: The Phase 1 portion was an open-label, dose-escalation study for patients with non-genetically defined R/R AML; seven single arm cohorts evaluated ziftomenib dosages between 50 and 1000 mg daily (n=30). The Phase 1b portion sought to optimize the dosage of ziftomenib and compared two dosages (200 mg daily, n=17, and 600 mg daily, n=36) in patients with R/R NPM1m AML. The Phase 2 portion enrolled 92 patients with R/R NPM1m AML treated at a ziftomenib dosage of 600 mg daily. Local testing was used for diagnosis and enrollment. The primary objective of the Phase 2 portion was determination of the CR + CRh rate.
L100: The pivotal cohort was the population of study participants enrolled in the Phase 2 portion of the study (n=92). L130: 2 Therapeutic Context
L131: 2.1. Analysis of Condition
L132: The Applicant's Position:
L133: Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a rapidly progressing and heterogeneous hematopoietic disease arising from genetic and epigenetic dysregulation in hematopoietic precursor cells leading to the clonal proliferation of myeloid precursors with an inability to differentiate into more mature cellular elements. As a result, there is an accumulation of these immature cells (ie, leukemic blasts) in the bone marrow, peripheral blood, and occasionally in other tissues, with a variable reduction in the production of normal red blood cells (RBCs), platelets, and mature granulocytes. The increased production of malignant cells, along with a reduction in these mature elements, results in a variety of systemic consequences. The clinical manifestations include cytopenias, bleeding or bruising, fatigue, and an increased risk of infection, which may all be accompanied by constitutional symptoms, metabolic abnormalities, and various other complications.
L134: Although AML is the most common form of acute leukemia in adults, it is a relatively rare disease. AML accounts for nearly 2% of all adult cancer deaths in the United States (US). In 2024, 20,800 people were estimated to be diagnosed with AML, and 11,220 were expected to die from AML (NCI, 2023). The median age at diagnosis is 69 years of age in adults, with approximately 60% of patients diagnosed at \geq 65 years of age and one-third of patients diagnosed at \geq 75 years of age. Overall, AML is a heterogenous disease in which high initial response rates are observed followed by frequent relapse. More than half of patients who achieve complete remission (CR) following induction therapy will eventually relapse within 1 to 3 years, and only 31.9% of patients with AML in the US will survive \geq 5 years (Kumar, 2011; NCI, 2023). With relapse, survival outcomes decline drastically; &lt;10% of patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) AML will survive \geq 3 years (Brandwein et al, 2020).
L135: Nucleophosmin-1 (NPM1) is an essential driver of and the most commonly mutated gene in AML, occurring in approximately 30% (ie, 6240) of new adult AML cases in the US annually (Döhner et al, 2017; NCI, 2023). NPM1 mutation (NPM1-m) is also detected in 91% of NPM1-m

L150: 2.2. Analysis of Current Treatment Options
L151: The Applicant's Position:
L152: Therapeutic options are complex and largely rely on the patient's particular AML genomic subtype, age at diagnosis, overall performance status, access to standards of care, ability to tolerate the proposed treatment, and ultimate goals of therapy. Treatment of AML is initially divided into induction, consolidation, and postremission therapy. Hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) is currently the only potentially curative option for AML; however, HSCT is typically reserved for patients in remission who would clearly benefit from it as it is accompanied by a distinct set of life-threatening risks and complications (eg, graft-versus-host disease and higher nonrelapse mortality).
L153: With the goal of eradicating leukemic cells to achieve CR, induction therapy with intensive chemotherapy is the standard of care for newly diagnosed AML patients able to tolerate the treatment intensity. Other treatment options for patients in this category include lower doses of chemotherapy, targeted agents, or hypomentylating agents.
L154: For consolidation or postremission therapy, treatment consists of HSCT; additional chemotherapy; and/or targeted therapies, such as FLT3, IDH1, and IDH2 inhibitors, for specific forms of AML. L216: 5 Nonclinical Pharmacology/Toxicology
L217: 5.1. Executive Summary
L218: The FDA's Assessment:
L219: Ziftomenib is a menin inhibitor. Menin is a nuclear scaffold protein that is part of protein complexes that regulate gene transcription via chromatin remodeling. Menin binds directly to the N-terminal of mixed lineage leukemia lysine methyltransferase 2a (MLL [KMT2A]), and this interaction is involved in nucleophosmin 1(NPM1)-driven gene regulation. Ziftomenib binds to menin at the KMT2A binding site and disrupts the interaction between menin and KMT2A. The proposed indication for ziftomenib is for the treatment of adult patients with relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with a susceptible NPM1 mutation. The leukemogenic activity of mutant NPM1 is mediated by the interactions between menin and MLL[KMT2A]. Susceptible NPM1 mutations are defined as those that result in loss of the nucleolar localization signal and the insertion of a new nuclear export signal leading to the cytoplasmic accumulation of mutant NPM1 and leukemogenesis.
L220: Ziftomenib inhibited the interaction between recombinant menin and a KMT2A peptide (IC50 = 37 nM), suggesting that ziftomenib can inhibit the interaction between menin and KMT2A fusion proteins. In binding assays conducted to determine off-target binding, ziftomenib did not show high levels of activity against the targets evaluated under the conditions tested, suggesting a low potential for off-target activity in a physiologically relevant context.

Other relevant evaluations
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed an increased numbers and/or larger lysosomes containing phospholipid- like material (in myelinoid aggregates, lamellar, and/or multivesicular bodies) within the cytoplasm of hepatocytes and Kupffer cells of ziftomenib-treated dogs when compared with lysosomes of vehicle-treated control animals. These findings persisted at the end of the recovery period, with somewhat decreased size and incidence of lysosomes containing phospholipid material within hepatocytes and Kupffer cells indicating partial recovery (ie, fewer lysosomes containing nascent phospholipid material). Taken together, the increased incidence and magnitude of lysosomal accumulation of phospholipid material in high-dose animals are consistent with a diagnosis of phospholipidosis.


Toxicokinetics
Systemic exposure to ziftomenib and KO-516 (metabolite) was independent of sex. Mean Cmax and AUC0-24h values for ziftomenib increased with increasing dose in an approximately dose-proportional manner on Day 1. On Day 28, mean Cmax values for ziftomenib increased with increasing dose in a less-than-dose-proportional manner while AUC0-24h values for ziftomenib increased with increasing dose in an approximately dose-proportional manner.
Mean Cmax and AUC0-24h values for KO-516 increased with increasing dose in an approximately dose-proportional manner on Day 1 and in a less-than-dose-proportional manner on Day 28. Systemic exposure to ziftomenib and KO-516 generally appeared to increase following repeated administration of ziftomenib at 20 and 60 mg/kg/day with no change at 200 mg/kg/day. Systemic exposure (AUC0-24h) to KO-516 was approximately 19% to 35% of the systemic exposure to ziftomenib.
Combined-sex systemic exposure (Cmax and AUC0-24h) to ziftomenib at the HNSTD (60 mg/kg/day) on Day 28 was 1150 ng/mL and 22,500 ng*hr/mL, respectively. Corresponding values for the KO-516 metabolite were 290 ng/mL and 5600 ng*hr/mL, respectively.

L391: Toxicokinetics
Systemic exposure to ziftofenib and its 2 metabolites, KO-516 and KO-739, was generally similar between males and females. KO-516-to-ziftofenib ratios for overall daily exposure (in terms of AUC0-24) were 0.258 to 0.405 on both evaluation days. KO-739-to-ziftofenib ratios for overall daily exposure (AUC0-24) were 0.913 to 1.57 on Day 1 and 1.79 to 6.25 on Day 91. Exposure (Cmax and AUC0-24) to all 3 analytes generally increased with increasing dose from 6 to 60 mg/kg/day in a greater-than-dose-proportional manner in both sexes on both Days 1 and 91. Exposure to KO-516 was nearly or approximately dose proportional on Day 91, in terms of Cmax in males and both Cmax and AUC0-24 in females. There was no meaningful accumulation of ziftofenib or KO-516 in plasma on Day 91 relative to Day 1. However, accumulation of KO-739 in plasma was noted in both sexes on Day 91, as indicated by mean accumulation ratios ranging from 2.04 to 5.78.
Systemic exposure (mean Cmax and AUC0-24) to ziftofenib at the NOAEL/HNSTD (20 mg/kg/day) on Day 91 was 274 ng/mL and 5100 ng*hr/mL, respectively, in males and 271 ng/mL and 4930 ng*hr/mL, respectively, in females. Corresponding values for the KO-516 metabolite were 104 ng/mL and 1740 ng*hr/mL, respectively, in males and 90.2 ng/mL and 1550 ng*hr/mL, respectively, in females. Corresponding values for the KO-739 metabolite were 1510 ng/mL and 22,000 ng*hr/mL, respectively, in males and 1630 ng/mL and 24,300 ng*hr/mL, respectively, in females.

L406: In the previously reviewed 28-day repeat-dose toxicology study in mice (study number 00275510, reviewed under IND 142028), animals were treated twice daily by oral gavage with ziftemenib at doses 40, 120, or 400 mg/kg/day. L486: 8.3.5. Analysis of Submission-Specific Safety Issues
L487: 8.3.5.1. Differentiation Syndrome
L488: The Applicant's Position:
L489: Preclinical PK/pharmacodynamic studies revealed that ziftomenib induces terminal differentiation and apoptosis, mechanisms that can manifest as DS in clinical settings. As expected, ziftomenib-related DS was observed throughout all phases of Study KO-MEN-001.
L490: Consistent with other targeted agents for the treatment of AML, differentiation syndrome emerged as a risk in Study KO-MEN-001 (Phase 1 Part 1a) in 1 patient with R/R KMT2A-r AML in the 1000 mg cohort experienced Grade 4 DS and subsequently died secondarily due to cardiac arrest. In Phase 1 Part 1b of Study KO-MEN-001, 17 patients (32.1%; predominantly KMT2A-r) experienced ziftomenib-related DS, including a Grade 5 (fatal) event in a patient with R/R KMT2A-r AML at the 200 mg dose level. Following the Grade 5 event, the DS mitigation guidance was issued, and enrollment of patients with R/R KMT2A-r AML was eventually closed.
L491: A DS mitigation guidance was subsequently developed and included additional monitoring on Days 4 and 10 for evidence of rising white blood cell count with peripheral evidence of myeloid differentiation as well as coagulation and inflammatory markers. Monitoring for specific symptoms that may be possible signs of DS was detailed. Finally, sites were instructed to proceed with early ziftomenib interruption and institution of steroid therapy at the time of suspicion of a DS event. Following implementation of this DS guidance, no additional Grade 4 or 5 events were reported in either genotype.
L492: A DS identification algorithm based on Montesinos 2009 and updated with Norsworthy 2020 was developed. A DS dataset encompassing all confirmed DS and potential DS cases (identified using the algorithm based on Norsworthy 2020) is included as ‘adrsadj’ in the KO-MEN-001 study folder and discussed in the KO-MEN-001 CSR.
L493: Overall, in the 600 mg QD monotherapy NPM1-m R/R AML safety analysis population, 27 patients (24.1%) experienced a TEAE of DS, with a median time to the first event of DS being 15.0 days (range: 3 to 46 days). There were 16 patients (14.3%) who experienced an SAE of DS, of which all were considered related to ziftomenib treatment. Fifteen patients (13.4%) experienced an event of DS of a CTCAE Grade 3, all of which were considered related to ziftomenib treatment. The DS mitigation plan outlined in Study KO-MEN-001 included immediate treatment interruption for a suspected DS event; there were 15 (13.4%) patients who experienced a TEAE of DS that led to a ziftomenib dose interruption. Although these DS events were considered serious, most cases were manageable per the DS mitigation guidance, with only 2 (1.8%) patients discontinuing ziftomenib treatment due to DS, 1 patient (0.9%) experiencing an event of DS that led to a dose reduction, and no patients having a CTCAE Grade 4 or 5 DS event. No patients in the 600 mg QD monotherapy NPM1-m R/R AML safety analysis population experienced TEAEs of DS that led to death.

L501: 8.3.5.3. Drug-Induced Liver Injury
L502: Data:
L503: Based on findings in preclinical toxicology studies suggestive of adverse effects on the hepatobiliary system, drug-induced liver injury was a potential risk during the development of ziftomenib. Overall, observed hepatic changes were generally mild to moderate and well managed according to Study KO MEN 001 procedures.

L514: 8.3.5.4. Bleeding Events
L515: The Applicant’s Position:
L516: Bleeding events are frequently seen in patients with active AML due to underlying marrow dysfunction with associated thrombocytopenia. More common bleeding events, such as epistaxis, have been reported in 15.1% of patients receiving ivosidenib (DiNardo et al, 2018), 17.1% of patients receiving gilteritinib (Perl et al, 2019), and 26.6% of patients receiving revumenib monotherapy (Issa et al, 2023).

There were 14 patients (12.5%) who reported a TEAE of epistaxis, with 2 (1.8%) patients reporting a CTCAE Grade 3 event and none being considered related to ziftomenib treatment. L522: Thrombotic events occur in about 8% to 9% of patients with newly diagnosed nonacute promyelocytic leukemia (APL; Libourel et al, 2016). L523: Taken together, these data support that ziftomenib monotherapy does not increase bleeding and thrombotic events over what is observed in an AML population.
L524: 8.3.5.5. Febrile Neutropenia and Infection
L525: The Applicant's Position:
L526: Neutropenic fever occurs in up to 80% of patients receiving chemotherapy for hematological malignancies, including AML, with a risk of major complications of 25% to 30% and fatality rates of 11%, increasing up to 50% when accompanied with sepsis and/or septic shock (Taplitz et al, 2018). In the 600 mg QD monotherapy NPM1-m R/R AML population, febrile neutropenia was reported in 25 patients (22.3%) and 20 patients (17.9%) had events that were considered serious. Three patients (2.7%) experienced an event of febrile neutropenia that was considered related to ziftomenib treatment.
L527: Due to neutropenia and being immunocompromised from both disease and treatment, infectious complications are common in AML patients. Pneumonia is one of the more common events occurring in AML patients, reported in 37% of AML patients receiving palliative therapy and 13.7% of patients receiving induction therapy (Tober et al, 2023). For patients receiving monotherapy with gilteritinib or ivosidenib, pneumonia was reported in 17.5% and 14.7% of patients, respectively, with Grade \geq 3 pneumonia occurring in 11.8% and 11.6% of patients, respectively (Perl et al, 2019, DiNardo et al, 2018). In front-line combination therapy with venetoclax, 23% of patients developed pneumonia, including 20% with Grade \geq 3 (DiNardo et al, 2018). In the 600 mg QD monotherapy NPM1-m R/R AML population, 24 patients (21.4%) reported an event of pneumonia, with 17 (15.2%) patients having of an event of a CTCAE Grade \geq 3 and 12 patients (10.7%) reporting an SAE of pneumonia. None of the events of pneumonia were considered related to ziftomenib treatment. Additional details are provided in Section 2.1.9.3 of Module 2.7.4 (narrative portion of ISS).

L726: 14.4.4.2. Background
L727: Ziftomenib (also known as KO-539) is a menin-MLL (KMT2A) inhibitor, which is being developed for the use as monotherapy in the treatment of adult patients with relapsed or refractory acute lymphoblastic leukemia (R/R AML) with a nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) mutation. The proposed dosing regimens of ziftomenib is 600 mg once daily, taken fasted with water at least 1 hour before or 2 hours after a meal. The available dose strength is 200 mg capsule.
L728: Following oral administration of a single dose of 400 mg ziftomenib in healthy subjects under the fasted state, a median time to peak concentration (tmax) was approximately 3 hours (KO-MEN-003). Ziftomenib increased more than dose proportionally over the single-dose range of 50 mg to 1000 mg (Cycle 1 Day 1 in part 1a) and 200 mg to 800 mg once daily (Cycle 2 Day 1 in phase 1b) in patients with R/R AML (KO-MEN-001). High inter-patient coefficient of variation (CV)% was observed ranging from 89% to 191% in part 1a and from 89% to 243% in part 1b. Steady state was reached within 15 days. Administration of ziftomenib once daily resulted in approximately 5-fold accumulation in AUC0-12 at steady state. At the 600 mg dose, the mean steady-state ziftomenib Cmax was 800 ng/mL and AUC0-24 was 12200 ng/mL (KO-PK-0001). L825: 14.4.4.4.4. Effects of staggered dosing of proton pump inhibitors on the exposure of ziftenoben
L826: The Applicant proposed staggering dosing of a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) (administered 2-12 h after ziftenoben) to minimize its effect on decreasing ziftenoben exposure, as observed with rabeprazole (KO-MEN-003).
L827: The PBPK analyses indicate that staggered dosing would not help minimize the interaction between PPIs (e.g., rabeprazole and esomeprazole) and ziftenoben for the following reasons:
L828: • As illustrated in FDA Figure 13, at the Applicant's proposed ziftenoben administration time points, the gastric pH values (intersections of the blue vertical lines with the observed time courses of gastric pH on Day 7), are mostly above the pH value at which the effect of rabeprazole on ziftenoben was observed (intersection of the red vertical and horizontal lines). Therefore, PPIs are expected to have the same, if not greater, effects on ziftenoben exposure as that observed if ziftenoben is administered at these time points.

L831: Konzifti (ziftofenib)
L832: As shown in FDA Table 68, the predicted effects of esomeprazole on ziftofenib exposure were similar to the observed effects of rabeprozole on ziftofenib, when ziftofenib was administered 10, 12, and 14 h following multiple-dose administration of a PPI, indicating that staggered dosing did not help to minimize the interaction.
The effect of esomeprazole on ziftofenib exposure was predicted to be minimal when the PPI was administered 2 h after ziftofenib administration, or when ziftofenib was administered 22 h after PPI administration, as indicated in FDA Table 68. However, we note that the PBPK analyses underpredicted the observed effect of rabeprozole on ziftofenib AUC by approximately 34% following once daily dosing of 10 mg rabeprozole for 5 days (FDA Table 68). This underprediction may result from the lower simulated pH compared to the observed and the pKa value of ziftofenib (FDA Figure 13). Ziftofenib is a poorly soluble weak base with pKa values of 8.2 and 4.78. Its ionization properties around pH 4.78 are expected to significantly affect its pharmacokinetics. The dynamic pH model failed to capture the nocturnal rise of gastric pH, and the simulated pH values were below the observed gastric pH and/or the pKa value of ziftofenib after 12 h of PPI administration (FDA Figure 13). Therefore, the effects of esomeprazole on ziftofenib exposure may be also underestimated if ziftofenib is administered 14 h or 22 h after esomeprazole administration.
The ziftofenib PBPK model failed to describe the ziftofenib plasma concentration-time profiles and did not capture its absorption phase in the absence of CYP3A inhibitors in cancer patients (FDA Figure 12).
L833: Taken together, the PBPK analyses do not support staggered dosing of PPIs to minimize the effects of PPIs on ziftofenib exposure.
