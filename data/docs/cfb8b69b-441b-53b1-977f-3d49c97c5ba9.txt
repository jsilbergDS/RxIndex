L46: ## 1 Executive Summary
L47: ## 1.1. Product Introduction
L48: Caplacizumab (Cablivi®) is a new molecular entity (NME). Caplacizumab is an injectable (intravenous (IV) and subcutaneous (SC)) humanized bivalent anti-von Willebrand Factor (vWF) antibody fragment that consists of two identical building blocks, genetically linked by a three-alanine linker. Caplacizumab blocks the A-domain of vWF and inhibits the interaction between vWF and platelets, thereby reducing vWF-mediated platelet adhesion.
L49: The recommended indication is Cablivi is indicated for the treatment of adult patients with acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (aTTP), in combination with plasma exchange (PE) and immunosuppressive therapy. Caplacizumab is administered on the first day of treatment as an 11 mg IV injection prior to PE followed by an 11 mg SC injection after completion of PE on that day. On subsequent treatment days during PE caplacizumab is administered as a 11 mg SC injection following daily PE. Additional caplacizumab treatment is administered as a daily 11 mg SC injection for 30 days after the PE period has been completed.
L50: *Reviewer comment: In the clinical trial protocol, the starting dose of caplacizumab was specified as 10 mg intravenous injection (delivered by withdrawing and administering all of the reconstituted caplacizumab solution) given prior to the first plasma exchange, followed by a daily 10mg subcutaneous injection after completion of plasma exchange, for the duration of daily plasma exchange, and for 30 days thereafter. The recommended dose of caplacizumab of 11 mg at the same schedule, is based upon studies evaluating the actual dose that can be withdrawn from the vial which contains caplacizumab. Therefore, the mean dose delivered in the trial was actually 11 mg and this dose is incorporated into the caplacizumab product label for this marketing application. However, throughout the clinical and statistical portions and clinical summary of this written review the protocol-specified caplacizumab 10mg dose terminology is used unless otherwise stipulated.*

It is characterized by small-vessel platelet-rich thrombi that cause thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), bleeding and end organ damage. aTTP is a medical emergency that is almost always fatal if treatment is not initiated promptly. L77: ## 2 Therapeutic Context
L78: ## 2.1. Analysis of Condition
L79: Acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (aTTP) is a rare disease with an incidence of approximately three cases per one million adults per year. The annual incidence of aTTP is estimated to be 1 new case/million people in the US. (Joly, 2017) The disease appears to be more common among female than male patients (estimated female: male ratio 2-3:1). (Tsai, 2007). aTTP is a thrombotic microangiopathy caused by severely reduced activity of the von Willebrand factor (vWF)-cleaving protease ADAMTS13 (typically &lt;10% of normal activity) due to the presence of autoantibodies inhibiting ADAMTS13 activity. It is characterized by small-vessel platelet-rich thrombi that cause thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), bleeding and end organ damage. Bleeding occurs commonly among patients with aTTP. Signs of bleeding among patients with aTTP include bruising, petechiae, epistaxis, hematuria, menorrhagia, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage. (Blombery, 2014) Bleeding manifested as petechiae, purpura and epistaxis is commonly observed (&gt;50% of patients). However, major overt bleeding is less commonly observed (&lt;10% patients). (Griffin, 2013). aTTP is a medical emergency that is almost always fatal if treatment is not initiated promptly. (Reese, 2013) Caplacizumab appears to bind to the A1-domain of vWF and inhibits the interaction between vWF and platelets thereby reducing vWF-mediated platelet adhesion and platelet consumption in formation of microthrombi.
L80: ## 2.2. Analysis of Current Treatment Options
L81: There are no FDA approved products for the treatment of aTTP. Treatment for aTTP includes daily plasma exchange (PE) therapy until the patient's platelet counts have returned to baseline and there is no further evidence of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and end organ damage. For patients who do not respond to daily plasma exchange or whose initial disease presentation is considered severe, i.e., ADAMTS13 activity levels are below 10%, corticosteroids are generally added to the daily plasma exchange. Rituximab (off-label), and/or additional immunosuppression may be added to daily plasma exchange and corticosteroids and may include drugs such as cyclophosphamide, vincristine, or cyclosporine. Treatment for aTTP continues until the patient's platelet counts have returned to baseline and there is no further evidence of MAHA and end organ damage. With therapy the estimated mortality rate among patients with an acute aTTP episode is decreased to 10-20%. (George, 2010) Approximately 15 to 20 percent of patients have an exacerbation of the disease when plasma exchange is stopped. (Rock, 1991)

L143: ## 5 Nonclinical Pharmacology/Toxicology
L144: ## 5.1. Executive Summary
L145: The nonclinical development program for caplacizumab was conducted in various cellular assay systems and in the mouse, guinea pig, cynomolgus monkey, and baboon to evaluate the pharmacology, pharmacokinetics, general toxicology, and reproductive and developmental effects. Caplacizumab targets the A1-domain of von Willebrand factor and inhibits the interaction of platelets with von Willebrand factor. Von Willebrand factor is a glycoprotein involved in platelet activation and adhesion. Caplacizumab is a first-in-class product and has been assigned an established pharmacological class of “von Willebrand factor-directed antibody fragment”.
L146: Caplacizumab demonstrated a strong affinity for multimeric von Willebrand factor with an average K_D value of 3.76 pM. The biological activity of caplacizumab was characterized using platelet adhesion assays, platelet aggregation assays, the platelet function analyser (PFA)-100 test, and ELISA binding assays. Caplacizumab was shown to inhibit the interaction of von Willebrand factor with platelet receptor glycoprotein GPIb-IX-V in a concentration-dependent manner including the inhibition of platelet adhesion to collagen types I and III and immobilized von Willebrand factor at a high shear rate, inhibition of ristocetin-induced platelet aggregation, and the inhibition of ristocetin-induced binding of purified von Willebrand factor to human fixed platelets (IC_{50}~0.006 µg/mL). In the PFA-100 test, caplacizumab prolonged the closure time, which is a demonstration of the inhibition of platelet adhesion and aggregation. While ristocetin-induced aggregation was completely inhibited, caplacizumab did not affect platelet aggregation induced by adenosine diphosphate (ADP), epinephrine, collagen, or thrombin. Additionally, caplacizumab did not affect the interaction of von Willebrand factor with collagen type VI or ADAMTS-13 (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with a thrombospondin type 1 motif, member 13).
L147: In both in vitro and in vivo studies, the pharmacological activity of caplacizumab was assessed using the ristocetin cofactor activity assay (RICO), which measures the interaction of von Willebrand factor with the platelet receptor glycoprotein GPIb-IX-V and was used as a marker for the pharmacological activity. In plasma from healthy volunteers and patients with TTP, caplacizumab inhibited von Willebrand factor binding to human platelets in ELISA inhibition assays with IC_{50} values ranging from 161 to 936 ng/mL, and the concentration needed to completely suppress the RICO response (RICO activity &lt;20%) ranged between 0.1 and 1.6 µg/mL.
L148: The ability of caplacizumab to cross-react with von Willebrand factor in the plasma of various species was assessed using a surface plasmon resonance-based binding assay, binding and
L149: inhibition ELISA assays, and platelet aggregation and adhesion studies. Caplacizumab interacted with plasma von Willebrand factor of baboon, rhesus monkey, cynomolgus monkey, pig, mini-pig, and guinea pig, but did not interact with plasma von Willebrand Factor of mouse, rat, hamster, rabbit, or dog. Based on these results, the cynomolgus monkey and guinea pig were selected as the relevant species for toxicology studies of caplacizumab. Further cross-reactivity evaluations show that caplacizumab binds with a similar mode of action to cynomolgus monkey and guinea pig von Willebrand factor as compared to human von Willebrand factor. Additionally, the relative affinity of caplacizumab determined using the monovalent building block of caplacizumab (PMP12A2hum1) was similar for the cynomolgus monkey and human von Willebrand Factor A1 domains, but 10-fold less for guinea pig von Willebrand Factor A1 domain.
L150: Secondary pharmacology studies showed that caplacizumab does not affect the activity of ADAMTS-13 which binds to and cleaves the A2-domain of von Willebrand factor and does not affect the binding of von Willebrand factor to fibrillar collagens at the A3-domain. These results indicate that the binding and activity of caplacizumab appears to be specific to the A1-domain of von Willebrand factor. Another study demonstrated that caplacizumab does not interact with human blood cells. The bleeding risk associated with caplacizumab treatment was assessed in multiple bleeding models in baboons. While skin bleeding time was prolonged following injection/infusion of pharmacologically active doses of caplacizumab, the effect was shorter lasting for caplacizumab than for the controls.
L151: No specific safety pharmacology studies were conducted with caplacizumab; however, the assessment of safety pharmacology was conducted as part of the 13-week toxicology study in guinea pigs and the 2-week, 13-week, and 26-week toxicology studies in cynomolgus monkeys. No safety pharmacology findings of concern were reported in these studies at doses up to 40 mg/kg/day.
L152: An absorption study was conducted to compare the pharmacokinetics of caplacizumab administered subcutaneously (the route of administration for the general toxicology studies and one of the clinical routes of administration) and intramuscularly (the route administration for the reproductive and developmental toxicology studies) once daily for 30 days in female guinea pigs. The pharmacokinetic profiles were comparable following a single subcutaneous or intramuscular administration including the half-life (subcutaneous: 25.32 hours; intramuscular 19.58 hours), volume of distribution (subcutaneous: 509.9 mL/kg; intramuscular 680.4 mL/kg), and clearance (subcutaneous: 23.30 mL/hr/kg; intramuscular: 25.57 mL/hr/kg). The absorption was slightly greater with the intramuscular route (AUC_{last}=15.71 hr*\mug/mL) compared to the subcutaneous route (AUC_{last}=9.26 hr*\mug/mL). Accumulation of caplacizumab was observed after 10 days of administration in two animals in the subcutaneous group, leading to a clear difference between subcutaneous and intramuscular following repeated administration. In a distribution study in mice following a single dose of radiolabeled caplacizumab administered intravenously free or in complex with human von Willebrand factor, the caplacizumab-von Willebrand factor complex was cleared slowly via the liver, while caplacizumab alone was
L153: cleared rapidly via the kidneys. The presence of radiolabeled caplacizumab in other organs was low except for the stomach. These data indicate that caplacizumab is cleared together with von Willebrand factor via the liver and excess caplacizumab is cleared via the kidney.
L154: Repeat-dose toxicology studies were conducted to assess the chronic toxicity of caplacizumab. The studies were conducted using the subcutaneous route of administration, which is consistent with the intended clinical route of administration. In the 13-week study in Dunkin-Hartley guinea pigs, caplacizumab was administered by subcutaneous bolus injection at 0, 0.4, 4, or 40 mg/kg/day for 13 weeks with an 8-week recovery period. In the 26-week study in Cynomolgus monkeys, caplacizumab was administered by subcutaneous bolus injection at 0, 0.4, 4, or 40 mg/kg/day administered for 26 weeks with a 13-week recovery period. No caplacizumab-related mortality was observed in guinea pigs; however, one male monkey treated with 40 mg/kg/day was euthanized early on Day 85 due to signs of anemia and being at risk of spontaneous bleeding. This monkey had a marked subcutaneous encapsulated hematoma in the right thigh with an ongoing hemorrhage. In male guinea pigs treated with the highest dose of 10 mg/kg (40 mg/kg/day), body weight gain during the treatment period (Weeks 0 to 13) was decreased by 39% compared to controls. The levels of Factor VIII and von Willebrand factor were decreased at all doses of caplacizumab during the treatment period and a pharmacodynamic response was observed in the RICO assay in both species. In monkeys, hematology changes included decreases in red blood cell count, hemoglobin, pack cell volume, and platelets, increases in reticulocytes, and prolonged APTT. Clinical chemistry results showed that increases in globulin and decreases in albumin and albumin/globulin ratio were observed in both guinea pigs and monkeys, while total protein was increased in guinea pigs and total bilirubin was increased in monkeys.

In guinea pigs, the finding of hemorrhagic subcutaneous tissue was observed, and the incidence and severity increased with dose with a significant change for hemorrhage observed microscopically at localizations 1 and 2 in males treated with 40 mg/kg/day compared to controls. Injection site findings were more prominent in monkeys with macroscopic findings of firm areas, dark focus, or edematous areas that correlated with microscopic findings. While the changes at the injection sites are related to the repeated subcutaneous administration, caplacizumab appears to have induced an exacerbation of the inflammatory changes in monkeys.

L311: ## 6 Clinical Pharmacology
L312: ## 6.1. Executive Summary
L313: Caplacizumab is a bivalent monoclonal antibody that binds to the A1 domain of von Willebrand factor (vWF) and inhibits the interaction between vWF and platelets. Evidence of effectiveness is derived from the Phase II (ALX-0681-2.1/10) and Phase III (ALX0681-C301) studies of caplacizumab in patients with aTTP. In both studies, the primary endpoint of time to platelet response compared to placebo was significantly reduced in patients treated with caplacizumab. Treatment emergent adverse events (TEAEs) did not appear to differ across the range of concentrations observed in the clinical trials conducted in patients with aTTP. The proposed dosing regimen is 11 mg IV prior to plasma exchange on first day of treatment, 11 mg SC after plasma exchange on first day of treatment, and 11 mg SC following plasma exchange for the duration of plasma exchange and for 30 days after stopping plasma exchange. The proposed dosing regimen is acceptable from a clinical pharmacology perspective and no therapeutic individualization is recommended based on intrinsic or extrinsic factors. This BLA is approvable from a clinical pharmacology perspective.
L314: ## 6.2. Summary of Clinical Pharmacology Assessment
L315: ## 6.2.1. Pharmacology and Clinical Pharmacokinetics
L316: Caplacizumab is a 28 kilodalton (kDa) bivalent monoclonal antibody consisting of two building blocks joined by a tri-alanine linker. Caplacizumab binds to the A1 domain of von Willebrand factor (vWF) and inhibits the interaction between vWF and platelets, subsequently preventing the formation of microthrombi. The clearance of caplacizumab is mediated through a combination of saturable target-mediated clearance and linear clearance through renal excretion of the free drug; as such, pharmacokinetic profiles differ between healthy subjects and patients with aTTP who present with higher vWF concentrations.
L317: In studies in healthy subjects, the increase in AUC_{0-inf} and C_{max} with increasing dose was less than dose-proportional. In healthy subjects following a once daily 10 mg subcutaneous dose steady-state is reached after the first dose and minimal drug accumulation occurs.
L318: Caplacizumab was initially developed as a liquid formulation which was used in the phase 1 dose-finding studies and the phase 2 clinical trial of caplacizumab in aTTP patients (ALX-0681-2.1/10). A lyophilized formulation was subsequently developed and used in the phase 3 clinical trial in patients with aTTP (ALX0681-C301) and is the to-be-marketed formulation. The comparability of the two formulations was evaluated in study ALX0681-C102, a cross-over study in 24 healthy male subjects given a single 10 mg SC. The geometric mean ratio of the lyophilized formulation to the liquid formulation was 102% (90% CI [REDACTED](b) (4)) and 97.2% (90% CI [REDACTED](b) (4)) for AUC_{0-inf} and C_{max}, respectively, demonstrating PK comparability as the 90% CI for both parameters fell entirely

L325: ## **Metabolism**
L326: Following the injection of radiolabeled caplacizumab bound to human vWF in mice, radioactivity was primarily detected in the liver. This is consistent with the distribution of free vWF, which concentrates in the liver and is removed from circulation via endocytosis by macrophages (PMID: 25712991).
L327: Target-bound caplacizumab is expected to be catabolized by macrophages within the liver, in addition to systemic catabolism by ubiquitous proteolytic enzymes. Of note, higher levels of vWF:Ag, which are present in patients with aTTP, increase the fraction of drug-target complex retained in the circulation, making the clearance of total caplacizumab target level- and concentration-dependent.

L332: ## 6.2.2. General Dosing and Therapeutic Individualization
L333: ## **General Dosing**
L334: The proposed dosing regimen is:
L335: - 11 mg IV prior to plasma exchange on first day of treatment;
- 11 mg SC after plasma exchange on first day of treatment;
L336: - 11 mg SC daily following plasma exchange for the duration of plasma exchange and for 30 days after stopping plasma exchange.
L337: This dosing regimen was used in the Phase III (ALX0681-C301) study of caplacizumab in patients with aTTP. The initial PK studies of caplacizumab and the Phase II Study ALX-0681-2.1/10 evaluated 10 mg caplacizumab doses. However, the 11 mg dose is unlikely to result in clinically significant differences in caplacizumab efficacy and safety compared to previous studies (see Section 6.3.2). The proposed dosing regimen is acceptable from a clinical pharmacology perspective.
L338: ## Therapeutic Individualization
L339: No therapeutic individualization is recommended based on intrinsic or extrinsic factors. Baseline creatinine clearance and body weight were identified as a statistically significant covariates in the population PK model. Small increases in exposure were predicted with decreases in creatinine clearance or in patients with lower body weights, but these changes are not expected to be clinically significant; therefore, dose adjustment is not recommended in patients with renal impairment and a fixed dose was considered appropriate regardless of body weight.
L340: The effect of hepatic impairment on caplacizumab PK was not assessed and is unknown. Because patients with hepatic impairment are prone to coagulopathies, increased monitoring for bleeding is recommended in these patients. Similarly, since anticoagulant medications and caplacizumab may both increase risk for bleeding, increased monitoring for bleeding is recommended in patients co-administered agents such as vitamin K antagonists, direct oral anticoagulants, or high-dose heparin.

L350: ## 6.3.2. Clinical Pharmacology Questions
L351: ## Does the clinical pharmacology program provide supportive evidence of effectiveness?
L352: The proposed dosing regimen was used in the Phase III (ALX0681-C301) study of caplacizumab in patients with aTTP and a statistically significant reduction in the time to platelet count response (the primary endpoint) was observed. As such, the 11 mg dose is unlikely to result in clinically significant differences in caplacizumab efficacy and safety compared to that observed in the previous studies.

L393: ## *Hepatic Impairment*
L394: The impact of hepatic impairment on caplacizumab PK, efficacy, and safety has not been evaluated and there are no data informing the use of caplacizumab in patients with severe acute or chronic liver failure.
L395: Animal data suggest that caplacizumab appears to concentrate primarily in the liver, and to a lesser extent in the spleen and other organs. This is consistent with the distribution of free vWF, which concentrates in the liver and is removed from circulation via endocytosis by macrophages (PMID: 25712991, 23809124).
L396: Since the predominant clearance mechanism is via macrophages rather than hepatocytes, hepatic impairment is not expected to have a direct effect on the clearance of caplacizumab. However, patients with hepatic impairment are prone to coagulopathies. Given that bleeding is an adverse effect of caplacizumab, there is a potential for increased pharmacodynamic effect of caplacizumab, such as bleeding in patients with hepatic impairment. As such, close monitoring for bleeding is recommended if caplacizumab is administered to patients with hepatic impairment.

L422: ## Are there clinically relevant food-drug or drug-drug interactions, and what is the appropriate management strategy?
L423: No formal *in vitro* or *in vivo* drug-drug interaction studies were conducted for caplacizumab, either as a victim or a perpetrator. In ALX0681-C301, 35 of 72 (48.6%) caplacizumab-treated patients also received an antithrombotic agent during the overall study period. Therefore, there is a potential for additive or synergistic risk of bleeding events when using agents such as vitamin K antagonists, direct oral anticoagulants, or high-dose heparin with caplacizumab. Because of the potential for increased bleeding risk, close monitoring for bleeding is recommended when caplacizumab is administered with anticoagulant medications.

L438: ## 7.2. Review Strategy
L439: The pivotal study to establish the efficacy of caplacizumab is Study ALX-0681-C301, hereafter referred to as HERCULES; the efficacy review will focus on this study. Study ALX-0681-2.1/10, hereafter referred to as TITAN, will be reviewed briefly; however, it plays only a supportive role for the evaluation of the efficacy of caplacizumab for the treatment of patients with aTTP. The efficacy database for caplacizumab for this application consists of 72 patients in the caplacizumab treatment group and 73 patients in the placebo treatment group. Safety data from the HERCULES study and TITAN study are pooled to enhance the caplacizumab safety database. The safety data from the HERCULES and TITAN were pooled because the designs of the studies were similar, i.e., both studies were blinded, randomized, placebo controlled, multicenter studies conducted in patients with aTTP. The primary objective of both studies was to evaluate the platelet response. In both studies the dosing of caplacizumab or matching placebo was similar, i.e, in the HERCULES and TITAN studies caplacizumab or matching placebo was administered after initiation of plasma exchange (PE). Caplacizumab or placebo was administered as a 10 mg bolus intravenous injection at least 15 minutes prior to plasma exchange followed by a 10 mg subcutaneous injection after completion of plasma exchange on day 1. Subsequently caplacizumab 10 mg or placebo was administered subcutaneously once daily following plasma exchange. Treatment with caplacizumab 10mg or placebo administered subcutaneously was continued after the plasma exchange period for 30 days following the last daily plasma exchange. If after initial treatment course, ADAMTS13 levels remained detectable or there were other clinical signs of persistent underlying disease, e.g. elevated LDH or evidence of schistocytosis, then treatment with caplacizumab 10mg or placebo administered subcutaneously once daily was extended for 7 day intervals for a maximum of 28 days. The first caplacizumab dose in the TITAN study could also be administered prior to the initiation of plasma exchange on study. Also, in the HERCULES study the median duration of exposure to study drug was 35 days (range 1-65 days) for the caplacizumab treatment group and 23 days (range 2-66) days for the placebo treatment group during the double blind period treatment period. In the TITAN study the median duration of exposure was 36 days (range 3-77 days) for the caplacizumab treatment group and 37 days (range 2-90 days) for the placebo treatment group. Therefore the pooled safety database for caplacizumab consists of data from 106 patients treated with caplacizumab (71 patients from the HERCULES study and 35 patients from the TITAN study) and 110 patients treated with placebo (73 patients from the HERCULES study and 37 patients from the TITAN study) who were exposed to at least one dose of study drug. The HERCULES study and TITAN study designs and efficacy results are reviewed in detail in section 8.1 Review of Relevant Individual Trials Used to Support Efficacy in this review below. L568: ## Trial Design
L569: **Primary Objective.** The primary objective is to assess the reduction in time to platelet response.
L570: **Treatment Arms.** The treatment arms are
L571: - caplacizumab + SOC
- placebo + SOC
L572: where SOC is the standard of care which includes
L573: - daily PE
- adjunctive immunosuppressant treatment e.g.,corticosteroids
- rituximab
L574: TITAN is a randomized (1:1), Phase 2, multicenter, single-blind5, placebo-controlled study in patients with acute episodes of aTTP who require treatment with PE. The design schema is summarized in Figure 13. Adolescent subjects are not randomized and are treated with ALX-0081.
L575: 5 Patients are blinded but site staff are not. The reason is that the dosing regimen was dependent on the results of the RICO test following administration of the initial 10 mg dose. The results unblind the arms to the staff.
L576: Figure 13. TITAN design schema. SOURCE Excerpted from the Clinical Overview (Module 2).
L577: 1 Subjects were randomized prior to the start of PE treatment. After the approval and implementation of Clinical Study Protocol Version 12.0, a subject could be randomized after an initial PE session, in which case the next PE session was designated as the first PE on-study.
L578: 2 Daily PE treatment period
L579: 3 PE tapering: optional, per local standard site practice.
L580: 4 Study drug treatment period: covers the period from the date of first study drug administration till the date of the last study drug administration. L642: ## 8.1.6. Assessment of Efficacy
L643: The efficacy of caplacizumab for the treatment of adult patients with aTTP, in combination with plasma exchange and immunosuppressive therapy was demonstrated in the HERCULES trial. The TITAN trial is considered supportive of the efficacy of the proposed indication for caplacizumab. The safety data from the HERCULES and TITAN trials was pooled and is discussed in section 8.2 Review of Safety in this review below.
L644: The HERCULES trial was a phase 3, randomized, double blind, placebo controlled, multicenter trial conducted in the European Union (EU). In the HERCULES trial patients received a single 10mg caplacizumab bolus intravenous injection or placebo prior to the first plasma exchange on study. Patients continued treatment with daily plasma exchange and immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapy). Patients were treated with daily subcutaneous injections of caplacizumab 10mg or placebo after completion of plasma exchange, for the duration of the daily plasma exchange period and for 30 days thereafter. If after the initial treatment course, sign(s) of persistent underlying disease such as suppressed ADAMTS13 activity levels remained present, treatment was extended for 7 day intervals for a maximum of 28 days.
L645: In the HERCULES study there were 145 adult (age ≥18 years) patients with a diagnosis of aTTP and platelet count &lt;100,000/µL randomized in the study (72 caplacizumab treatment arm (71 treated), 73 patients placebo treatment arm (73 treated)). The primary endpoint of the study was time to platelet response, defined by platelet count ≥150,000/µL and cessation of daily plasma exchange within 5 days of satisfying the platelet threshold. The median time to platelet
L646: count response was 2.7 days (95%CI=1.9-2.8 days) in the caplacizumab treatment group compared to 2.9 days (95%CI=2.7-3.6 days) in the placebo treatment group (HR 1.6 (95%CI=1.1-2.2, p=0.01). However, in the HERCULES trial there were 3 (4%) patients treated with caplacizumab and 28 (38%) patients treated with placebo with an exacerbation of aTPP defined as thrombocytopenia after initial recovery of platelet count (platelet count \ge 150,000/\mu\text{L} with subsequent stop of daily plasma exchange within 5 days) that required reinitiation of daily plasma exchange during the 30-day post- daily plasma exchange period. There were 0 (0%) patients treated with caplacizumab compared to 3 (4%) patients treated with placebo whose death was related to aTPP. A similar proportion of patients between each treatment arm were reported to have at least one treatment-emergent major thromboembolic event (6 (9%) patients treated with caplacizumab compared to 6(8%) patients treated with placebo). The proportion of patients with a recurrence of aTPP in the overall study period (the drug treatment period plus the 28-day follow-up period after discontinuation of drug treatment) was significantly lower in the caplacizumab treatment arm (9/72 (13%) patients) compared to the placebo treatment arm (28/73 (38%) patients) (p&lt;0.001)).
L647: Although a decrease in the time to response in platelet count was observed among patients treated with caplacizumab compared to those patients treated with placebo, i.e., median time to platelet count response was 2.7 days (95%CI=1.9-2.8 days) in the caplacizumab treatment group compared to 2.9 days (95%CI=2.7-3.6 days) in the placebo treatment group (HR 1.6 (95%CI=1.1-2.2, p=0.01)) and is statistically significant and supports caplacizumab's efficacy, this result's clinical relevance is less important. From a clinical perspective the key benefit of caplacizumab for treatment of adult patients with aTPP, in combination with plasma exchange and immunosuppressive therapy is demonstrated that by the important secondary endpoints of the HERCULES study, i.e., the proportion of patients with aTPP related death, recurrence of aTPP or at least one treatment-emergent major thromboembolic event was significantly lower among in the caplacizumab treatment arm compared to the placebo treatment arm (9/72 (13%) patients in the caplacizumab group compared to 36/73 (49%) patients in the placebo group) (p&lt;0.0001). Also, the proportion of patients with a recurrence of aTPP in the overall study period was significantly lower in the caplacizumab group (9/72 (13%) patients) compared to the placebo group (28/73 (38%) patients) (p&lt;0.001)).
L648: ## 8.2. Review of Safety
L649: Acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (aTPP) is a rare and potentially fatal disease. (Joly, 2017) In order to enlarge the safety database for caplacizumab for the treatment of aTPP the safety data from the HERCULES (ALX0681-C301) trial was pooled with safety data from the TITAN (ALX-0681-2.1/10) trial. The designs of the TITAN study and HERCULES study are described in detail in sections 8.1.1 and 8.1.4, respectively, in this review above. The design of the TITAN study was a single-blind study consisting of a study drug treatment period and a follow-up period. The HERCULES study incorporated a double blind study drug treatment period, an open label treatment period in case of recurrence of aTPP on therapy, and a follow-
L650: up period. Therefore, the safety data from the TITAN single blind treatment period and the HERCULES double blind treatment period were pooled to enlarge the caplacizumab treatment safety database and better isolate the safety concerns for caplacizumab.
L651: ## 8.2.1. Safety Review Approach
L652: The safety of caplacizumab was evaluated in two placebo-controlled clinical studies HERCULES and TITAN. In the HERCULES trial 71 patients received caplacizumab and 73 patients received placebo. In the TITAN trial 35 patients received caplacizumab and 37 patients received placebo. Therefore, the safety data reflect exposure to study drug during the blinded periods of both studies which includes 106 patients with aTTP who received at least one dose of caplacizumab and 110 patients with aTTP who received at least one dose of placebo.

The patient was diagnosed with aTTP and was started on plasma exchange and corticosteroid therapy. Caplacizumab was administered per protocol. Other ischemic areas were visible in the right occipital seat and in the nuclei of the right base. L801: I concur with the recommendation above to approve caplacizumab, a biologic and new molecular entity. Caplacizumab is a von Willebrand factor (vWF)-directed antibody fragment for the treatment of adult patients with acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (aTTP), in combination with plasma exchange and immunosuppressive therapy. The product is the first ever approved by the FDA for the treatment of aTTP. aTTP is a life-threatening and potentially fatal disorder as a consequence of ADAMTS13 deficiency. Caplacizumab works by blocking the A-domain of vWF which inhibits the interaction between vWF and platelets. Thereby reducing vWF-mediated platelet adhesion and interfering with clot formation and propagation. Plasma exchange and immunosuppressive agents suppress production of any ADAMTS13 autoantibodies which perpetuate the ADAMTS13 deficiency.
L802: Clinical benefit was established in the HERCULES trial with acceptable safety. The TITAN trial provided supportive evidence. This approval will change the way patients with aTTP are treated because of its novel mechanism of action which adds to the standard treatment of plasma exchange and immunosuppression. If used promptly caplacizumab use may improve the outcomes for these patients, particularly the morbidity/mortality.

L829: Caplacizumab (ALX-0081) is a Nanobody approximately 28 kilodalton (kDa) in size consisting of two identical, humanized building blocks (PMP12A2hum1), joined by a tri-alanine linker. Caplacizumab binds to the A1-domain of von Willebrand factor (vWF) and specifically inhibits the interaction between vWF and platelets.
L830: Acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (aTTP) results in the impaired processing of ultra-large von Willebrand factor (ULvWF) multimers. In ULvWF multimers, the platelet-binding A1 domain interacts spontaneously with platelets, resulting in platelet adhesion and the formation of long platelet strings and microthrombi, which is responsible for the significant mortality and morbidity associated with this disease.
L831: The dosage form of caplacizumab is lyophilized powder in single-use vials containing 10 mg per vial. The following dosing regimen was used.
L832: - Patients with aTTP received a 10 mg i.v. bolus of caplacizumab prior to the first study-associated plasma exchange, to achieve immediate neutralization of vWF.
- Thereafter, 10 mg caplacizumab was administered daily via s.c. administration after each plasma exchange, to maintain neutralization of vWF and allow faster normalization of platelet counts.
L833: - Post-daily plasma exchange, patients received daily s.c. administration of 10 mg caplacizumab for at least 30 days, to maintain neutralization of vWF and to prevent exacerbations.
L834: The cross-discipline Office of Clinical Pharmacology (OCP) review team held a scoping meeting to gain input on the following key questions:
L835: - Is the proposed dosing regimen supported by the clinical pharmacology data?
- Is dose individualization necessary based on intrinsic factors?
- What is the incidence of the formation of anti-drug antibodies (ADA) and what is the effect on PK/PD and efficacy?

L875: ## Base model
L876: vWF is a multimeric plasma protein that plays a pivotal role in hemostasis and thrombosis by recruiting and activating platelets at sites of vascular injury. Therefore, vWF acts as a molecular bridge between the subendothelial collagen and the platelet receptor GPIb-IX-V complex. The bivalent Nanobody caplacizumab specifically blocks the interaction of vWF with the platelet receptor GPIb-IX-V, through binding of the vWF A1 domain. Therefore, caplacizumab prevents microthrombi formation, which makes it a promising drug in the treatment of TTP.
L877: The population PK/PD model of the interaction between caplacizumab and vWF in different adult populations following i.v and s.c administration was adequately described by a full targeted mediated drug disposition model. The model included a two-compartment drug disposition model with first-order linear elimination of free drug and two parallel first-order absorption processes following s.c dosing of caplacizumab. The model described the formation of drug-vWF complexes with the ability to form both dimers and trimers. The production, maturation and release of vWF were described by transit compartments and a vWF pool with feedback effects stimulating the production and release of vWF when vWF decreased below the subjects baseline level. For aTTP patients, disease progression and effects governed by plasma exchange (PE) treatment were adequately captured by i) a disease progression model dependent on time and ii) removal of free vWF, free drug and drug-vWF complex by PE. The TMDD model used for considering the active TTP disease status and the PE procedure, is illustrated in Figure 1.
L878: Body weight was allometrically included in the model with fixed exponents (0.75 or 1) for clearance (CL), intercompartmental clearance (Q), central volume of distribution (Vc) and peripheral volume of distribution (Vp). Covariate effects were evaluated based on graphical evaluation by means of stratified prediction corrected visual predictive checks (pcVPCs) and univariate evaluation. Age, sex, race, blood group, concomitant treatment (anticoagulants, immunosuppressants or antibiotics) and ADA class (any ADA, pre-existing ADA, treatment-emergent ADA) did not affect the PK and PD profiles. However, the number of subjects with especially treatment emergent anti-drug antibodies was low and hence this does not rule out a true effect of anti-drug antibodies on the PK. Only baseline CRCL was identified as a statistically significant covariate (although estimated with a high relative standard error (RSE) of 46%) on CL, with a minor reduction in CL for patients with CRCL below the median CRCL (100 ml/min) in aTTP patients.
L879: Inter-individual variability (IIV) was modelled assuming a log-normal distribution for patient level random effects. Residual variability was tested as additive, proportional or both on the dependent variable. Additive models on In-transformed dependent variable were investigated as well. Model evaluation and selection of the base model were based on standard statistical criteria of goodness-of-fit such as a decrease in the minimum objective function value (OFV),
L880: accuracy of parameter estimation (i.e., 95% confidence interval excluding 0), successful model convergence, and diagnostic plots.

L884: ## Covariate analysis
L885: Given the complexity of the model and long run times, a traditional covariate model building approach was not feasible. Covariates were included and evaluated for the aTTP model parameters based on prior knowledge and/or special concern. For highly correlated covariates, the most clinical relevant covariate was evaluated and thereby was BMI not evaluated.
L886: For the baseline covariates where no clinical relevant effects were expected, only a graphical evaluation was performed by means of stratified prediction corrected visual predictive checks (pcVPCs). If the pcVPCs illustrated an adequate predictive performance over the entire range of the covariate it indicated that no strong (clinical relevant) covariate relationship had been overlooked. Stratified pcVPCs were also created to confirm the correct inclusion or absence of an effect when the covariate had formally been evaluated. Covariate effects assessed by
L887: pcVPCs were: age, sex, CRCL, race, and blood group. Note that CRCL&gt;120 was capped as 120 for covariate effect analysis.
L888: For body weight a potential deviation from allometry was evaluated by estimation of the exponents for the body weight effect on CL, Vc, Q and Vp, respectively.
L889: Continuous covariates relationships were generally coded as power models and categorical covariates were generally coded as a fractional difference to the most common category both added as additive effects on the log-transformed of the typical parameter.

A MaxiSorp plate is coated with an anti-ALX-0081 (caplacizumab) mouse monoclonal antibody after which it is incubated with blocking buffer. During the blocking step, calibration, QC and validation samples are incubated with acetic acid to avoid target interference. The acid is neutralized with neutralization buffer. After a washing step of the coated MaxiSorp plate, calibration, QC and validation samples are transferred to the plate. During this incubation step, ALX-0081, present in the samples, is allowed to bind to the coated anti-ALX-0081 mouse monoclonal antibody. After a washing step, a biotinylated bivalent anti-ALX-0081 Nanobody is incubated to detect ALX-0081 captured onto the plate. Following a wash step to remove unbound biotinylated anti-ALX-0081, streptavidin-HRP is added to the wells. Following a wash step to remove unbound streptavidin-HRP, TMB substrate is added to the wells and color develops in proportion to the amount of ALX-0081. The reaction is stopped with HCl and the absorbance measured.

L981: ## Pharmacodynamic Assays
L982: The primary pharmacodynamic assays used to assess caplacizumab activity were the ristocetin-induced platelet aggregation (RIPA) and ristocetin-induced cofactor activity (RICO). Both assays are *in vitro* assays based on addition of ristocetin to plasma in the presence of platelets causing platelet aggregation, which is inhibited in the presence of caplacizumab. The RIPA assay measures the change in turbidity of test samples of platelet rich plasma following addition of ristocetin whereas the RICO assay measures change in turbidity after addition of ristocetin in test samples of platelet poor plasma added to lyophilized platelets; according to the Applicant the two assays yield equivalent results.
L983: In study ALX-0081-1.2/08, the RIPA and RICO method implemented at the study site was used according to the respective standard operating procedure of the individual study site. The RIPA assay used in ALX-0081-01/06 was validated and performed at [REDACTED] (b) (4). The RICO assay was qualified or validated and performed at multiple locations throughout caplacizumab development (b) (4).
L984: vWF:Ag concentrations were quantified as a biomarker of caplacizumab's effect on target disposition. vWF:Ag is quantified using an immunoturbidimetric assay that captures vWF:Ag present in plasma using polyclonal anti-vWF antisera and subsequently detected using HRP-labeled polyclonal anti-vWF antisera after which binding was visualized via a colorimetric reaction. According to the Applicant, these diagnostic kits suffer from drug interference
L985: (underestimation of vWF:Ag levels in individual plasma samples spiked with caplacizumab); therefore, the methodology was adapted through spiking the diluent used to dilute calibrators, quality control (QC) and patient samples with an excess of caplacizumab. The rationale is that all available binding sites of vWF in both calibrators and QC samples, as well as in patient samples already containing caplacizumab, are saturated with and therefore similarly affected by caplacizumab.
