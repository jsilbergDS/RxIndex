L21: ## I. Executive Summary
L22: ## 1. Summary of Regulatory Action
L23: The available data are adequate to support the approval of triheptanoin as “a source of calories and fatty acids for the treatment of pediatric and adult patients with molecularly confirmed long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (LC-FAOD).” However, the available data are not adequate to support the Applicant’s proposed claims that triheptanoin reduces major clinical events (MCEs) (b) (4). The data also do not support the Applicant’s requests for a priority review or a pediatric priority review voucher.
L24: Evidence of efficacy for triheptanoin as a source of calories and fatty acids is based upon one clinical investigation supported by the available scientific knowledge about medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs). The review team concluded that the Gillingham et al. trial was adequate and well-controlled to support the more limited indication of triheptanoin as a source of calories and fatty acids (Gillingham et al. 2017).
L25: Evidence of safety is derived from studies UX007-CL201/UX007-CL202 and is supported by expanded access narratives.
L26: The review team concludes that the benefits of a pharmaceutical grade source of calories and fatty acids outweigh the risks of gastrointestinal symptoms in patients with LC-FAOD. The review team exercised considerable regulatory flexibility in reaching this conclusion. First, the Gillingham trial was underpowered to establish true noninferiority between MCT oil and triheptanoin. Second, knowledge regarding the use of MCTs in the treatment of LC-FAOD is limited, as the use of MCT oil has not been studied in randomized trials apart from the Gillingham trial. The uncertainty regarding benefit is acceptable in this case because of the serious, life-threatening nature of the disease with no current FDA-approved treatment options, and because the uncertainty is balanced by acceptable certainty regarding the safety profile.
L27: The review team concluded that it would be misleading to rely on study UX007-CL201 to claim that triheptanoin reduces major clinical events (b) (4).
L28: as study UX007-CL201 was neither adequately conducted nor well-controlled for these purposes. The study did not use a design that permits a valid comparison with a control; did not employ a method of assigning patients to treatment and control groups such as randomization that minimizes bias; and did not take adequate measures such as blinding to minimize bias on the part of the subjects, observers, and analysts of the data. Furthermore, the definitions for clinical events used to assess the subjects’ response to treatment were vague and not well-defined and reliable, and it was unclear whether they were applied consistently across the retrospective and prospective study periods.
L29: FDA does not find it acceptable to rely on the open-label, single-arm design chosen for study UX007-CL201 when designs providing more scientific certainty are potentially feasible.1 Acceptance of these study designs imposes implications for the next generation of drug development, payer support, and patient access. It is possible that triheptanoin reduces major clinical events (b)(4) but this is a hypothesis that awaits further testing in adequate and well-controlled trials.
L30: The initial review of the new drug application (NDA) for triheptanoin was notable for robust disagreement within the review team regarding whether there was sufficient evidence of efficacy to support filing an NDA given the concerns for bias in study UX007-CL201 and the resulting uncertainty regarding clinical benefit. Drs. Zand, Donohue, Tracy, Russek-Cohen, and Johnson recommended a refuse-to-file action because study UX007-CL201 was not adequate and well-controlled, recommending that the Applicant should have performed a randomized study to compare the occurrence of clinical events in patients receiving triheptanoin to a control group (e.g., patients receiving MCT oil) as originally planned.
L31: The Division and Office Directors, Drs. Roman and Beitz, favored filing the NDA for review and consideration of a more limited nutritional indication for triheptanoin, i.e., as a source of calories and fatty acids. Ultimately, the review team reached consensus that the data from the randomized Gillingham (2017) trial were adequate to support a recommendation for approval for the more limited nutritional indication. Reaching this conclusion regarding efficacy required taking the unusual step of relying exclusively on the efficacy data from the randomized Gillingham study, which was conducted by an academic investigator, funded by a grant from the FDA's Office of Orphan Products Development, and for which the Applicant obtained right of reference, rather than the data collected by the Applicant directly in study UX007-CL201. Since the results from study UX007-CL201 could not be relied upon to support efficacy, the Applicant's requests for Priority Review and a Pediatric Priority Rare Disease Voucher could not be supported. See Section III.12 for the full regulatory history.

L37: Dimension	Evidence and Uncertainties	Conclusions and Reasons
Analysis of Condition	Long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (LC-FAOD) are a group of rare, autosomal recessive disorders caused by defects in nuclear genes that encode mitochondrial enzymes important for the conversion of dietary long-chain fatty acids into energy. Patients with pathogenic variants in these genes are at high risk for catabolism, which can be life-threatening. Clinically, catabolism can manifest as acute rhabdomyolysis, hypoglycemia, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Some patients have more chronic symptoms of pain and fatigue from subacute rhabdomyolysis and progressive cardiomyopathy.
In a January 2019 type C patient engagement meeting, patients shared with the Division that symptoms of fatigue, muscle cramping/muscle pain, and the challenges of living with a chronic disease with recurrent clinical symptoms are the symptoms they find most burdensome and significantly impact their quality of life both physically and emotionally.2
LC-FAOD are serious and life-threatening diseases.
L38: 2 DARRTS; NDA 213687, Section 1.6.3; Type CLC-FAOD Patient Engagement Meeting, January 9, 2019
L39: Dimension	Evidence and Uncertainties	Conclusions and Reasons
Current Treatment Options	The standard of care for patients with LC-FAOD is careful dietary management under the guidance of dieticians who regularly adjust patients' diets based on age, disease severity, and overall caloric requirements.
Medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil is the most commonly used source of medium-chain triglyceride for LC-FAOD patients. As a dietary supplement, the percentage of medium-chain triglycerides (C6, C8, C10, or C12) in each product is not regulated and thus varies. The triglyceride thought to be most useful as an energy source is C8, the percentage of which may vary from 40% to 70% in marketed MCT oils.3 Patients appear to have different levels of tolerability to MCT oil, which may also impact adherence. Gastrointestinal side effects are common.
The degree to which MCT oil tolerability and dietary adherence may reduce the risk of metabolic events due to catabolism is unknown, as neither have ever been studied systematically in randomized trials. Since identification of the underlying genetic defects in 1970, physicians have recommended that patients increase their intake of MCTs with MCT oil, which they can metabolize, and decrease the intake of the long-chain fatty acids that they cannot metabolize (Engel et al. 1970).
Despite careful adherence to dietary recommendations and MCT oil supplementation, many patients still are hospitalized with metabolic events due to underlying catabolism.2
Many patients with LC-FAOD have life-threatening metabolic events, due to catabolism, despite adherence to available therapies such as dietary management and MCT oil supplementation. There are no FDA-approved therapies for LC-FAOD.

L41: Dimension	Evidence and Uncertainties	Conclusions and Reasons
Benefit	Triheptanoin is an MCT oil that provides 8.3 kcal/mL.
Gillingham et al. (2017) performed a double-blind trial in patients age 7 years to 64 years with mild to moderate LC-FAOD and randomized them to triheptanoin or trioctanoin at a dose of 20% of daily caloric intake (DCI). After 4 months, no clinically meaningful differences were observed between the two treatment groups in metabolic hospitalizations, cardiac function on resting echocardiogram and treadmill ergometry, or metabolic biomarkers including glucose, insulin, lactate, total serum ketones, acylcarnitines, or serum-free fatty acid concentrations.
The Applicant performed an open-label, single-arm study, UX007-CL201, in 29 patients age 11 months to 59 years with moderate to severe LC-FAOD, and treated them with triheptanoin at a dose of up to 35% of daily caloric intake. Patients were followed prospectively for 78 weeks for metabolic hospitalizations, and this was compared to the event rate in a retrospective chart review. However, records regarding dietary management during the retrospective and prospective periods were sparse, and what data are available indicate that patients' diets improved between the two periods. No measures were taken to minimize this or other important potential sources of bias in this nonrandomized study.
The available chemistry data are adequate to support the efficacy of triheptanoin as a source of calories and fatty acids.
The lack of clinically meaningful differences observed by Gillingham et al. after 4 months of treatment with triheptanoin versus trioctanoin in LC-FAOD patients provides adequate qualitative evidence of efficacy to support the use of triheptanoin as an alternative source of MCT oil in patients with LC-FAOD. While this research study was designed with an exploratory primary endpoint, the Division found the direct comparison of the clinical course of LC-FAOD patients randomized to C8 or triheptanoin (C7) was clinically meaningful. Although purified C8 is not available for commercial use, it is a component of all MCT oils, the current standard of care for dietary management. The degree of uncertainty inherent in this qualitative assessment of benefit is acceptable as no randomized studies of MCT oil versus placebo are available.
The available data are not adequate to support the Applicant's proposed claims that triheptanoin reduces the frequency and duration of hospitalizations for major clinical events because the study was not well-controlled. Study UX007-CL201 did not use a design that permits a valid comparison with a control, did not employ a method of assigning patients to treatment and control groups that minimizes bias, did not take adequate measures to minimize bias on the part of the subjects, observers and analysts of the data, nor did it assess the subjects' response with well-defined and reliable methods.
L42: Dimension	Evidence and Uncertainties	Conclusions and Reasons
Risk and Risk Management	The safety database included 79 patients enrolled in studies UX007-CL201 and UX007-CL202. Patients enrolled in Gillingham et al. were not included in the safety database due to the limitations of information available. Available safety data on 67 patients in expanded access INDs from birth through adulthood were reviewed. As the expanded access IND safety data were limited, it was included descriptively to support the findings from the 79 patients enrolled in studies UX007-CL201/UX007-CL202. These patients aged from 0.3 to 64 years of age and were exposed for a median duration of 22.8 months (Range of 0.1 to 41.3 months).
Adverse reactions reported in at least 5% of triheptanoin treated patients in studies UX007-CL201 and UX007-CL202 included gastroenteritis, gastrointestinal disorder, vomiting and diarrhea.
The available safety profiles from the LC-FAOD expanded access programs and Gillingham et al. were less detailed and described patients on a greater range of doses of triheptanoin, but the adverse event profile observed was similar to what was seen in studies UX007-CL201 and UX007-CL202.
Triheptanoin adheres to gastrostomy tubes (G-tubes) made of polyvinyl chloride (PVC).
Triheptanoin degrades polystyrene.
LC-FAOD patients who are catabolic can experience gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, emesis and diarrhea. These GI symptoms may also represent adverse events in a high-fat diet, including the diet recommended with triheptanoin in LC-FAOD patients.
In general, there is limited information on the management of pregnancy with maternal LC-FAOD. No pregnancies were observed during the studies or expanded access INDs reviewed, so the long-term impact on fetal development is unknown.
Overall, the safety evaluation is adequate to assess the safety of triheptanoin in patients with LC-FAOD over a dosage range of 25% to 35% DCI. The safety profile is well-characterized and none of the identified safety issues would preclude approval of triheptanoin.
Labeling Warnings and Precautions (section 5.1) alert concerns with use of gastrostomy tubes (G-tubes) containing polyvinyl chloride and recommend routine monitoring of G-tube function.
As a patient's gastrointestinal symptoms can represent adverse events to triheptanoin or catabolism, product labeling recommends evaluation by a metabolic specialist familiar with LC-FAOD.
The impact of use of a synthetic MCT during pregnancy is unknown, and unable to be predicted from the available nonclinical data. Therefore, the Applicant will complete a 10-year pregnancy study to evaluate fetal risk.
L43: ## Conclusions Regarding Benefit-Risk
L44: Patients diagnosed with LC-FAOD are at continual risk for life-threatening episodes of catabolism, which can manifest as rhabdomyolysis, cardiomyopathy, and/or hypoglycemia. No drugs or biologics have been approved for the treatment of LC-FAOD, and current management is reliant upon dietary modification directed toward decreased ingestion of long-chain fatty acids, supplementation of medium-chain fatty acids, and sufficient intake of both calories and protein. The most commonly used dietary MCT is MCT oil, and the composition of the oil is variable, as it is not regulated in production.
L45: A randomized, double-blind trial of 4 months compared purified trioctanoin (C8) to triheptanoin (C7) in patients clinically diagnosed with mild LC-FAOD. Patients were dosed at 20% daily caloric intake (DCI) of dietary C8 or C7. Although there were no clinical or laboratory differences between study arms, the study did demonstrate that C7 is a source of calories and fatty acids that can be used to treat LC-FAOD patients without inducing catabolism.
L46: Interpretation of efficacy in an additional 78-week open-label study was impacted by concurrent dietary changes. Therefore, this study was primarily used to determine the long-term safety of triheptanoin in the treatment of severe LC-FAOD patients. Patients received triheptanoin at 25% to 35% DCI. Clinical events associated with catabolism (rhabdomyolysis, cardiomyopathy, hypoglycemia) did not worsen during the study and triheptanoin generally was well tolerated despite the anticipated gastrointestinal adverse events.
L47: Expanded access (EA) narratives also were reviewed. The narratives included 11 molecularly confirmed patients who initiated triheptanoin from birth to 12 months after they were diagnosed with cardiomyopathy related to their LC-FAOD. The narratives did not contain details about diet composition, caloric intake from intravenous fluids, or concomitant medications. However, upon review of the information provided in the narratives, we conclude that triheptanoin is generally tolerated in LC-FAOD patients from birth through adulthood.
L48: With all factors considered, the benefits of triheptanoin as a source of calories outweigh the risks for adverse events and will provide a controlled source of dietary medium-chain triglycerides for the treatment of LC-FAOD.

L56: ## Description of Clinical Condition
L57: Patients diagnosed with LC-FAOD are unable to use long-chain fat obtained from diet or catabolism to create tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle substrates (also called anaplerotic substrates). Specifically, this group of autosomal recessive disorders is caused by defects in nuclear genes that encode mitochondrial enzymes important for the creation of anaplerotic substrates. These enzymes transport long-chain fatty acids across the mitochondrial membrane. Ultimately, long-chain fats are metabolized into two-carbon aceto-acetate and converted into energy via the TCA cycle.
L58: In patients diagnosed with LC-FAOD, partial or incomplete oxidation of fatty acids leads to accumulation of potentially toxic fatty acid intermediates, a reduction of anaplerotic substrates and impaired gluconeogenesis. Patients who are unable to metabolize long-chain fatty acids may experience acute life-threatening metabolic crises during times of increased energy demand, such as growth, common infection or moderate exercise. These metabolic crises are caused by catabolism. For the sake of clarity in this review, the term “metabolic crises” refers to metabolic crises due to catabolism. In LC-FAOD patients, these crises most commonly manifest as acute rhabdomyolysis, hypoglycemia, and/or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and can be precipitous, resulting in life-threatening and rapid deterioration that requires emergency medical management. Patients may also demonstrate chronic symptoms of subacute rhabdomyolysis and progressive cardiomyopathy.
L59: ## Current Standard of Care
L60: LC-FAOD are a group of inborn errors of metabolism. There are no approved drugs for the treatment of LC-FAOD. Current management is dependent upon dietary modification with a low-fat diet, limitation of ingestion of long-chain fatty acids, and dietary ingestion of MCTs, naturally occurring fat that is not impacted by these metabolic defects. The most commonly used formulation for MCT is as an oil, although infant formulations also contain MCT supplementation.
L61: The most recent dietary guidelines recommend levels of MCT in diet that are stratified by patient age and disease severity (Southeast Regional Genetics Network 2019a) noted in Table 3. Disease severity is assessed by the frequency and severity of metabolic crises, and dietary sufficiency is commonly assessed by multiple factors, including the number of interim metabolic crises since the last assessment, individual patient growth, and biochemical analyses of macronutrient intake (i.e., creatine kinase levels and plasma amino acids).

L67: MCT oil is the most commonly ingested dietary supplement for patients with LC-FAOD. It is a natural mixture of C6, C8, C10, and C12 triglycerides. L68: Regardless of disease severity, patients with LC-FAOD appear to have different levels of adherence and/or tolerability to MCT oil, compounding any underlying risk for catabolism and metabolic crises. Patients with LC-FAOD came for a type C patient engagement meeting on January 9, 2019, to share their own symptoms with the Agency. Fatigue, lethargy, muscle cramps, muscle pain, and difficulty with cognitive “focus” were common symptoms they reported despite diets containing MCT oil. At times, these symptoms were a prodrome to the more severe manifestations of rhabdomyolysis, hypoglycemia and cardiomyopathy. In addition, patients shared about the emotional burden of having a chronic disease with daily management challenges.
L69: As there are no currently approved drugs for the treatment of LC-FAOD, the goal of dietary management is to provide sufficient caloric intake, to limit dietary intake of long-chain and very long-chain fat, and to provide additional sources of medium-chain fat. The rationale for use of medium-chain fat supplementation is that this fat will bypass the need to metabolize longer-chain
L70: fats, which require the specific enzymatic machinery upset by the pathophysiology in LC-FAOD patients.
L71: ## Regulatory History
L72: The regulatory history of this product is complex and notable for robust disagreement among members of the review team.
L73: Briefly, in 2017, the Agency denied breakthrough designation on the basis of results from the open-label, single-arm study UX007-CL201, citing concerns about potential confounding from concomitant dietary changes, and recommending a controlled study. No additional studies were performed, and 91 patients were treated under expanded access INDs as the Applicant sought unsuccessfully to persuade the Division to accept an NDA based on the results of study UX007-CL201.
L74: Following a request for reconsideration by Ultragenyx, Dr. Beitz accepted the Applicant's proposal to submit an NDA based on the data from study UX007-CL201 and the Gillingham trial. The Applicant submitted the NDA on July 31, 2019, seeking a priority review, a pediatric priority review voucher, and a claim for a reduction in major clinical events based on the results of study UX007-CL201. The clinical and statistical reviewers (Drs. Zand, Donohue, Tracy, Russek-Cohen, and Johnson) recommended a refuse-to-file action, noting that study UX007-CL201 was not an adequate and well-controlled trial. Dr. Beitz acknowledged the limitations of study UX007-CL201 and the recommendations of the clinical and statistical reviewers. However, Dr. Beitz concluded that the available evidence appeared sufficient to file the NDA under a standard review clock for a more limited claim as an alternative source of calories and medium-chain triglycerides. Ultimately, the review team concluded that the evidence from Gillingham (2017) was sufficient to support a recommendation for approval for this more limited nutrition claim.
L75: The FDA is aware that triheptanoin could be categorized as a medical food. However, if a product meets the definition of two or more product categories under the FD&amp;C Act, the manufacturer or Applicant can decide the category under which to market the product, and the Applicant chose to market triheptanoin as a drug.
L76: Triheptanoin was granted orphan drug designation (ODD #154740) April 15, 2015, Fast Track designation March 7, 2019, and rare pediatric disease designation (#RPD-2019-208) March 28, 2019.

L91: ## Determination of Efficacy
L92: The Gillingham (2017) trial was considered adequate, well-controlled, and the most interpretable of all data sources as it was a double-blind, randomized controlled trial with an active comparator arm. The trial compared purified trioctanoin (C8) to food-grade triheptanoin (C7).
L93: The source of triheptanoin used in the Gillingham (2017) trial was food grade, not commercial grade as used in studies UX007-CL201/CL202. However, the chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) team compared the composition of both products and determined that there were no significant differences. Therefore, the efficacy of the to-be-marketed product can be inferred from the food-grade product used in Gillingham (2017). The trioctanoin (C8) oil in the Gillingham trial was a purified natural product and allowed evaluation of consistent percentages of DCI between the food-grade triheptanoin and trioctanoin. The consistency of C8 or C7 dosages in the Gillingham (2017) trial was important for use in the determination of efficacy.
L94: Neither study UX007-CL201 nor the EA narratives were used to determine the efficacy of triheptanoin. The concern for study UX007-CL201 centered around the study design, a nonrandomized, open-label, single-arm study that compared events retrospectively obtained from the pre-triheptanoin period to events observed on triheptanoin treatment. In addition, there was a significant level of missing data from the retrospective period, variation in approach to the clinical management of LC-FAOD patients prior to enrollment, and concern for confounding from diet prior to triheptanoin exposure. Interpretation of the EA narratives also was impacted by missing data (concomitant medications, information on dietary changes of all dietary components (total kcal/kg/day; protein, etc.) before and after initiation and other medical interventions.
L95: The Gillingham trial differs from studies UX007-CL201 and UX007-CL202 in that the source of even-numbered medium-chain fat in studies UX007-CL201/202 was MCT oil and in the Gillingham trial it was purified food grade C8 oil. Each formulation of MCT oil used in studies UX007-CL201 and UX007-CL202 could vary in its composition with differences in the proportion of C6, C8, C10, and C12. Neither study specified a brand source of naturally-occurring or processed MCT oil, so patient dietary records may reflect these variations. In contrast, the Gillingham trial used a food-grade, purified C8 oil, allowing for a consistent comparison of C8 to C7 among patients. As MCT oil may contain 40% to 70% C8, patients enrolled in study UX007-CL201 may have pretreatment laboratory and clinical evaluations that are reflective of diets consisting of different concentrations of MCT oil. Patient level data of dietary analysis prior to enrollment were not obtained for these patients.
L96: ## Determination of Safety
L97: Study UX007-CL201 and the open-label extension study UX007-CL202 were reviewed to determine the safety of triheptanoin in patients with LC-FAOD. Both studies prospectively assessed safety and therefore were the most reliable of the data submitted for review. The adverse events reported in the Gillingham (2017) trial were considered supportive, in part
L98: because the dose of triheptanoin was lower (20% DCI compared to 24% to 40% DCI). The inclusion criteria for studies UX007-CL201/202 specified LC-FAOD patients of greater severity than those enrolled in Gillingham (2017); therefore, the adverse events reported in this combined cohort may reflect a greater sensitivity to dietary changes and greater general risk for adverse events. This population would also be the most informative for the safety analysis of triheptanoin. Due to missing data that confounded full clinical interpretation, the adverse events reported in the EA narratives were also considered supportive.

L119: ## 5.1. Nonclinical Assessment of Potential Effectiveness
L120: The nonclinical assessment characterizing the effectiveness of triheptanoin (UX007) in LC-FAOD comes from published pharmacodynamic (PD) studies using tissues from in vitro and in vivo animal models.
L121: - In excised rat liver perfused with ^{13}\text{C}-labeled 1mM heptanoate, it was demonstrated that for every molecule of heptanoate (metabolite of triheptanoin) uptake by the liver, two molecules of acetyl-CoA and one molecule of propionyl-CoA are produced. The acetyl-CoA enters the TCA cycle where it is used for adenosine triphosphate production and ketogenesis (Deng et al. 2009). The effects of a single IV infusion of ^{13}\text{C} heptanoate were studied in the brains of wildtype mice and compared to those of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT-1)-deficient transgenic mice. Although glucose concentrations were significantly elevated in both wildtype and GLUT-1 knockout (KO) mice following heptanoate infusion, the brain levels of acetyl CoA were higher in the GLUT-1 KO animals than in the wildtype mice. Similarly, although the neurotransmitter glutamine levels were higher than the glutamate levels in the brain of both animals, the glutamine levels were significantly higher in the KO animal brain than in the wildtype animal brain, indicating a potential compensatory mechanism by reactive astrocytes to the lack of GLUT-1 protein. The evidence of labeled substrate and metabolites in both the liver and brain suggests that heptanoate can diffuse across the blood brain barrier and plasma membrane to produce energy for all TCA cycle-dependent cell types (Marin-Valencia et al. 2013).
L122: - The effects of normal long-chain triglyceride diet (5% total fat representing 12% of metabolizable energy), medium-chain triglyceride diet (5% total fat representing 12% of metabolizable energy), or triheptanoin diet (5% total fat representing 12% of metabolizable energy) for 3 months and 12 months were compared in wildtype mice and in very long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficient (VLCAD^{-/-}) or KO mice. KO mice fed MCT diet for a year presented with progressive decrease in heart function and chronic energy deficiency, eventually leading to cardiomyopathy without any reversal or attenuation when compared to mice on control diets. In contrast, triheptanoin diet was better tolerated in KO mice and was not associated with progressive decline but stability in cardiac function. Thus, the progressive pathology observed in KO mice fed long-chain triglyceride and MCT diet may not be predictive of the clinical VLCAD enzyme deficiency. Although triheptanoin diet did not significantly improve cardiac function in mice, the lack of progression was notably different from the progressive disease observed in the MCT treated animals (Tucci et al. 2014; Tucci et al. 2017).
- The anticonvulsant effects of dietary heptanoin (35% of daily caloric intake) were studied in two mouse chronic seizure models for a treatment period of up to 7.5. The triheptanoin diet significantly delayed development of kindled seizures in fully kindled mice fed 35% triheptanoin compared to the standard diet. The triheptanoin diet resulted in reversal of tonic extension by 40% in pilocarpine pentylenetetrazole-induced seizure in status epilepticus (SE) model mice (Willis et al. 2010).
- Measurement of blood and brain metabolites in mice demonstrated that epileptic tissue shows changes in metabolism and TCA activity, including significant increases in malonyl-CoA and decreases in propionyl-, acetyl-, and \beta-hydroxybutyryl-CoA in SE compared to non-SE mouse brain. In addition, aspartate and GABA were decreased compared to non-SE mice. SE mice fed triheptanoin diet had propionyl-CoA but not acetyl-CoA levels restored, whereas methylmalonyl- and HMG-CoA levels were increased in triheptanoin-fed SE mice but not in non-SE mice, showing a connection between the metabolism of triheptanoin and the production of anaplerotic intermediates in tissues. Triheptanoin diet doubled the levels of brain \beta-hydroxybutyryl-CoA in SE and non-SE mice. Thus, the data indicate that the metabolism of triheptanoin is different in epileptic tissue compared to normal brain (Willis et al. 2010).
- In an isolated heart rat model of cardiac hypertrophy, triheptanoin diet at 7% or 30% of daily caloric intake for 5 weeks improved glucose oxidation and preserved cardiac power, which resulted in the prevention of contractile dysfunction. Tissue doppler measurements showed that left ventricular hypertrophy was significantly reduced, with improved diastolic function in mice fed 30% triheptanoin compared to mice on a control diet. In the isolated working rat heart perfusions, triheptanoin treatment improved myocardial glucose oxidation, but not cardiac fatty acid oxidation. The isolated heart from mice fed the 30% triheptanoin diet showed the highest level of C5-ketone body, beta-hydroxypentanoate (BHP) in blood, compared to the 7% triheptanoin diet or control diet. Triheptanoin treatment, however, did not affect myocardial insulin sensitivity. The study demonstrated that cardiac hypertrophy is likely related to altered anaplerosis (Nguyen et al. 2015).

L151: ## 6.2. Design of Clinical Trials Intended to Demonstrate Benefit to Patients
L152: ## 6.2.1. Trial Design
L153: The studies submitted for review in this NDA differed in their designs. The Applicant intended that the data from study UX007-CL201, a phase 2 study designed as an open-label switch from historical control in patients diagnosed with severe LC-FAOD, would be used to evaluate the efficacy of triheptanoin. However, study UX007-CL201 was not an adequate or well-controlled study due to the amount of missing data and confounding dietary changes that occurred after study enrollment. In contrast, the Gillingham trial was well controlled and the data were adequate given two treatment arms and confirmatory evidence given the underlying chemistry of triheptanoin as medium-chain fatty acid.
L154: Unlike study UX007-CL201, the Gillingham trial enrolled patients with nonsevere but stable LC-FAOD and followed them for only 4 months. The data from these trials are discussed in Section 6.3.
L155: Based upon review of the inclusion criteria for the Gillingham trial and study UX007-CL201 (Table 11) the categorization of nonsevere and severe LC-FAOD patients appears to rely upon each investigator's clinical assessment. The initial Gillingham (2017) protocol submission only required a molecular diagnosis of LC-FAOD for enrollment. However, a protocol amendment required that patients have at least one previous episode of rhabdomyolysis. By comparison, study UX007-CL201 required patients to have at least one predefined clinical- or laboratory-based criteria descriptive of high risk for poor catabolic control.

L163: ## Study UX007-CL201
L164: Study UX007-CL201 was a phase 2, single arm, multicenter, prospective study to evaluate the impact of triheptanoin in patients 6 years of age and older who had been previously diagnosed with serious clinical manifestations of LC-FAOD despite current management. L195: ## Gillingham (2017)
L196: Randomization to food grade C7 or C8 in a 1:1 fashion was stratified by medical diagnosis (CPT II, VLCAD, or TFP/LCHAD deficiency) and trial site. This study was initiated as blinded to patients (single blinded) until a study coordinator was identified.5 A total of 17 patients were enrolled at the time of the amendment. All models included treatment, medical diagnosis, study site and time (baseline or follow-up) as fixed effects, and included patient as a random effect. Skewed data were log transformed. The analyses were conducted in the intent-to-treat population including all randomized patients.

This value was based on results from a prior study in which the mean (SD) z-score was -1.12 (3.12) and 5.22 (1.82) for subjects receiving a high-protein or standard diet, respectively.

L202: ## Study UX007-CL201
L203: Study UX007-CL201 was designed as an exploratory/proof-of-concept study and not as a registrational study. The target sample size of 30 patients was based on feasibility rather than on a testable statistical objective and hypothesis. The statistical analysis plan (SAP) states that the primary study objective was to evaluate the impact of triheptanoin on acute clinical pathophysiology associated with LC-FAOD following 24 weeks of treatment evaluated by several outcome measures. However, following discussions with the Agency, the Applicant focused primarily on the outcome of MCEs comprising hypoglycemia, rhabdomyolysis, and cardiomyopathy over 78 weeks of treatment, which was an extension period outcome. These MCEs were defined rather broadly but focused upon clinical events of rhabdomyolysis, hypoglycemia, and cardiomyopathy that resulted in any hospitalization, emergency department/acute care visit, or emergency intervention (defined as any unscheduled administration of therapeutics at home or in the clinic).
L204: *Comments: Skeletal myopathy, hepatic disease, and cardiac disease are widely reported events of clinical importance in patients with LC-FAODs. The study protocol specifically lists each of these as key efficacy outcomes; however, the composite comprising these events is not explicitly stated in the protocol or SAP as a primary or main outcome. While this is a limitation and is illustrative of the exploratory nature of the study, the combination of these clinical events, each of which represents negative outcomes and are of similar clinical importance, is acceptable. To support the analyses of the composite outcome, the rate and duration of each component were calculated and were presented for the pre-triheptanoin and the triheptanoin periods.*
L205: The numbers and durations of MCEs were calculated as the total number of events or total duration of clinical events (days), respectively, and annualized by dividing by duration (days) divided by the total duration (days) of data collection/365.25. The MCE annualized events rates (AER) and event durations (AED) from the retrospective (pre-triheptanoin period) and the triheptanoin period were compared by the Applicant using a paired t-test. Given the absence of randomization, time point zero was defined based on study enrollment.
L206: Per the Agency's guidance, the Applicant also analyzed AER and AED using a negative binomial regression (NBR) model to account for follow-up days less than 78 weeks during the pre-triheptanoin period (for infants less than 18 months of age at time of study) and the correlation among events in the same patient when applicable. The NBR model includes period
L207: (pre-triheptanoin versus triheptanoin) as a covariate and time in each period as an offset (log transformed time (years) on study). Covariate effects were tested using the Wald and score statistics.
L208: *Comments: The NBR sensitivity analysis was added after discussion with the Agency and was not specified in the SAP. This is the more-appropriate approach to analyzing the primary outcome, which is a count. The paired t-test submitted by the Applicant assumes differences in annual event rates are normally distributed and this seems inappropriate. As such, the Agency views the results from this analysis as primary when interpreting results of this study. It should also be noted that while the choice of analysis did not impact statistical significance, the interpretation of the results is still a concern.*
L209: Additional analyses requested by the Agency during the NDA review included one that accounted for the time-varying nature of triheptanoin dosing during the prospective part of the study while also accounting for the dependence between events occurring in the same patient. This analysis used the model described by Anderson and Gill (AG) (Andersen and Gill 1982), which is a modified Cox proportional hazards model modeling time to MCE that assumes that the correlation between event times for a patient can be explained by a time-varying covariate. In the model for study UX007-CL201, the modeled effect was change (5%, 10%, and 15%) in percent daily caloric intake from pretreatment (baseline) MCT to triheptanoin as a time-varying covariate and included on-treatment diet data (gathered from patient completed diary) and on-study MCEs. The model does not include events occurring during the pre-triheptanoin period as the model effect is change in percent DCI during study. The model includes data collected from 25 of the 29 patients who supplied diet diary data during the study. However, the amount of missing diet-based data for the retrospective period was considerably higher.

L211: The methods used to analyze annualized MCEs observed/reported in study UX007-CL202 were the same as those described for study UX007-CL201. Annualized MCEs are presented by individual study cohort only. No comparative analyses are presented (see comment below) and no inferential testing performed.
L212: *Comments: The Applicant compared the MCE annualized event rates between the pre-triheptanoin period and the triheptanoin period in the UX007-CL201 rollover cohort. This analysis comparing the 18-month pre-triheptanoin event rate to the 36-month triheptanoin (combined UX007-CL201/CL202) data suffers from a major limitation, namely that the pre-triheptanoin observation period is one-half the duration of the combined UX007-C201/C202 exposure period. For this reason, findings from this biased analysis are not presented in this review.*
L213: *In addition, the Applicant compared the MCE annualized event rates between the pre-triheptanoin and triheptanoin periods in the triheptanoin-naïve cohort. The analysis is not presented in this review as it is likely confounded by prior treatment (pre-triheptanoin) for which data were not adequately collected and accounted for in the analysis. Essentially, this comparison suffers from the same issues as described for the results presented for study UX007-CL201 and is compounded by the level of disease heterogeneity in the patients enrolled.*

L251: In summary, the Gillingham (2017) trial was a randomized, double-blind, proof-of-concept trial performed in 32 patients with LC-FAOD with a history of nonsevere disease who were stable at baseline. Patients received an average dose of approximately 15% total DCI of either triheptanoin or trioctanoin. The trial did not demonstrate a difference between treatment arms in the clinical events of interest or in the incidence of rhabdomyolysis. Changes in cardiac function were limited to a small set of patients with evaluable data and were of questionable clinical relevance. Ultimately, labeling reflects that patients in both groups demonstrated a similar mean-fold change from baseline in left ventricular ejection fraction and in wall mass on resting echocardiogram. They also exhibited similar maximal heart rates on treadmill ergometry.
L252: The trial results suggest that stable patients with nonsevere LC-FAOD generally tolerated C7 or C8 treatment without suffering from severe clinical outcomes during the 4 months of treatment. Given the trial's limited duration and negative findings, this study does not provide adequate evidence of efficacy to demonstrate the superiority of triheptanoin over other sources of medium-chain triglycerides for the treatment of LC-FAOD. However, these findings do show that triheptanoin is well-tolerated in patients with LC-FAOD. Qualitatively, no clinically meaningful differences are observed between treatment with triheptanoin and trioctanoin, providing adequate evidence of efficacy to support a recommendation for approval of
L253: triheptanoin as an alternative source of calories and fatty acids for the treatment of patients with LC-FAOD.

L262: A major challenge in interpreting results from study UX007-CL201 is the retrospective nature of the pre-triheptanoin events and nutritional status. The Applicant attempted to retrospectively obtain diet-specific information from the pre-triheptanoin period covering up to at most 78 weeks prior to enrollment. L292: ## 6.5. Review Issues Relevant to the Evaluation of Benefit
L293: The review team was unable to find conclusive evidence to support superior efficacy of triheptanoin over the current standard of care for the indication proposed by the Applicant with NDA submission. A more limited indication was agreed for triheptanoin “as a source of calories and odd-chain fatty acids for the treatment of pediatric and adult patients with molecularly confirmed long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (LC-FAOD).”
L294: Identified review concerns included the lack of adequate and well-controlled clinical trial data in patients with LC-FAOD, whose disease is not adequately controlled on current management. Those review issues relevant to the evaluation of benefit included insufficient duration and scope of data provided by the Gillingham randomized comparative trial and also included the study design of the Applicant’s phase 2 clinical study UX007-CL201. Due to these concerns, only limited study data were provided in labeling, which was restricted to a global interpretation of Gillingham (2017) and supported by data on treatment from studies UX007-201/UX007-202.
L295: ## 6.5.1. Gillingham Trial
L296: ## Issues
L297: Lack of long-term natural history data describing the heterogeneity of disease and rate of disease progression
L298: ## Analysis
L299: Because the Gillingham (2017) trial was a randomized, double-blind, comparative design, the data support the efficacy of triheptanoin in patients with LC-FAOD. An important factor in this evaluation is that the to-be-marketed formulation of triheptanoin is a comparable product to the food-grade triheptanoin used in the trial (IND 113386). Refer to CMC comments in Section 9 for more details.
L300: While this trial provides randomized, comparator data, it is limited in the ability to provide conclusive, long-term data on the efficacy of triheptanoin for the Applicant's proposed dose and indication for the following reasons.
L301: - On average, the triheptanoin in Gillingham (2017) trial dose comprised approximately 17% of total DCI, considerably less than the Applicant's initially proposed 25% to 35% DCI target dose range. These estimates were based on partially complete patient diet diaries collected at three time points during the trial.
- The level of disease in the study population was prespecified as nonsevere, where patients were selected by medical histories without severe or repetitive catabolism or associated sequelae. The Applicant's initially proposed indication for triheptanoin was for the treatment of patients with LC-FAOD. In contrast, the Gillingham (2017) trial required stable disease but did not require optimized control.
- The trial duration was only 4 months with in-person assessments occurring at baseline and at month 4. This limited trial duration prevents a complete assessment of long-term treatment maintenance and tolerability.
- This trial was designed as a research trial to objectively assess the effects of triheptanoin on total energy exposure and several other outcomes that are all considered exploratory. Although the trial collected information on incidence of rhabdomyolysis—a clinically-meaningful outcome—the trial was not powered to demonstrate a difference between treatment arms on this outcome.
- The effect of triheptanoin and trioctanoin on cardiac physiology was assessed with resting ECHO and treadmill ergometry. After 4 months, patients treated with triheptanoin and trioctanoin had comparable findings on resting ECHO, with similar mean-fold change from baseline in LVEF and wall mass. Patients in the two treatment groups also had similar maximal heart rates on treadmill ergometry.
L302: ## Conclusion
L303: The basis for the Division's rationale for changing the indication to "a medium-chain triglyceride indicated as a source of calories and fatty acids for the treatment of pediatric and adult patients with molecularly confirmed long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (LC-FAOD)" is the following:
L304: - The data provided from the Gillingham (2017) trial can be used to support the use of triheptanoin in place of MCT oil in patients with LC-FAOD. While this trial only enrolled clinically stable patients, it is supported by the safety profiles of patients enrolled in studies UX007-CL201/UX007-CL202 and EA INDs who were at greater risk for metabolic events due to catabolism. This decision is predicated on the current
L305: understanding that LC-FAOD patients who receive insufficient calories or too much long-chain fatty acid (via oral intake, G-tube or intravenous administration) can experience life threatening metabolic crises within hours to days (Merritt et al. 2018).

- The data do not provide evidence to support triheptanoin use to prevent or to reduce clinical events as this study did not measure such outcomes.
L306: ## 6.5.2. Nonrandomized Study UX007-CL201
L307: ## Issue
L308: The Applicant's primary study, submitted to demonstrate both efficacy and safety, was not an adequate and well-controlled study.
L309: ## Analysis
L310: Study UX007-CL201 was not an adequate and well-controlled study as efficacy outcomes described by MCE were significantly confounded by additional changes in diet made prior to and at the time of introduction of triheptanoin. Therefore, results from study UX007-CL201 cannot serve as the primary evidence to support the efficacy of triheptanoin for the proposed indication and will not be included in section 14 of the label.
L311: As discussed in Section 6.2.1, the study design is flawed as patients, on average, received anywhere from 0% to 33% DCI as medium-chain triglycerides during the pre-triheptanoin run-in period. Upon switch to triheptanoin, this amount increased to between 25% and 35% DCI. Therefore, the results are confounded by the change in percentage of medium-chain fat daily calories. Upon starting triheptanoin, there was an average of a nearly 20% increase in total DCI. Because diet is the current standard of care for LC-FAOD, this DCI increase makes it difficult to separate the notable change in total intake of medium-chain fat and total caloric intake from any possible treatment effect from triheptanoin. Furthermore, data on diet management during the pre-triheptanoin period covering up to 78 weeks are grossly lacking in detail except for the data collected during the run-in period covering 4 weeks prior to initiation of triheptanoin.
L312: Though the Applicant reports that study investigators ensured complete medical records for the 18 months prior to triheptanoin initiation upon enrollment, the diet-related data are grossly inadequate. Specifically, data were limited to one or two pre-triheptanoin assessments in only 11 out of 29 patients. Management guidelines by Genetic Metabolic Dietitians International recommend that patients diagnosed with VLCAD are assessed by a metabolic nutritionist and/or a medical provider at least annually when disease is stable and more frequently when disease is unstable (Southeast Regional Genetics Network 2019b). While the guidelines were written for patients diagnosed with VLCAD, they can be applicable for all patients with LC-FAOD. Patients eligible for study UX007-CL201 were required to have a recent history of multiple episodes of acute disease exacerbation or chronic disease exacerbation. The lack of diet-specific data in more than half of this severely affected study population suggests an issue of data quality as it would not be expected given the recommendations for management of disease this severe. Further, the sparseness of the pre-triheptanoin data on dietary intake provided on the 11 enrolled patients calls into question whether these data can be accurately extrapolated to the remaining 18 patients for whom data were not available. Given the variability in LC-FAOD medical diagnoses, patient ages, clinical sites and standards of practice, there is no way to verify that the missing data (n=18 patients) resemble the available data (n=11 patients).
L313: ## Conclusion
L314: Findings from study UX007-CL201 can be used to infer that the change from MCT oil to triheptanoin appears reasonably safe, as it does not appear to increase the risk of serious adverse events. In patients who were not on any source of MCT oil prior to enrollment, the introduction of triheptanoin into their diet also did not appear to provoke catabolism.
L315: ## **6.5.3. Variability in Clinical Events Assessment (Study UX007-CL201)**
L316: ## Issue
L317: The criteria for hospitalization or emergency department visits due to MCEs were not standardized.
L318: ## Analysis
L319: In study UX007-CL201, major clinical outcomes experienced prior to treatment with triheptanoin were collected retrospectively, by each investigator, at the time of screening. However, the definitions of each event (rhabdomyolysis, hypoglycemia, and cardiomyopathy) were loosely defined in the study protocol. This allowed for variability across clinical centers and investigators regarding what constituted an event. Specifically, major rhabdomyolysis events were those leading to emergency room/acute care visits, hospitalizations, emergency interventions (at home or in the clinic), or any similar event. The protocol did not specify target creatine kinase values or any other clinical measure to define this outcome, or interventions to be made. Similarly, the evaluation for cardiomyopathy considered ECHO, ECG or additional tests (not specified) as medically indicated. Hypoglycemia was more specifically defined as serum glucose levels &lt;60 mg/dL that occur at home and require treatment.
L320: ## Conclusion
L321: Given the subjective nature of these definitions for events comprising the primary outcome there is concern that variability by investigator may have been introduced. Therefore, the comparative assessments of the pre-triheptanoin and triheptanoin event rates cannot be viewed as conclusive.
L322: ## **6.5.4. Data Do Not Support the Proposed Indication**
L323: ## Issue
L324: The data submitted in the NDA do not support the Applicant's proposed indication of use for triheptanoin.
L325: ## Assessment
L326: The Applicant's initially proposed indication was the following: "[REDACTED] (b) (4)"
"[REDACTED] for the treatment of adult and pediatric patients (b) (4)"
"[REDACTED] with long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (LC-FAOD) [REDACTED] (b) (4)"
L327: Due to the level of confounded dietary data in study UX007-CL201, this proposed indication cannot be considered. However, triheptanoin may be considered a source of dietary calories, as similar to dietary sources of C8, based upon the following:
L328: - During the Gillingham (2017) trial (IND 113386), patients on C8 had sufficient exposure duration to assess their risk of catabolism. Patients on C8 did not immediately go into catabolism or worsen in their disease status, as would be expected if they ingested food that they could not utilize based upon the underlying common mechanisms of LC-FAOD.
- Records from studies UX007-CL201 and UX007-CL202 demonstrated continued growth at anticipated rates after switchover from MCT oil or previous diet, suggesting that the calories supplied by triheptanoin were used similarly as those from MCT oil. Taken together, these data suggest that triheptanoin is an appropriate source of dietary medium-chain fat for patients with LC-FAOD.
L329: ## Conclusion
L330: The Agency recommends the following indication: Triheptanoin “is a medium-chain triglyceride indicated as a source of calories and fatty acids for the treatment of pediatric and adult patients with molecularly confirmed long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (LC-FAOD).”
L331: ## 7. Risk and Risk Management
L332: ## 7.1. Potential Risks or Safety Concerns Based on Nonclinical Data
L333: There were no significant safety concerns identified in general toxicology studies conducted following oral administration of triheptanoin in rats and minipigs. Oral administration of triheptanoin for 9-months in minipigs was well tolerated. In the single-dose oral gavage administration of triheptanoin at up to 4.75 g/kg (5.0 ml/kg) to adult male and female Wistar rats, a dose equivalent to 13% daily caloric intake (study CL74-95-1045), there were no animal deaths or signs of toxicity seen for 14 days following dosing.
L334: The chronic (9-month) dietary administration of triheptanoin at 30% (up to 21 g/kg) or 50% (up to 35 g/kg) of diet or soybean oil (control diet) in juvenile (2 months old) male rats was generally well tolerated (Ataide et al. 2009). There were no significant differences observed in the clinical chemistries and lipid profile or in the urinalysis between the control and treatment groups. However, the relative liver weights were higher in the soybean oil diet group than in the triheptanoin diet group, and all the study animals showed macrovesicular hepatic steatosis upon histopathological examination. There was no correlation of the steatosis to clinical chemistries, suggesting that liver function was not affected. Histopathological changes seen in the small intestine include discrete atrophy or hyperplasia of villi, which did not affect digestive functions. In addition, lymphoid follicular hyperplasia, a case of muco-secreting adenocarcinoma in the duodenum, and four cases of “other alterations” in the duodenum were observed in treated animals, but not in control animals.
L335: The authors stated that similar alterations (adenocarcinoma and two cases of alterations) were also observed in the control animals administered soybean oil. However, the study did not use
L336: any saline or water control, and the relevance of these findings to treatment cannot be ruled out. This was not a good laboratory practice (GLP)-compliant toxicology study, and only a limited number of tissues were examined histologically. The proliferative changes observed in the rat intestine may suggest that oral administration of triheptanoin has a potential for causing tumors in rodents. In addition, in a study conducted by NTP, chronic administration of a similar LCT (tricaprylin, C8) in rats caused increased incidences of fore-stomach and pancreatic adenoma but not carcinoma (National Toxicology 1994).
L337: The chronic (9-month) dietary toxicity of triheptanoin was assessed in juvenile male and female minipigs (the equivalent human age of &gt;2 years) at doses of 10%, 30%, or 50% DCI following dosing (study UX007-PC002). Triheptanoin was well tolerated without any treatment-related toxicity or deaths. Two high-dose animals died from a prolapsed penis and a urinary tract infection, which are common occurrences in minipigs. There were no test article-related changes in body weight, body weight gain, hematology profiles, organ weights, or histopathology, and no target organs of toxicity were identified. The brief inappetence seen in the 30% and 50% DCI dose groups was over in 2 days as the animals ate all their rations afterwards. The observed changes in hematology, clinical chemistry, and lipid parameters were not dose-related, occurred intermittently in one sex or the other, and are secondary to reduced iron and protein content in the dietary admixture. Gross and histopathology assessments did not show any changes in any organ or tissue that could be considered treatment-related. The no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) for daily administration of triheptanoin for 9 months in minipigs was 50% DCI. The safety margin for clinical dose is approximately 4X, and is based on the area under the concentration time curve (AUC) of heptanoate at the NOAEL in the minipig study and the mean AUC in the clinical study (UX007-CL101) at a dose of 1.25 g/kg/day or 30% DCI.
L338: Triheptanoin was not mutagenic in the Ames assay up to 4800 µg/plate, did not induce chromosomal aberration in vitro in human peripheral blood lymphocytes up to 409 µg/ml, and it was negative in the in vivo rat micronucleus assay at up to 2000 mg/kg/day.
L339: In the segment 1 oral reproductive toxicity study in rats, there were no treatment-related effects on male and female fertility. The slow acceptance of the female rats to triheptanoin (10%, 30%, or 50% DCI) lasted 1 week before adjustment. Body weights were significantly reduced in the 50% DCI dose group in the first week, and body weight gains were reduced in males dosed with 50% DCI and in females dosed with 30% and 50% DCI prior to mating. There were no noteworthy treatment-related necropsy findings in the animals. There were also no treatment effects on mating performance or relative reproductive organ weights. There was no impact on male or female fertility. The NOAEL for fertility and reproductive performance in male and female rats was 50% DCI.
L340: In the segment II oral embryofetal developmental toxicity study in rats dosed with triheptanoin at 10%, 30%, or 50% DCI, there were no clinical signs of toxicity observed over the gestation period of days 6 through 17 except reduced food consumption and reduced body weight. However, gross necropsy observations showed one dam in the 30% DCI dose group with brown fluid in the uterus and another dam in the 50% DCI dose group had red ovaries. Reduced food consumption from inappetence in the 30% and 50% DCI dose group resulted in reduced body weight and body weight gain in the dams during gestation days 6 through 12. Although litter viability and litter weights were not affected, there were incidences of skeletal malformations in the fetuses from dams in the 50% DCI dose group. Skeletal examination of fetuses from the 30% DCI dose groups were unaffected. The alterations seen in the fetuses from the 50% DCI dose
L341: dams were associated with maternal toxicity occurring from reduced body weight and body weight gain. The NOAEL for maternal toxicity of triheptanoin was 10% DCI, while the NOAEL for embryofetal developmental toxicity was 30% DCI. The adverse effects observed in pregnant rats were related to reduced body weights and lower mean body weight gain, indicative of maternal toxicity in both 30% and 50% triheptanoin treated dams. The body weight reductions observed in the pregnant animals was due to reduced food intake, possibly associated with flavor aversion to triheptanoin admixed in the diet which is not a normal constituent of rodent chow. Thus, the adverse effects observed in these animals were attributed to a taste aversion effect triggering a reduced food intake and are not related to the pharmacological effects of triheptanoin or its primary metabolites.
L342: In the segment II oral embryofetal developmental toxicity study in rabbits dosed with triheptanoin at 10%, 20%, or 30% DCI, dietary inappetence was very severe with animals in the 20% DCI dose group consuming 70 g to 90 g out of 120 g of food provided and those in the 30% DCI dose group consuming just 13-20 g out of the 105 g of food provided. However, rabbits in the 10% DCI dose group were unaffected. Consequently, there were both increases and decreases in body weight at these doses. Thus, oral gavage administration of a high dose of triheptanoin oil was not tolerated by the rabbits. Two rabbits in the 30% DCI dose group died on gestation days 13 and 19. One death was due to gavage trauma while the other death was due to inappetence and food aversion. There were clinical evidences of scant feces, abortion, premature delivery, vaginal discharge, red material, and pups/placental discharge in the 30% DCI treatment group. Pre-implantation (0.7% to 8.9%) and post-implantation loss (2% to 8.8%) occurred in all the treated rabbits, while the numbers of corpora lutea of rabbits in the 20% and 30% DCI groups were significantly elevated. High dose (30% DCI) exposure fetuses in two separate full-term gravid litters had skeletal malformations that were related to maternal toxicity. The NOAEL for maternal toxicity was 10% DCI, while the NOAEL for developmental toxicity was 20% DCI of triheptanoin. The decreased body weights observed in rabbits is related to decreased feed intake secondary to forced gavage administration of a large volume of triheptanoin oil and is not relevant to humans.
L343: In the pre- and postnatal development study, the effects of oral dietary administration of triheptanoin (10%, 30%, and 50% DCI) to female rats and their offspring were studied. Triheptanoin dosing in the F0 generation of dams from implantation to gestation and lactation resulted in reduced mean maternal body weight gain during gestation, reduced mean maternal food consumption in all the treated rats during gestation and lactation period, and persistently reduced mean body weight gain in the 30% and 50% DCI dose groups. However, there were no triheptanoin (UX007)-related effects on F0 maternal macroscopic findings, delivery, or litter observations. The observed reductions in mean body weight gain were the effects of initial food aversion in the F0 generation dams. The two deaths of the F0 dams (from the 10% and 50% DCI dose exposure groups) were unrelated to UX007 administration. In the preweaning F1 generation pups, there were no adverse clinical observations, behavioral changes, or necropsy changes. However, reduced mean body weights and changes in sexual maturation and in organ weights were observed through the postpartum days 1 to 28 in the 30% and 50% DCI exposed F1 pups as a result of exposure to UX007. In the F1 pups, minimal to mild alterations in hematology and serum chemistry were observed in the 50% DCI exposure group, and microscopic minimal vacuolation was seen in the liver hepatocytes of the 30% and 50% exposure groups. The observed changes were considered secondary to the reduced food, and probably also secondary
L344: to reduced water consumption. All the F1 generation survived until scheduled euthanasia. Exposure to UX007 was found in day 21 postpartum pups who were also probably exposed to the test article during the gestation or lactation period and via direct consumption. The overall effects of UX007 were seen as weight reduction in treated dams stemming from food aversion, resulting in pups that were smaller and had sexual maturation changes although not behavioral changes. The effects of UX007 seen in the F1 generation were secondary to the maternal toxicities and not considered clinically relevant. The NOAEL could not be determined in the maternal F0 generation, but the NOAEL for F1 generation was 50% DCI of UX007.
L345: In a special toxicity study, dermal sensitization was performed with the intradermal injection of 0.1 ml of triheptanoin in male albino guinea pigs every other day for a total of nine injections. Triheptanoin was not a dermal sensitizing agent in male albino guinea pigs.
L346: ## 7.2. Potential Risks or Safety Concerns Based on Drug Class or Other Drug-Specific Factors
L347: Triheptanoin is a colorless, odorless liquid composed entirely from oil. The anticipated risks for this product are gastrointestinal (GI) adverse events, such as diarrhea, emesis/vomiting, and possibly pancreatitis or gall stone formation. Patients with LC-FAOD who become dehydrated due to emesis/vomiting, persistent diarrhea, or poor caloric intake are at additional risk for catabolism, often manifesting as rhabdomyolysis. The review team is aware that while a GI adverse event may create a higher risk for catabolism, there is a need to monitor patients' total caloric intake during dosage titration, especially in patients with gastrointestinal adverse reactions, and to adjust all components of the diet as needed to prevent catabolism.

L349: Not applicable. This product has not been approved in the U.S. market or in any foreign market; therefore, no postmarketing experience is available at the time of this review.

L452: ## 7.6.7. Cardiac Events
L453: One manifestation of LC-FAOD disease pathology is the development of cardiomyopathy. While patients with frequent disease exacerbation report rhabdomyolysis most frequently, there is no
L454: ability to predict which patients will develop cardiomyopathy. Some patients who have developed cardiomyopathy have presented during a protracted duration of intermittent catabolism. Recovery occurs during anabolism, though patients who have already developed cardiomyopathy are at risk for redevelopment in the future. It is not common to identify an arrhythmia not associated with cardiomyopathy in these patients. It is also not common to identify ischemia not associated with cardiomyopathy in these patients.
L455: There is no evidence of an association of clinical events related to QT prolongation with triheptanoin treatment in the available safety data. The Applicant did collect ECGs in both study UX007-CL201 and study UX007-CL202, and no subjects were identified with QTc &gt;480 ms or \DeltaQTc &gt;60 ms. The unknown timing of ECG collection relative to dosing and the quality of ECG acquisition and measurements limit the data; however, there were no risks identified for cardiac arrhythmia or QT prolongation with triheptanoin use.
L456: No unanticipated adverse cardiac events were identified during this development program.
L457: ## 7.7. Review Issues Relevant to the Evaluation of Risk
L458: ## 7.7.1. Anticipated Risks
L459: ## Issue
L460: Patients who ingest a large amount of dietary fat are at higher risk for gastrointestinal adverse events.
L461: ## Assessment
L462: - A retrospective chart review of 20 patients initiated on triheptanoin of up to 35% DCI reported diarrhea in 60% of patients and GI upset in 35% of patients (Vockley et al. 2015), (Table 7).
- Gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, gastroenteritis, abdominal pain) were the most commonly reported AEs that were not major clinical events in studies UX007-CL201/UX007-CL202.
- This review was not able to determine if triheptanoin related GI events were increased compared to other sources of medium-chain triglycerides, such as MCT oil as studies UX007-CL201/UX007-CL202 did not collect AE data during the retrospective pre-triheptanoin period.
- More GI AEs were reported in patients who were exposed to higher doses of triheptanoin (25% to 35%).
L463: ## Conclusion
L464: The GI adverse events are anticipated risks to ingestion of triheptanoin. These AEs may be mitigated by changes in the dose of MCT, source of MCT, and other dietary modifications. Labeling will reflect these recommendations.
L465: ## 7.7.2. Risk During Pregnancy
L466: ## Issue
L467: There are no clinical study data to assess the risk of use of triheptanoin during pregnancy.
L468: ## Assessment
L469: - Improved newborn screening technologies have increased the rates of LC-FAOD detection in infancy. Early dietary management has improved health and longevity of patients with LC-FAOD, who otherwise might not have survived childhood. These patients, who are diagnosed as adults or managed through adulthood, may now provide an understanding of dietary experience during pregnancy and fetal risk.
- Triheptanoin is a source of medium-chain triglycerides for the treatment of LC-FAOD. Long-term use, and thus, use during pregnancy, is anticipated.
L470: ## Conclusion
L471: A PMR for a pregnancy registry would inform the use of triheptanoin during pregnancy.
L472: ## 7.7.3. Risks for Use With Specific Plastic Materials
L473: ## Issue
L474: Feeding tubes containing polyvinyl chloride (PVC) or containers containing polystyrene are not recommended for use with triheptanoin.
L475: ## Assessment
L476: Triheptanoin may degrade PVC or dissolve polystyrene plastics upon contact.
L477: ## Conclusion
L478: Labeling reflects these recommendations. Refer to Section 9.1 for details.
L479: ## 7.7.4. Long QT Study Waiver
L480: ## Issue
L481: A designated study to assess the risk for long QT syndrome has not been completed by the Applicant.
L482: ## Assessment
L483: Triheptanoin is a medium-chain triglyceride and is a source of calories and fatty acids for patients diagnosed with LC-FAOD. It has a low systemic exposure and is ultimately used as a source of dietary fat in this population. Furthermore, a review of the cardiac data did not identify cardiac risks for generalized arrhythmia, QT prolongation, or other cardiac events not associated with catabolism in these patients. Given these factors, the risk for heptanoate and other active metabolites appears low.

L485: A designated long QT study will be waived.
L486: ## 8. Therapeutic Individualization
L487: ## 8.1. Intrinsic Factors
L488: As a caloric source, the total daily dose of triheptanoin should be individualized based on disease severity, metabolic needs, and tolerability. Therefore, all patients treated with triheptanoin should be under the care of a clinical specialist knowledgeable in appropriate disease-related dietary management that is based upon current nutritional recommendations.
L489: ## Hepatic/Renal Function
L490: The effects of hepatic impairment or renal impairment on exposure of triheptanoin and its metabolites are unknown because there are neither dedicated clinical studies in subjects with such impairments nor clinical data from patients with LC-FAOD who have hepatic or renal impairment. Of note, metabolism of heptanoate primarily occurs in the liver as does energy metabolism, such as beta-oxidation and the TCA cycle. Hepatic disease may alter energy demands and lipid metabolism in the liver, but it is unknown how hepatic impairment affects lipid metabolism and nutritional requirements, including medium-chain fat in patients with LC-FAOD.
L491: On the other hand, urinary excretion of triheptanoin metabolites was minimal, so it is unlikely that renal impairment would significantly affect drug exposure.
L492: ## Pancreatic Insufficiency
L493: Pancreatic enzymes hydrolyze triheptanoin and release three moles of heptanoate as medium-chain fatty acids in the small intestine. Low or absent pancreatic enzymes may result in reduced absorption of heptanoate, subsequently leading to insufficient supplementation of medium-chain fat in patients with LC-FAOD. Unlike triheptanoin, MCT oil does not require hydrolysis by pancreatic enzyme to be absorbed. Patients with pancreatic insufficiency should avoid administration of triheptanoin.
L494: ## 8.2. Drug Interactions
L495: Triheptanoin is converted into heptanoate (C7 fatty acid) in the intestine by multiple types of pancreatic lipases. Patients should avoid coadministration of triheptanoin with lipase inhibitors (e.g., orlistat) because reduced conversion from triheptanoin to heptanoates leads to insufficient energy supply from C7 fatty acids.
L496: ## 8.3. Pediatric Labeling/Plans for Pediatric Drug Development
L497: Triheptanoin was granted orphan-drug designation on April 15, 2015, for the treatment of fatty acid oxidation disorders. Currently, there are no plans for additional pediatric studies, and triheptanoin is exempt from the Pediatric Research Equity Act (21 U.S.C. 355c). The Applicant has provided sufficient justification and right of reference to INDs where LC-FAOD patients
L498: were initiated on triheptanoin soon after birth. Therefore, triheptanoin will be indicated in patients with LC-FAOD from birth through adulthood.
L499: ## 8.4. Pregnancy and Lactation
L500: In a segment II embryofetal developmental toxicity study in rats, triheptanoin levels of 30% to 50% DCI correlated with decreased oral intake, resulting in weight reduction and smaller pups with sexual maturation changes. In the segment II embryofetal developmental toxicity study in rabbits, severe anorexia was noted in animals dosed as low as 20% DCI. Refer to Section 7.1 for greater detail in the toxicology review.
L501: Currently, there are few documented reports of pregnancy outcomes with maternal LC-FAOD. With improvements in LC-FAOD diagnoses, there is a need to better understand the impact of triheptanoin as a synthetic fat on the risk for fetal anomalies. The Applicant will be asked to conduct a single-arm, pregnancy safety study in women exposed to triheptanoin during pregnancy to assess risks of pregnancy complications and adverse effects on the developing fetus and neonate. Refer to Section III.22.

L871: ## 14.1.1. Population PK Analysis
L872: Pharmacokinetic data from aforementioned clinical studies showed a large between-subject variability. Also note that plasma heptanoate concentrations do not show a typical linear absorption and elimination kinetics after oral administration of triheptanoin. The Applicant's population PK analysis (Population PK Study Report, ULTR-PMX-UX007-852) is limited by insufficient PK data due to sparse PK sampling in a small number of patients with LC-FAOD, and may not be adequate to describe PK of heptanoate. L879: - The measure of the clinical response in UX007-CL201 (i.e., change from baseline in annualized major clinical events) is deemed confounded by modification in dietary management after initiation of triheptanoin. Refer to Section I.1 “Summary of Regulatory Action.”

L885: ## Protein Biding Studies
L886: ## Study UX007-PC020: Protein Binding of Heptanoate
L887: In the human plasma, heptanoate exhibited moderately high human plasma protein binding (~80% at the range of 40\mu\text{M} to 400\mu\text{M}).
L888: ## Study UX007-PC018: Protein Binding of Valproic Acid in the Presence of Heptanoate
L889: In the presence of 400\mu\text{M} heptanoate, the unbound fraction of valproic acid increased about twofold, suggesting that heptanoate may competitively displace valproic acid from binding to plasma proteins. Presence of valproic acid did not affect the protein binding of heptanoate.
L890: ## Metabolism
L891: ## Study UX007-PC015: Lipase-Mediated Metabolism
L892: The in vitro evaluation of lipase-mediated metabolism showed that triheptanoin was hydrolyzed by three lipases tested (pancreatic triglyceride lipase, pancreatic triglyceride lipase-related protein 2, and carboxyl ester lipase).
L893: ## Study UX007-PC023: Metabolic Stability of Heptanoate in Human Hepatocytes
L894: In vitro metabolism of heptanoate was investigated using human hepatocytes. The mean half-life of heptanoate was estimated to be 28 minutes in the human hepatocytes. The in vitro-in vivo scaled hepatic clearance of heptanoate in humans was estimated to be 18.5 mL/min/kg, corresponding to ~90% of human hepatic blood flow.
L895: ## Study UX007-PC019: CYP and UGT-Mediated Metabolism
L896: Based on the in vitro evaluation in the human microsomes, neither heptanoate nor BHP was a sensitive substrate of human CYP or UGT enzymes.
L897: ## Study UX007-PC025: Identification of Metabolites in Human Liver Microsomes
L898: In vitro metabolites of heptanoate from human liver microsomes, no oxidation metabolites of heptanoate were detected, indicating that heptanoate is not a sensitive substrate of CYP enzymes. A direct glucuronide conjugate of heptanoate was observed, but the amount of this metabolite was negligible (~0.08 ppm).
L899: ## Drug-Drug Interactions
L900: ## Study UX007-PC021: Potential CYP Inhibition
L901: The potential for heptanoate or BHP to elicit drug-drug interactions via an inhibition of CYPs was investigated in an in vitro study. At 40 and 400\muM of heptanoate or 10 and 50\muM of BHP, neither heptanoate nor BHP showed appreciable inhibition of the major human CYP isoforms tested: CYP3A4, CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP2D6.

L970: ## CONTRAINDICATIONS, WARNINGS AND PRECAUTIONS
L971: There are no contraindications. A Warning and Precaution was added to address the potential safety concern of feeding tube dysfunction. During the clinical trials, adverse events of G-tube dysfunction were reported in patients receiving triheptanoin. The contribution of the product to the events was not clear, but MCT oil is known to degrade certain plastics (polystyrene) and triheptanoin adheres to PVC. The Applicant asserts that there is no evidence that plastic degrades triheptanoin. The compatibility studies support lack of degradation in silicone feeding tubes for up to 60 minutes. While causality cannot be established, the contribution of the drug cannot be ruled out. Therefore, it is recommended that healthcare providers regularly monitor the feeding tube to ensure proper functioning and integrity.
L972: A Warning and Precaution was also added to describe the risk of intestinal malabsorption of heptanoate in patients with pancreatic insufficiency. Dojolvi should be avoided in patients with pancreatic insufficiency because it is not possible to monitor pharmacodynamic or clinical response to triheptanoin to guide dosing.
L973: ## ADVERSE REACTIONS
L974: The review team based the safety evaluation on 79 patients and includes patients enrolled in studies UX007-201/UX007/202 (as discussed in Section II.7.5 of the Integrated Review). This population allowed for long-term assessment of most patients in study UX007-201 and was considered the most informative population. Adverse reactions reported in these patients were gastrointestinal in nature.
L975: ## DRUG INTERACTIONS
L976: Triheptanoin is hydrolyzed to heptanoate by pancreatic lipases in the intestine. Therefore, coadministration with pancreatic lipase inhibitors (e.g., orlistat) may reduce exposure to heptanoate and thereby potentially reduce the clinical effect of triheptanoin and is not recommended (see Section II.5 of the Integrated Review).
L977: ## USE IN SPECIFIC POPULATIONS
L978: ## Pregnancy
L979: There are no available data on triheptanoin use in pregnant women to evaluate for a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes.
L980: In animal reproduction studies conducted in pregnant rats and rabbits administered triheptanoin during the period of organogenesis, the primary toxicological effect (reduced body weight gain) was considered to be specific to decreased food consumption related to taste aversion in animals, and therefore is not relevant to clinical use in the intended populations (see Section II.7.1 of the Integrated Review).
L981: The Applicant will be required to conduct a pregnancy safety study collecting pregnancy outcome data (see Section 22). Women are advised to report pregnancies to the Applicant.
L982: ## Lactation
L983: There are no data on the presence of triheptanoin or its metabolites in human milk, the effects on the breastfed infant, or the effects on milk production. Studies in animals have not been conducted to assess the excretion of triheptanoin in the milk, and systemic exposure of triheptanoin or its metabolites was not detected in nursing rat pups.
L984: ## Pediatrics
L985: The product is recommended for approval in all age groups (birth through adult).
L986: ## Geriatrics
L987: There were no patients 65 years of age or older in the clinical trials or the safety database.
L988: ## CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY
L989: The mechanism of action was limited to triheptanoin providing a source of calories and fatty acids, which serve as a substrate for energy production. Other claims attributed to the chemical composition as an MCT consisting of three odd-chain 7-carbon length fatty acids (heptanoate) have not been substantiated.
L990: No formal pharmacodynamic studies have been conducted.
L991: Triheptanoin is hydrolyzed to heptanoate which can be measured in the plasma and the pharmacokinetic characteristics after single and multiple doses in healthy subjects are described.
L992: ## NONCLINICAL TOXICOLOGY
L993: Triheptanoin is not genotoxic and has no effect on fertility. Nonclinical findings of tumorigenicity in the gastrointestinal tract of rats are described.
L994: ## CLINICAL STUDIES
L995: As described in Section II.6.2.1, Gillingham (2017) (referred to as study 3 in the PI), was considered an adequate and well-controlled study to support use of triheptanoin as a source of calories and fatty acids and is the only clinical data described in this section. (b) (4)

Metabolic markers of metabolism (e.g., glucose, insulin, lactate) were described in terms of no differences between the treatment groups. Cardiovascular indices of left ventricular ejection fraction and wall mass on echocardiography and maximal heart rate on treadmill ergometry were described as similar.

L1057: Diagram illustrating the mechanism of action of Triheptanoin (UX007) in TCA Cycle Intermediates.
The diagram shows the metabolism of two types of fatty acids: C16-18 Fatty Acid and C7 Fatty Acid (UX007).
C16-18 Fatty Acid Pathway:
Fatty Acid is converted to Fatty Acyl Co-A.
Fatty Acyl Co-A is converted to Acylcarnitine via CPT I (Carnitine Palmitoyltransferase I).
Acylcarnitine is converted to Fatty acyl CoA via CPT II (Carnitine Acylcarnitine Translocase).
Fatty acyl CoA is converted to AC-CoA (Acetyl Coenzyme A) via VLCAD (Very Long-Chain Acyl-CoA Dehydrogenase).
C7 Fatty Acid Pathway:
C7 Fatty Acid (UX007) is converted to C7 Fatty Acid (\text{CH}_3\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2\text{-COOH}).
C7 Fatty Acid is metabolized by Medium Chain FAO Enzymes.
Two C2 Acetyl CoAs (\text{CH}_3\text{-CO-S-CoA}) are produced per FA (Step 1).
One C3 Propionyl CoA (\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{-CO-S-CoA}) is produced per FA (Step 2).
AC-CoA and Propionyl CoA Entry into the Krebs Cycle:
AC-CoA enters the Krebs Cycle, producing \text{CIT} (Citrate), \text{aKG} (\alpha-ketoglutarate), \text{Succ-CoA} (Succinyl-CoA), and \text{PROP-CoA} (Propionyl CoA).
\text{PROP-CoA} is converted to \text{MMA-CoA} (Methylmalonyl-Coenzyme A).
\text{MMA-CoA} is converted to \text{Succ-CoA}.
Electron Transport Chain (ETC) and Beta-oxidation Spiral:
The Beta-oxidation Spiral generates \text{NADH/FADH}_2.
\text{NADH/FADH}_2 feeds into the Electron Transport Chain (Complexes I, III, IV) to produce \text{ATP} and \text{H}^+ ions.
