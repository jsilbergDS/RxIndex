L17: ## 1. Executive Summary
L18: ## 1.1. Product Introduction
L19: Trilaciclib (Cosela®) is a new molecular entity (NME). Trilaciclib is an intravenously (IV) administered inhibitor of cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) that appears to cause transient gap 1 phase (G1) cell cycle arrest of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) within the bone marrow, i.e., cells that give rise to circulating neutrophils, red blood cells (RBCs) and platelets. HSPC proliferation is dependent on CDK4/6 activity.
L20: The recommended trilaciclib indication is to decrease the incidence of chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression in adult patients (b) (4) a platinum/etoposide-containing regimen or topotecan-containing regimen for extensive stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC). The proposed dosing of trilaciclib is 240 mg/m2 administered as a 30-minute intravenous infusion completed within 4 hours prior to the start of chemotherapy on each day chemotherapy is administered.
L21: ## 1.2. Conclusions on Substantial Evidence of Effectiveness
L22: The effectiveness of trilaciclib to decrease the incidence of chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression in adult patients treated with a platinum/etoposide-containing regimen or topotecan-containing regimen for ES-SCLC was demonstrated in three similarly designed phase 1b/phase 2 studies. These studies (Study 1, Study 2 and Study 3) were generally similarly designed. Studies 1 and 2 were first-line chemotherapy studies; Study 3 was a second/third line chemotherapy study. All were randomized, placebo-controlled studies that evaluated the safety and efficacy of trilaciclib 240mg/m2 administered IV or matching placebo (PBO) up to four hours prior to chemotherapy on days 1, 2 and 3 of 21-day chemotherapy cycles that consisted of combination etoposide, platinum (cisplatin or carboplatin) and atezolizumab (E/P/A) or topotecan (T) until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. In Study 1 the mean duration of severe neutropenia (DSN) in cycle 1 of chemotherapy was 0 days in the trilaciclib group and 4 days in the placebo group, p&lt;0.0001. The proportion of patients with a severe neutropenia (SN) event was 1/54 (2%) in the trilaciclib group and 26/53 (49%) in the placebo group. Similarly, in Study 2 the mean DSN in cycle 1 of chemotherapy was 0 days in the trilaciclib group and 4 days in the placebo group, p&lt;0.0001. The proportion of patients with a SN event was 2/39 (5%) in the trilaciclib group and 16/38 (42%) in the placebo group, p=0.0049. In Study 3, in patients treated with second- or third-line chemotherapy, the mean DSN was 2 days in the trilaciclib group and 7 days in the placebo group, p&lt;0.0001. The proportion of patients with a SN event was 13/32 (41%) in the trilaciclib group and 22/29 (76%) in the placebo group, p&lt;0.05. Thus, in three adequate and well-controlled studies in adult patients treated with a platinum/etoposide-containing regimen or topotecan-containing regimen for ES-SCLC, trilaciclib decreased the DSN and percentage of patients with SN.
L23: These studies provide substantial evidence of efficacy for the NDA. DSN is an established surrogate endpoint for FN and infections. DSN and SN are closely related, and have been used previously as the basis of marketing approvals for CSFs, such as filgrastim. Treatment effects were highly statistically significant and clinically relevant in magnitude in all three studies.
L24: For patients in the trilaciclib groups of the three studies, there were trends in favor of fewer all-cause chemotherapy dose reductions, less need for RBC transfusions after week 5 of chemotherapy, and less use of rescue colony stimulating factor (G-CSF), although the studies were not designed to assess these differences. Across all three studies, the proportion of patients with a serious adverse event (SAE) of infection was slightly lower in trilaciclib-treated patients than in patients who received placebo.
L25: Patients treated with CSFs, such as filgrastim, are at risk for serious adverse reactions such as fatal splenic rupture, acute respiratory distress, alveolar hemorrhage and hemoptysis, capillary leak syndrome and vasculitis. In this development program, patients treated with trilaciclib had an increased risk of thrombophlebitis and hypersensitivity reactions (primarily skin rash). Also, interstitial lung disease (ILD) and/or pneumonitis can occur in patients treated with cyclin-dependent kinases (CDK)4/6 inhibitors, though ILD was not reported among patients in this development program. For patients with ES-SCLC who are not candidates for CSFs, trilaciclib offers an important therapeutic alternative.

L28: ## Benefit-Risk Integrated Assessment
L29: Chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression is a common dose-limiting and potentially fatal complication of cancer treatment. Patients with chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression may have decreased bone marrow production of all three cell lineages, i.e., white blood cells, red blood cells and platelets, and may have an increased risk of adverse events (AEs) related to infection, anemia and bleeding, respectively, which can be serious and life threatening (Maxwell, 1992). The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) states that the predicted risk of febrile neutropenia (FN) is &gt; 20% in patients with small cell lung cancer (SCLC) treated with topotecan (T) chemotherapy regimens and the predicted risk of FN is 10-20% with etoposide/platinum (E/P) chemotherapy regimens. The applicant conducted three generally similarly designed, randomized, placebo-controlled studies [Study 1, Study 2 and Study 3] that evaluated the safety and efficacy of trilaciclib 240 mg/m2 administered IV or matching placebo up to four hours prior to chemotherapy on days 1, 2 and 3 of 21-day chemotherapy cycles that consisted of combination etoposide/platinum with or without atezolizumab (E/P/A), or topotecan; until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. These studies are relevant to the indication, in that FN prophylaxis with CSF therapy would generally be recommended for patients enrolled in these studies. In Study 1 the mean duration of severe neutropenia (DSN) in cycle 1 of chemotherapy was 0 and 4 days in the trilaciclib and placebo groups, respectively, p&lt;0.0001. The proportions of patients with a SN event, defined as absolute neutrophil count (ANC) value of &lt;500/\muL, were 1/54 (2%) in the trilaciclib group and 26/53 (49%) in the placebo group. Similarly, in Study 2 the mean DSN in cycle 1 of chemotherapy was 0 days in the trilaciclib group and 4 days in the placebo group, p&lt;0.0001. The proportion of patients with a SN event was 2/39 (5%) in the trilaciclib group and 16/38 (42%) in the placebo group, (p=0.0049). In Study 3, in patients treated with second- or third-line chemotherapy, the mean DSN was 2 days in the trilaciclib group and 7 days in the placebo group. The proportions of patients with a SN event were 13/32 (41%) and 22/29 (76%). Safety data comes from patients who received at least one dose of trilaciclib (122 patients) or placebo (118 patients). Serious adverse reactions including respiratory failure, hemorrhage, and thrombosis were reported in approximately 4% in the three studies. In sum, approximately 25% of patients can be expected to have a significant adverse reaction; however, relatively few patients in the trilaciclib group discontinued therapy because of treatment emergent AEs, i.e., 9%. There is a theoretical risk that the cytoreductive effect of chemotherapy may be decreased because of the mechanism of action of trilaciclib. In Study 3 the median progression-free survival (PFS) was 4 months in the trilaciclib arm and 4 months in placebo arm. The PFS hazard ratio was 0.88 with 95% confidence interval (CI) of (0.50, 1.54) in favor of trilaciclib. The median overall survival (OS) was 6 months in the trilaciclib arm and 7 months in placebo arm. The OS hazard ratio was 1.38 with 95% CI of (0.78, 2.45) in favor of placebo. Patient follow-up in the clinical trials was relatively short (median follow-up duration was 6-12 months), which may not be a sufficient period of time to observe differences in PFS or OS. The applicant should conduct a postmarketing study with a longer follow-up duration to evaluate differences in PFS and OS between the treatment groups.

L37: ## 2. Therapeutic Context
L38: ## 2.1. Analysis of Condition
L39: There are an estimated 200,000 new cases of lung cancer annually in the US, and small cell lung cancer represents about 10-15% of cases. (See website https://www.cancer.net/cancer-
L40: types/lung-cancer-small-cell/statistics; last accessed January 28, 2021.) Therefore, the annual incidence of small cell lung cancer is approximately 30,000 cases, of which only about 20-25% of cases can be considered cured. It can be estimated that about (b) (4) patients annually would be candidates for trilaciclib. (See website https://www.uptodate.com/contents/small-cell-lung-cancer-treatment-beyond-the-basics; last accessed January 28, 2021). Chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression may negatively impact chemotherapy effectiveness by requiring chemotherapy dosing adjustments, interruption or termination. Trilaciclib is an intravenously (IV) administered cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibitor that appears to cause transient gap 1 phase (G1) cell cycle arrest of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) within the bone marrow. Inhibition of CDK4/6 inhibits phosphorylation of the retinoblastoma (Rb) gene product which would normally drive HPSCs through the cell cycle (Kitamura, 2009). Inhibition of CDK4/6 may protect HPSC deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) from damage by co-administered chemotherapy and preserve long-term bone marrow function. Malignant tumors such as SCLC have a loss of Rb function, i.e., are Rb independent, and therefore do not appear to be protected from the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy in the presence of trilaciclib (Gordon, 2011).
L41: Primary prophylaxis of SN or FN is recommended for the prevention of FN in patients who are at high risk on the basis of age, medical history, disease characteristics, and myelotoxicity of the chemotherapy regimen. Epoetin alfa (Epogen®/Procrit®) was approved for marketing on June 1, 1989 under BLA 103234, and is indicated for the treatment of anemia due to the effects of concomitant myelosuppressive chemotherapy, and upon initiation, there is a minimum of two additional months of planned chemotherapy.

L78: ## 4.4. Nonclinical Pharmacology/Toxicology
L79: A Pharmacology/Toxicology review was completed by Dr. David Carlson, final signature date January 15, 2021. The review by Dr. Carlson states that the maximum recommended clinical dose of trilaciclib is 240 mg/m2 trilaciclib administered intravenously (IV) infusion over 30 minutes once daily (b) (4)
L80: Dr. Carlson states that theoretically trilaciclib may protect tumor cells from targeted cytotoxicity/apoptosis by chemotherapeutic agents. Dr. Carlson states that pharmacology data demonstrated dependence on Rb for cell cycle arrest mediated by CDK4/6 inhibition. Trilaciclib had no effect on cell proliferation in cell cultures lacking the Rb signaling pathway. Trilaciclib did induce cell cycle arrest in Rb+ tumor cell lines, showing a direct effect on tumor cell growth and potential use as a chemotherapeutic drug against Rb+ cancers. Also, trilaciclib had no direct effect on cell proliferation on SCLC tumor cells in various in vitro and in vivo models, and the indicated use requires genotoxic chemotherapeutics to target the SCLC tumor cells.
L81: Dr. Carlson's review states that a single major human metabolite, M8, was identified in clinical trials which was not predicted from in vitro metabolism studies. Two other metabolites, M2 and M6, were identified in humans at approximately 4% and 6% relative to parent drug, respectively. M2 potently inhibits CDK4/6 but M6 and M8 did not inhibit CDK4/6 or other CDKs and were considered inactive metabolites. The low level of M2 is not expected to contribute to clinical pharmacologic activity. Negligible levels of M8 (&lt;1%) were detected in rat plasma and none were detected in dog plasma, so animals were not exposed to M8 at expected clinical exposure levels in toxicology studies. Dr. Carlson states the genotoxic potential of M8 is unknown, but any potential genotoxicity is not expected to be clinically relevant considering the indicated use in patients with SCLC being treated with known genotoxic chemotherapeutic agents. Dr. Carlson recommends that further qualification of M8 is not recommended for patients with advanced cancer. Dr. Carlson's review recommends approval of the trilaciclib
L82: application under NDA 214200 from a nonclinical perspective.

L101: ## Study 1 Design
L102: Study 1 titled, "Phase 2 Study of Carboplatin, Etoposide, and Atezolizumab With or Without Trilaciclib (G1T28) in Patients with Untreated ES-SCLC," was a phase 2, randomized (1:1), double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter (56 study sites overall) study, that enrolled 54 and 53 patients in the trilaciclib and PBO groups, respectively. Randomization was stratified by Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (0 to 1 versus 2) and presence of brain metastases (yes versus no). The chemotherapy backbone consisted of E 100mg/m2 administered IV once daily on Days 1, 2, and 3 of each 21-day cycle, P (AUC 5 mg/mL/min using the Calvert formula) administered on Day 1 of each 21-day cycle and A 1200 mg administered IV on Day 1 of each 21-day cycle. Trilaciclib 240 mg/m2 or placebo was administered IV on Days 1 to 3 with E/P/A therapy for up to four 21-day cycles. Treatment was continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, withdrawal of consent, or discontinuation by investigator. The coprimary efficacy endpoints were the duration of SN (Grade 4) in Cycle 1 and proportion of patients with SN (Grade 4). The overall type I error rate for the primary endpoint analysis was 0.025 (1-sided) and the type II error rate used to compute sample size was 0.10 (corresponding to 90% power). Secondary endpoints were evaluation of OS, all-cause dose reductions (number of events), proportion of patients requiring RBC transfusions after 4 weeks of therapy, and proportion of patients requiring G-CSF rescue therapy. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the secondary endpoints. The study schema is shown below in the applicant's figure.

L119: ## Study 2 Design
L120: Study 2 titled, "Phase 1b/2a Safety and Pharmacokinetic Study of G1T28 in Patients with Extensive Stage Small Cell Lung Cancer (SCLC) Receiving Etoposide and Carboplatin Chemotherapy," was a 2-part, multicenter (71 study sites overall), phase 1b/2a study designed to evaluate the safety and pharmacokinetics (PK) of trilaciclib in combination with E/P chemotherapy for patients with newly diagnosed ES-SCLC. Part 2 consisted of a randomized (1:1),
L121: double-blind, placebo-controlled portion. All parts of the study included 3 study phases, i.e., a screening portion, treatment portion and follow-up portion. L130: ## Study 3 Design
L131: Study 3 titled, "Phase 1b/2a Safety and Pharmacokinetic Study of G1T28 In Patients with Previously Treated Extensive Stage Small Cell Lung Cancer (SCLC) Receiving Topotecan Chemotherapy," was a phase 1b/2a study of trilaciclib administered in combination with T in patients with previously treated ES-SCLC. The study consisted of 2 parts, i.e., a phase 1b, open-label, dose-finding portion (Part 1) and a phase 2a, randomized, double-blind, placebo-
L132: controlled portion (Parts 2A and 2B). The results from Part 1, in combination with results from the dose escalation cohorts in Study 1 and Part 1 of Study 2, established the recommended phase 2 dose of trilaciclib, i.e., 240 mg/m2 administered with chemotherapy (T). Part 2A evaluated two T doses and patients were randomized in a 2:1 ratio to either trilaciclib and T (0.75 mg/m2) or placebo and T (1.5 mg/m2). The applicant states that T exposures between the groups in Part 2A, i.e., T 0.75 mg/m2 and T 1.5 mg/m2, showed clinically relevant drug-drug interaction (DDI). The applicant then added Part 2B to further evaluate the combination of trilaciclib 240 mg/m2 with the standard-of-care dosing of T, i.e., 1.5 mg/m2. Topotecan 1.5 mg/m2 was administered IV on days 1-5 of each 21-day cycle. In Part 2B, patients were randomized to 2:1 to receive trilaciclib 240 mg/m2 or placebo administered IV once daily prior to T on Days 1 to 5 of each 21-day chemotherapy cycle. Randomization for Part 2B was stratified on the basis of ECOG performance status (0 to 1 versus 2). The applicant's figure below shows the study schema for Part 2B of Study 3. L295: ## 8.6. Safety in the Postmarket Setting
L296: ## 8.6.1. Expectations on Safety in the Postmarket Setting
L297: The intended indication for trilaciclib is to decrease the incidence of chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression in adult patients treated with a P/E-containing regimen or T-containing regimen for ES-SCLC. ES-SCLC is an aggressive disease. The number patients with any lung cancer is approximately 250,000 in the US of which approximately 13% are diagnosed with SCLC. The 5-year survival rate for individuals with SCLC is poor at approximately 6% (Ramaswamy, 2006). It can be expected that trilaciclib would be expected to be administered to a subset of these patients who are not candidates for other approved CSFs. Trilaciclib would be administered and monitored in a clinical setting along with a patient's chemotherapy regimen, which is individualized to patients by their physician. L314: ## 12. Postmarketing Requirements and Commitments
L315: Dr. Qing Xu, the statistical reviewer, noted that the anti-tumor efficacy, assessed on the basis of PFS and OS, lacked adequate statistical power. An increased risk of death was observed in Study 3, with a hazard ratio of 1.38 (95% CI: 0.78, 2.45) in favor of the *placebo* group. Given the small sample size for the study (32 and 29 patients in the trilaciclib and placebo groups, respectively), the 95% CI of hazard ratio was wide, and the estimate of the hazard ratio may be unreliable. Therefore, no definitive conclusion regarding the impact of trilaciclib on OS can be drawn from these data. In addition to the small numbers of patients in the studies, the median follow-up time was limited in the studies, as noted above. In Study 3, the median follow-up time was 6 months.
