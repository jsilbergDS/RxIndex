L443: ## II. BACKGROUND
L444: Chemo Research, SL seeks the approval of benznidazole for the treatment of Chagas disease (**CD**), the third most common parasitic disease in the world (after malaria and schistosomiasis). CD is caused by the protozoan parasite *Trypanosoma cruzi* when transmitted to humans by the triatomine bug (reduviid bed bug), by defecation on mucous membrane or skin bite with defecation. About 8 million people worldwide (mostly Latin America) live with CD-related (cardiac) morbidity, and over 10,000 die each year from CD. Currently there is no approved treatment for CD.
L445: CD is considered rare in the United States but estimated prevalence is increasing with changing demographics and increasing public awareness. The effectiveness of the sponsor's study medication in treating CD (2014 Orphan Designation) as proposed in this NDA (Priority Review) is supported solely by foreign medical literature. The published studies were audited at GCP inspections of the sponsor and a CI site (Sosa-Estani) where the following key study was conducted.
L446: ## Benznidazole Chagas Study
L447: *Efficacy of chemotherapy with benznidazole in children in indeterminate phase of Chagas disease.* Estani S et al. The American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene 59(6):526-529, 1998
L448: This double-blind randomized clinical trial was conducted between 1991 and 1995 in northwestern rural Argentina, at 14 localities considered endemic for *T. cruzi* and under continuous CD surveillance. Children assumed to be chronically (\ge 6 years) infected with *T. cruzi* were enrolled into the trial after screening and exclusion for any chronic health condition, acute or chronic infection (other than CD), unstable residence (social status), or lack of parental consent.
L449: - 106 subjects were enrolled into the study, 55 for benznidazole and 51 for placebo. For each of the 14 localities (geographic strata), subjects were matched by age and the study treatment (benznidazole 5 mg/kg/day or matching placebo) were administered for 60 days by trained parents, teachers, or nurses.
- Efficacy evaluations were performed using serologic testing, immediately before and at 3, 6, 12, 18, 24 and 48 months after treatment initiation. An enzyme immunoassay (**EIA**) using *T. cruzi* flagellar antigen was used to assess parasitologic cure (**PC**). The serologic testing included EIA, indirect hemagglutinin assay (**IHA**), and indirect immunofluorescence assay (**IFA**).
- Safety evaluations were performed at Days 21 and 60, to include interim history including monitoring for adverse events (**AEs**), physical examination, electrocardiogram (**ECG**), hematology testing (including erythrocyte sedimentation rate), chemistry testing (including liver function tests, **LFTs**), and urinalysis.
- **AEs** were considered: (1) mild if study treatment was completed; (2) moderate if study treatment was suspended but without requiring special treatment; and (3) severe if study treatment was suspended and special treatment was required.

L562: ## REGULATORY HISTORY
L563: - Benznidazole (BNZ) is an antiprotozoal agent of the nitroimidazole group used to treat Chagas disease and is included in the WHO Essential Medicines List to treat Chagas disease. It has been adopted as a first choice in the treatment of Chagas disease in most endemic countries.1,2
- In use since the early 1970s for the treatment of *Trypanosoma cruzi* infection, Chagas disease (CD).
- Not approved in the US.
- The original drug registration of benznidazole was performed by Roche in Brazil, Argentina, Bolivia, Uruguay, Peru, Nicaragua and Japan in the 1970s as Radanil®, Ragonil® or Rochagan®. In 2003, Roche decided to donate all commercial rights and the technology to manufacture benznidazole to the Brazilian government, Laboratorio Farmaceutico de Pernambuco (LAFEPE). Roche was the sole producer of benznidazole until the technology was transferred to LAFEPE.
- On December 29, 2016, Chemo Research, S.L. submitted a new NDA 209570 for (b) (4) (benznidazole).
- On April 7, 2017, the Applicant submitted revised labeling.

L574: ## Chagas Disease (CD) and Pregnancy
L575: Chagas disease, or American trypanosomiasis, is a zoonosis caused by the parasite *Trypanosoma cruzi* (a flagellated protozoan parasite). World Health Organization (WHO) disease burden estimates place CD first among parasitic diseases in the Americas.6 Twelve million people are estimated to have CD, and 25,000 CD-related deaths occur each year in

L578: the world.7 On the basis of the size of the Latin American immigrant population and the estimates of *T. cruzi* prevalence in their home countries, it is estimated that 300,000 infected immigrants reside in the United States8. Infection is life-long in the absence of effective treatment. The most important consequence of *T. cruzi* infection is cardiomyopathy, which occurs in 20 to 30% of infected persons9.
L579: Maternal infection can cause adverse effects during pregnancy relating to fetal growth and maturity, abortion, prematurity, increased neonatal mortality and transmission of Chagas Disease.10,11 Congenital transmission of *T. cruzi* is diagnosed when a neonate is born from an infected mother with positive serology or *T. cruzi* parasites circulating in the blood; when *T. cruzi* parasites are detected in the neonate at birth or shortly thereafter, or when *T. cruzi* antibodies not of maternal origin are detected after birth; and when transmission to the neonate by vectors or blood transfusion has been ruled out.7
L580: Only two drugs, nifurtimox and benznidazole are currently available for the treatment of Chagas Disease, both with similar effectiveness and limitations12, 13. Both drugs are associated with a high risk of toxicity in adults, especially dermatologic reactions12. Benznidazole is the most commonly used drug in South America, due to availability issues12.
L581: Because safety of benznidazole in pregnancy has not been established, it is not recommended for use in treating pregnant women9. Prevalence of vertical transmission of *T. cruzi* infection from immunocompetent women to their fetus varies from 0.1% to 18% among regions14, and such transmission is strongly associated with the maternal blood-parasite load9,15. However, patients co-infected with HIV exhibit higher levels of parasitemia and a higher congenital transmission rate than those who are not co-infected16. Overall, 60-90% of congenitally infected children are asymptomatic. A small percentage of infected children present with clinical conditions common to other congenital infections, including hepatosplenomegaly,

L593: ## Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling
L594: On June 30, 2015, the “*Content and Format of Labeling for Human Prescription Drug and Biological Products; Requirements for Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling*,”20 also known as the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR) went into effect. The PLLR requirements include a change to the structure and content of labeling for human prescription drug and biologic products with regard to pregnancy and lactation and create a new subsection for information with regard to females and males of reproductive potential. Specifically, the pregnancy categories (A, B, C, D and X) are removed from all prescription drug and biological product labeling and a new format is required for all products that are subject to the 2006 Physicians Labeling Rule21 format to include information about the risks and benefits of using these products during pregnancy and lactation.

L622: ## Summary
L623: Very limited human pregnancy outcome data (three cases with information on infant outcomes) were found in the published literature for benznidazole. Two of the three exposures were in the third trimester, and therefore, are uninformative with regards to potential teratogenicity. The third case was similarly uninformative due to very early exposure (within the first 2 weeks after conception, therefore likely before implantation). The findings in animal studies demonstrated increased fetal loss and eye anomalies at doses under the maximum human doses. Since there is evidence of fetal risk based on animal studies, a signal for mutagenicity and limited available data in humans, DPMH agrees with the Applicant and recommends that benznidazole not be used routinely during pregnancy (ie individual cases where maternal or fetal mortality or significant morbidity is threatened may be the exceptions). In addition, DPMH recommends adding a Warning and Precaution for embryo-fetal toxicity to the labeling for benznidazole.

L625: ## *Disease-associated maternal and/or embryo/fetal risk*
L626: Published data from case-control and observational studies on chronic Chagas Disease during pregnancy are inconsistent in their findings. Some studies showed an increased risk of pregnancy loss, prematurity and neonatal mortality in pregnant women who have chronic Chagas Disease while other studies did not demonstrate these findings. Chronic Chagas Disease is usually not life-threatening. Since pregnancy findings are inconsistent, treatment of chronic Chagas Disease during pregnancy is not recommended due to risk of embryofetal toxicity from [REDACTED](b) (4).
L627: Acute symptomatic Chagas Disease is rare in pregnant women; however, symptoms may be serious or life-threatening. There have been reports of pregnant women with life-threatening symptoms associated with acute Chagas Disease who were treated with benznidazole. If a pregnant woman presents with acute symptomatic Chagas Disease, the risks versus benefits of [REDACTED](b) (4) treatment to the mother and the fetus should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

L656: There are data on the presence of benznidazole in human milk. A calculated relative infant dose of 12.3% was reported from a study of 12 lactating women31. This issue was discussed with the clinical team, the pharmacology/toxicology team and the clinical pharmacologist from DAIP and consensus was reached that “Breastfeeding is not recommended” during use of benznidazole.

L666: ## DPMH's Review of Literature
L667: DPMH conducted a search of published literature in PubMed and Embase regarding benznidazole and its effects on fertility and found several nonclinical articles which will not be discussed further in this review as they are not to be included in labeling. Articles regarding the toxicity, mutagenicity and potential carcinogenicity of benznidazole were reviewed, and in addition to the animal findings, contributed to concerns about the use of this product in pregnancy and lactation.
L668: ## Summary
L669: There are no human data available on the effect of benznidazole on fertility. Based on animal fertility studies, benznidazole may impair fertility in male patients. Therefore, section 8.3 will also contain a section describing the potential for benznidazole to impair male fertility. Based on the animal findings and the Warning and Precaution regarding embryo-fetal toxicity, recommendations for pregnancy testing and contraception will be added to section 8.3.

L691: ## 8.1 Pregnancy
L692: ## Risk Summary
L693: Based on findings from animal studies, [REDACTED] (b) (4) cause fetal harm when administered to a pregnant woman. Published postmarketing reports on benznidazole use during pregnancy are insufficient to inform a drug-associated risk of adverse pregnancy-related outcomes. There are risks to the fetus associated with Chagas Disease (see *Clinical Considerations*). In animal reproduction studies, benznidazole administered orally to pregnant rats and rabbits during organogenesis was associated with fetal malformations at doses approximately 1-3 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) in rats (anasarca, anophthalmia, and/or microphthalmia) and doses approximately 0.3-1.3 times the MRHD in rabbits (ventricular septal defect). (see *Data*). Advise pregnant women of the potential risk to a fetus.
L694: The estimated background risk of major birth defects and miscarriage for the indicated population is unknown. All pregnancies have a background risk of birth defect, loss, or other adverse outcomes. In the U.S. general population, the estimated background risk of major birth defects and miscarriage in clinically recognized pregnancies is 2-4% and 15-20%, respectively.
L695: ## Clinical Considerations
L696: ## *Disease-associated maternal and/or embryo/fetal risk*
L697: Published data from case-control and observational studies on chronic Chagas Disease during pregnancy are inconsistent in their findings. Some studies showed an increased risk of pregnancy loss, prematurity and neonatal mortality in pregnant women who have chronic Chagas Disease while other studies did not demonstrate these findings. Chronic Chagas Disease is usually not life-threatening. Since pregnancy findings are inconsistent, treatment of chronic Chagas Disease during pregnancy is not recommended due to risk of embryofetal toxicity from [REDACTED] (b) (4).
L698: Acute symptomatic Chagas Disease is rare in pregnant women; however, symptoms may be serious or life-threatening. There have been reports of pregnant women with life-threatening symptoms associated with acute Chagas Disease who were treated with benznidazole. If a pregnant woman presents with acute symptomatic Chagas Disease, the risks versus benefits
L699: of (b) (4) treatment to the mother and the fetus should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.
L700: ## Data
L701: ## *Animal Data*
L702: In an embryo-fetal toxicity study in pregnant rats, an oral dose of benznidazole of 150 mg/kg/day during organogenesis (days 6-17 of gestation) was associated with maternal weight loss, reduced fetal weights, and smaller litter sizes. Benznidazole was also associated with fetal malformations including anasarca at a dose of 50 mg/kg/day and anasarca and eye abnormalities (anophthalmia and microphthalmia) at a dose of 150 mg/kg/day (approximately equivalent to 1 and 3 times the maximum recommended human dose based on whole body surface area comparisons). The NOAEL dose for maternal toxicity in this study, 50 mg/kg/day, is approximately equal to the maximum recommended dose in humans based on body surface area comparisons. The NOAEL dose for fetal toxicity was 15 mg/kg/day which is approximately equivalent to 0.3 times the maximum recommended dose in humans based on whole body surface area comparisons.
L703: In an embryo-fetal study in pregnant rabbits, oral (gavage) administration of benznidazole during organogenesis (days 6 to 19 of gestation) at a dose of 25 mg/kg/day was associated with maternal toxicity including reduced weight gain and food consumption and abortions in 2/20 females in this group. Benznidazole was also associated with fetal abnormalities including ventricular septal defect at doses of 7.5 and 25 mg/kg/day (approximately equivalent to 0.3 and 1.3 times the maximum recommended human dose based on whole body surface area comparisons). The NOAEL values for maternal and fetal toxicity in this study were 7.5 and 2.5 mg/kg/day respectively, which are equivalent to approximately 0.3 and 0.1 times the maximum recommended dose in humans based on body surface area comparisons.
L704: In a pre- postnatal study in rats, first generation (F1) pups born to dams administered 15, 50, and 75 mg/kg/day benznidazole demonstrated normal pre-weaning behavior, physical and functional development, neurological findings, and reproductive parameters. However, cesarean section data for first generation (F1) females in the high-dose group included significantly higher pre-implantation loss and significantly lower mean values for corpora lutea counts, number of implantations, and number of live embryos. Also small testes and/or epididymides were observed in 1/20 and 2/20 first generation males in the mid- and high-dose groups respectively, and two of the affected animals failed to mate or induce pregnancy. However, the mean values for mating performance, fertility index, testes weight, testes and epididymides sperm counts, and epididymal sperm motility and progression were not altered in any of the F1 males in benznidazole treatment groups. The number of live second generation (F2) fetuses born to F1 dams was reduced in the high-dose group. The NOAEL value was considered to be 50 mg/kg/day which is approximately equal to the maximum recommended dose in humans based on body surface area comparisons.

L740: ## Consult Review Comments to CDER:
L741: The following review comments are provided for internal discussion and consideration.
L742: Positive results from at least two serological assays using *T. cruzi* antigens have been considered evidence of exposure to *T. cruzi*. A variety of different assay platforms, antigen preparations, and output measures have been used for these purposes. Due to the challenges of directly detecting the organism during infection, different serological assay results have also been considered as evidence of treatment efficacy in the field. Potential difficulties with this approach have been discussed in the clinical literature, and are common to serological assays that are used for other infectious diseases (e.g., persistence of some antibodies after infection resolution, slow changes in antibody response, potential cross reactivity with other infections, etc.).
L743: Among the preclinical and clinical evidence presented in the NDA, the applicant presented information to support the use of *T. cruzi* serological assay results as surrogate markers for parasite reduction and potentially clearance (sterile cure). It is proposed that as surrogate markers of parasite reduction, these serological results could also be used as surrogate markers to predict clinical disease progression (or lack thereof) in *T. cruzi* infected patient (b) (4)
L744: ## **Overall recommendation:**
L745: The regulatory decision to approve or not approve benznidazole for the proposed indications should rely foremost on the results of clinical outcome measures as primary endpoints in the context of known risks. There is uncertainty about the performance characteristics of the assays highlighted in the NDA, and there is uncertainty about the predictive power of serological results to reliably discriminate which subjects will go on to receive clinical benefit. Thus, while the applicant has provided evidence of an overall trend of a reduced serological assay result signal in the benznidazole arms when compared to the placebo treatment arms from two small randomized controlled trials, it is not clear that a precise estimate of treatment effect could be calculated from these serological results alone. Our recommendation is that serological results be considered supportive information (b)(4)
L746: Additional details are discussed below, including the recommendation that the ELISA assays should be interpreted as qualitative assays.

L761: ## *Detecting decreases in anti-parasite antibodies over time*
L762: The decrease in anti-parasite antibodies over time (with or without full conversion to seronegative status) has been proposed as surrogate evidence for the reduction of parasite burden, and thus a surrogate endpoint for treatment efficacy. The applicant has described antibody reduction as changes in IFA/IHA titer results, and also as changes in ELISA numerical results. Both types of measurements are discussed below.
L763: ## Decreases in IFA/IHA titer results – general comments
L764: - IFA and IHA are conventional assays that use whole parasite and whole parasite lysates, respectively, and so these assays detect a broad mixture of antibodies to many different *T. cruzi* antigens.
- Defining the term titer in relation to IFA/IHA.
L765: Titer results are produced when qualitative assays such as IFA/IHA are performed on serially diluted samples. Each dilution is tested individually, and the most dilute sample that is positive by the assay is reported as the titer (e.g., 1:16 or 1:256). Samples with high antibody concentration require many serial dilutions before a negative result is produced.
L766: - Titer results are relative, not absolute values.
L767: As in other disease examples (e.g., syphilis nontreponemal antibodies), it is understood that titers cannot be compared between different assay platforms, nor are they necessarily comparable for results of the same assay performed at different labs.
L768: - Titer change and clinical relevance.
L769: It is not clear what would be considered a clinically relevant antibody titer change for *T.*
L770: *cruzi* infection. Ideally, there would be an established consensus in the field (e.g., a four-fold or greater change in syphilis nontreponemal assay titers is considered significant). We are not aware of a consensus of clinically relevant levels of *T. cruzi* antibodies. Different measures are described in the *T. cruzi* literature depending on the setting (e.g., two or three-fold changes, titer changes after conversion to geometric mean, etc.)
L771: ## 5. Assay reproducibility.
L772: Under ideal circumstances, the reproducibility (precision) of the assays should also be taken into consideration when evaluating changes in antibody titer. For example, if an assay titer result can vary plus or minus one doubling dilution due to intrinsic technical/procedural effects, then a two-fold titer change would not be considered significant in this assay. It is not clear what the within-assay or between-assay variability was for the IFA and IHA assays used in the RCTs.
L773: Differences noted between IFA and IHA results in the two RCTs
L774: ## 6. The IFA and IHA assays reported different titer result trends in each RCT.
L775: In the Sosa-Estani trial, IHA titers were generally higher than IFA titers for the same samples at baseline. In contrast, in the De Andrade trial, IFA titers were generally higher than IHA titers for the same samples at baseline. The reasons for this discrepancy are not clear, but could be attributable to differences in reagent preparations, technician subjectivity for manually read assays, quality control measures, or differences in lab environments (e.g., humidity can affect IHA readings).

L783: Interpreting decreases in individual ELISA numerical results.
L784: - ELISAs produce an underlying numerical output value from a sample reading. For most colorimetric ELISAs that are read in a spectrophotometer, this value is the raw optical density (OD) value. Other values such as relative light units (RLUs) can be used for ELISAs that have a chemiluminescent format.
- ELISA results can also be reported as index signal-to-cutoff (S/CO) values in which the raw output value for each sample is divided by an assay cut-off value.
L785: For example: 0.180 OD value / 0.150 OD cut-off value = 1.2 Index S/CO value.
L786: - Usefulness of a calibrator reagents and controls for ELISAs. Ideally, an assay manufacturer/developer establishes the assay cut-off value (positive/negative determination) by providing a separate control or calibrator reagent that is included with each test run. A calibrator can reduce assay variability from run-to-run, lot-to-lot of reagents, etc. by serving as a unified standard for establishing the cut-off.
L787: Alternatively, the assay cut-off can be defined using parameters from each assay run.
For example:
L788: Cut-off value = 10 X the mean value of the negative control sample result – the mean value of the background control result
L789: This approach may allow greater assay variability between runs or between reagent preparations over time.
L790: As an illustration of the range of assay variability that is possible for ELISAs, a subset of precision results from two FDA-cleared Chagas disease serology ELISA package inserts are shown in Table 5. The largest variability (%CV) that was observed run-to-run was 5.1% in one test and 15.8% in the other.

L793: 11. We recommend that changes in individual ELISA numerical results not be used to infer a quantitative assessment of antibody decrease. The qualitative result (positive/negative) is the more appropriate output interpretation for single-read ELISAs.

- The ELISAs used in the two RCTs provide a single qualitative result for each sample measurement. It is not appropriate to assume that qualitative ELISA results can be interpreted as absolute quantitative results simply because they use underlying numerical values such as OD or S/CO. A change from 0.3 OD to 0.15 OD does not necessarily indicate a twofold decrease in antibodies. Quantitative assays would need additional supporting information such as Limit of Quantitation, linearity/variability across the assay dynamic range, and ideally an external consensus reference standard material for the assay target.
- Precision data is not available for the assays as they were performed at the time of the trials. If the assay variability is not established, it is not clear what amount of observed signal change is within the expected run-to-run variability.
- The use of assay calibrators and controls was not always clear. We note also that both RCTs took place prior to the production of the WHO International Reference Standards for antibodies to *T. cruzi* (2011), which is reference standard material intended for assay quality control and harmonization.
L794: ## *Discussion of conventional versus non-conventional serology assays*
L795: 12. Conventional serology assays are the traditional ELISA, IFA, and IHA assays that use whole organisms or crude lysates as capture antigens. These assays have been used to screen populations for exposure, are known to detect antibodies that can persist for
L796: years after a *T. cruzi* infection, and can show some cross reactivity with antibodies produced from other infections (e.g., *Leishmania*). For these reasons, conventional assays are usually interpreted in a “two-out-of-three” algorithm using assays with different antigens/formats. Fluctuations in individual conventional serological results (e.g., increases, decreases, and occasional seroconversions between positive and negative) have been described as part of the evolution of antibody responses in untreated patients over time.1
L797: In the context of the submission, non-conventional assays use select recombinant or purified parasite antigens. The F29 ELISA (Sosa-Estani trial) uses a recombinant preparation of the F29 calcium binding protein that was previously identified in *T. cruzi* epimastigotes. The AT ELISA (De Andrade trial) uses purified F2/3 antigens from *T. cruzi* trypomastigotes which are produced in the course of human infection. It is proposed that the AT ELISA detects host antibodies that have the ability to lyse parasites in the presence of complement (lytic antibodies). Previous studies that measured lytic antibodies directly in complement-mediated lysis assays proposed that lytic antibodies detect live trypomastigotes during infection, and that these lytic antibodies do not persist after the resolution of infection.
L798: 13. Benefits and risks of non-conventional assays. One goal of using selected antigens in non-conventional assays would be to improve assay specificity by reducing or eliminating cross reactivity with samples from other infections. Another goal would be to develop assays that have outputs that change more rapidly in response to therapy, and that might therefore serve as surrogate markers to distinguish active from past (resolved) infections. The risks of using assays that are developed from only a select complement of antigens are that the assays may be less sensitive, that they may miss important subpopulations of antibody signal, and that they may be more susceptible to regional differences in circulating parasite strains/antigens.2

14. F29 ELISA and AT ELISA results. In both trials, both non-conventional assays (AT ELISA and F29 ELISA) produced more seronegative conversions in the benznidazole treated group versus the placebo group. The association is supportive of a general drop in antibody levels described by conventional serology assays; however, it is not clear how well negative results from either of the non-conventional assay predict which subjects will be clinical treatment successes. This predictive value would be needed to consider either non-conventional assay result as a primary surrogate endpoint for treatment efficacy.
L799: Other sources of uncertainty and points for consideration:
L800: a. If a non-conventional assay that uses fewer antigens happens to be less sensitive than a conventional assay, it is possible that the non-conventional assay could produce a negative result following an overall reduction in antibody levels, even when that antibody reduction was not associated with clinical benefit.

L821: ## 3 OVERALL ASSESSMENT OF THE MATERIALS REVIEWED
L822: On December 29, 2016, Chemo Research, S. L. submitted a New Drug Application (NDA 209570) for benznidazole tablets. The Applicant's primary proposed proprietary name, [REDACTED](b) (4) ***, was found unacceptable on March 17, 2017. Subsequently, on April 7, 2017, the Applicant submitted a new request for proprietary name review and revised labels and labeling to reflect the proposed proprietary name, [REDACTED](b) (4) ***.
L823: We performed a risk assessment of the proposed container label, carton labeling and Prescribing (PI) Information for benznidazole tablets to identify deficiencies that may lead to medication errors and for areas that could be improved.
L824: ## Prescribing Information (PI)
L825: Our review of the *Dosage and Administration, Dosage Forms and Strengths and How Supplied* sections of the PI identified areas that could be improved to prevent medication errors. We note that the “Dosage Forms and Strength” section of the Highlights and section 11, *Description*, of the PI describe the benznidazole 100 mg tablets as “[REDACTED](b) (4) scored. It is debossed with the letter, “E”, on one side of each quarter portion”. However, the product labeling does not indicate that the benznidazole 100 mg tablets have functional scoring. The use of the term *functional scoring* in the labeling can communicate to healthcare providers that the product has been evaluated
L826: against the established criteriaa. Additionally, such labeling should appear in all of the following sections of the prescribing information: “Dosage Forms and Strength” section of the Highlights, “Dosage Forms and Strength” section of the Full Prescribing Information and “How Supplied” section of the Full Prescribing Information.
L827: We acknowledge that benznidazole has weight based dosing and is available as [REDACTED] scored tablets. Benznidazole 100 mg tablets can be split into one-half or one-quarter at the score, which may allow physicians to prescribe doses of 25 mg, 50 mg, or 75 mg using the 100 mg tablet. The proposed PI states that benznidazole tablets may be made into a slurry using water for the pediatric population with [REDACTED] body weight. We are concerned that the volume of water needed to prepare the slurry is not stated in the PI. The preparation directions for slurry need to be further clarified.
L828: We have provided recommendations in section 4.1.
L829: ## Container Label and Carton Labeling
L830: In our review of the container labels and carton labeling for Benznidazole 12.5 mg tablets and Benznidazole 100 mg tablets, we found the container labels and carton labeling are not adequately differentiated. We are concerned that this may contribute to product selection errors leading to dispensing and administration of the wrong strength, wrong dose, or both (See Appendix G). We also identified areas that could be improved to increase clarity, prominence, and readability of important safety information. We also note the net quantity statement is in close proximity to the product strength statement. The product is available as 12.5 mg and 100 mg tablet strengths in quantities of 100. A user could mistakenly read the quantity of 100 as the strength for the 12.5 mg tablets. This type of error is supported by postmarket medication error reports. In section 4.2, we provide recommendations to mitigate dosing confusion and promote the safe use of this product.
L831: ## 4 CONCLUSION &amp; RECOMMENDATIONS
L832: The labels and labeling can be improved to increase clarity and prominence of important information to promote safe use of this product (See section 4.1 and section 4.2).
